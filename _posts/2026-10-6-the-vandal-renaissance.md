title: "The Vandal Renaissance | 484-550 ad"
---

A German group called the Vandals took over a large region of North Africa in the mid 400s and made the city Carthage their capital. The region was a previously Roman province with millions of Latin-speaking inhabitants who far outnumbered their new leaders, and as a result, the Vandals did not impose their foreign customs, but adapted to Roman ones. They learned and used the Latin language for administration, and quickly discovered Roman poetry and mythology. Then they started patronizing the arts, just like prior Roman emperors did.

Three of the towering literary figures produced by the Vandal Renaissance were Dracontius, Fulgentius, and Corippus. All of them had political problems with the Vandal leadership, but all of them benefited from its schools and contributed to the intellectual flourishing of the era.

Dracontius produced a prequel to the Iliad called The Abduction of Helen, a sequel to the Odyssey called The Tragedy of Orestes, a sort of “character study” of Medea from Jason and the Argonauts, and a poem called The Praise of God which narrates a collection of bible stories. All of these were in a style called “Latin hexameters,” a then-popular style of poetry commonly used in Rome’s “golden age” to tell stories of Greek and Roman mythology.

Fulgentius reflected the era’s renewed interest in classical mythology in perhaps an even more impactful way than Dracontius. He wrote a companion piece to the Aeneid called The Exposition of Vergil. The Aeneid was one of the principal texts of Roman mythology, an epic narrative poem in which human figures interact with gods and goddesses, explore the underworld, and participate in wars and intrigues. In the Exposition, the author of the Aeneid, Vergil, is a character – and a ghost. The ghost of Vergil appears to the narrator of Fulgentius’s book and unpacks the symbolism of the Aeneid, explaining how each section of the Aeneid represents different periods of human life, and how the actions and discoveries of the characters reflect virtue and vice. Fulgentius also produced a similar book, The Mythologies, which does essentially the same thing, but for dozens of popular mythological stories from Greece and Rome.

Corippus was perhaps the crowning achievement of the Vandal Renaissance, at least in literature. Educated in Vandal literary schools, Corippus became an expert poet and then witnessed the destruction of the Vandal kingdom at the hands of the Byzantine Empire. He put his skills to the service of the new political masters of his homeland, and composed The Johannis, an epic narrative poem describing the battles of John Troglita, a Byzantine general who helped overthrow the Vandals and then was appointed as a governor or duke of the region. Corippus ended up moving to Byzantium to recite his poetry in the imperial court, and his Johannis is widely considered the most ambitious “war epic” since the first century ad. Arguably, it remained so until the Song of Roland was composed over five hundred years later.

The Vandal Renaissance did not just happen in Africa. Parallel movements in Italy and the Byzantine Empire happened as well, and they all influenced one another. In the Byzantine Empire, a school of liberal arts had been founded by Emperor Theodosius in 425 ad, with pensions for educators in Latin and Greek. Justinian I preserved this institution in the 500s when he made provisions for it in his new law code, The Institutes. He also sent scholars to give renewed vigor to the old literary schools of Italy after invading it and ending the reign of the Ostrogoths.

These literary schools were noticed elsewhere. In France, the Council of Vaison (529 AD) explicitly tried to foster literacy for priests and others by the establishment of their own literacy schools:

“We decree that all \[priests\] who are positioned in the parishes should accomodate the young lectors...following the practice that we learned was introduced throughout Italy and has been quite beneficiary. Nourishing them spiritually just as good fathers do, \[these priests\] should instruct them how to sing psalms and acquaint oneself with the Holy Scripture and the law of the Lord. Thus, they will...receive an eternal reward from the Lord.” Canon 1 of the Council of Vaison

The establishment of schools all over western Europe had significant effects. In Italy, Sedulius wrote his Song of Easter, which was an epic adaptation of an eclectic selection of Bible stories similar to Dracontius’s Praise of God. In Spain, Prudentius wrote epic poems about the dramatic martyr stories of various Christian saints and an allegory of virtue and vice presented as martial one-on-one showdowns, similar to the showdown between Aeneas and Turnus at the end of the Aeneid, but explicitly Christian. In France, four authors – Cyprianus Gallus, Paulinus of Nola, Claudius Victorius, and Paulinus of Perigeaux – wrote epic, Vergilian versifications of multiple books of the bible and the lives of two French saints. In Byzantium, Nonnus of Panopolis produced an adaptation of the Gospel of John in the style of a Homeric epic, and a very long narrative poem about the character Dionysius from pagan mythology, detailing his adventures and mixing them with subtle Christian allegories.

The language and style of these poems show that the pagan classics were heavily studied in Christian schools, and Fulgentius’s mythological works, in particular, strengthened the existing interest in studying and preserving Roman mythology. To that end, in the 500s, a man named Cassiodorus founded a monastery called The Vivarium, part of whose goal was the preservation of literature. He wrote a book called The Institutes in which he described how “secular learning” and “sacred learning” should both be pursued by his monks, and praised the reading and preservation of secular, pagan literature in these words:

“\[The\] holy Fathers have not decreed that the study of secular letters should be rejected...since to a considerable degree we learn to understand Sacred Scripture from this source. ... \[For by\] passing through them we should be eager to gain useful and redemptive wisdom from the Father of Lights. ... Many of our fathers, schooled in secular learning and abiding in the law of the Lord, reached true wisdom...the very faithful servant of God, Moses himself, did this, of whom it is written that he was learned in all the wisdoms of the Egyptians (Acts 7:22). And let us imitate these men, carefully but without hesitation and hastening to read both kinds of teaching if we can...with the full knowledge...\[that\] all wisdom comes from the Lord.” Cassiodorus, The Institutes, Book I, Chapter 28

As a result, Cassiodorus included quotations from the Aeneid and other pagan books in his Institutes. For example, in Book 2, Chapter 3, the following words are quoted from the Aeneid book 10: “Supply me with weapons... \[I will fling\] against the Rutuli \[those spears\] which were fixed in Greeks' bodies on the plains of Troy.” Cassiodorus clearly expected his monks to read and preserve such secular works alongside spiritual ones.

The Vandals and their interest in the pagan classics thus strengthened interest in them throughout Europe, and led to additional efforts to preserve and study them. Thus, fifth century Christians wrote similar narratives about the heroes of their own faith, and also – as exemplified by Dracontius – wrote sequels and prequels about the same old pagan characters, and – as exemplified by Corippus – wrote new “war epics” with new characters every bit as gripping as Aeneas.
