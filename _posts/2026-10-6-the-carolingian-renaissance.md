title: "The Carolingian Renaissance | 780-877 ad"
---

The Carolingian Renaissance is named after Charlemagne, whose French name was Carolus Magnus. He became the principal leader of France in this period, and extended his territory into parts of Spain, Germany, and Italy. While not initially very educated himself, Charlemagne had a personal goal of giving his kingdom an intellectual “brain factory” like the Visigoths had in the previous century. (The Visigothic kingdom, by the way, was mostly destroyed in the 700s by the powerful Umayyad Caliphate of Islam, with which Charlemagne was often at war.) To that end, he began inviting foreign scholars to his court to grow a literary and intellectual movement there.

Among them was Blessed Alcuin of York. The School of York was a “spiritual child” of Monkwearmouth-Jarrow, mentioned in a previous section. It was started in the 600s by St. Paulinus of York, but did not immediately show the intellectual flourishing of its southern neighbors. That changed when one of Bede’s students, a man named Egbert, moved there in the 700s and bought books copied from the scriptoria of the surrounding abbeys like Bede’s. There, he taught the liberal arts and his school produced perhaps the greatest luminary who would grace Charlemagne’s court: Alcuin.

Alcuin was among the people invited to the court of Charlemagne, and when he arrived, he found he was not alone. Charlemagne also summoned a Greek-speaking scholar named Paul the Deacon (he was from Monte Cassino, Italy) and a man named Elissaios, who was a diplomat from Byzantium who Charlemagne asked to stay because he was smart. St. Fardulf of Saint Denis and Theodulf of Orleans were two other intellectuals there.

Together, these men established Aachen Academy, a school of liberal arts, primarily for the sons of Carolingian noblemen, though Theodulf insisted that they educate anyone who might want to be literate, nobles and peasants alike. They also loved playing intellectual games with one another. They adopted nicknames (like King David for Charlemagne and Homer for a poet named St. Angilbert), they wrote bantering letters to one another that they would recite as part of literature competitions, and they composed multi-person skits for themselves and their students to memorize and act out on special occasions (like the Contention of Spring and Winter and the Dispute with Pippin).

A poem I think is worth highlighting here is Verses on Jacob and Joseph by Paulinus II of Aquileia, which retells the biblical story of Joseph and the Coat of Many Colors in a verse format featuring “accentual” rhythm similar to the format of then-contemporary hymns. Also, Alcuin’s Metrical Life of St. Willibrord is similar to Bede’s Metrical Life of St. Cuthbert, discussed above, and Alcuin’s poem The Founders of York dramatizes the history of the School of York in the style of a foundational epic like the Aeneid. The poem combines military battles, miraculous interventions, and a description of social life within that venerable institution to stir “holy envy” and inspire others to imitate the intellectual fervor he recalled from his happy youth there.

Perhaps nothing shows the literary contrast of imperial Rome and Carolingian Germany than that: the greatest poets of old Rome (such as Vergil and Lucan) wrote epics about the foundation of their empire. The greatest poets of Carolingian Germany (such as Alcuin) wrote epics about the foundation of their schools. The old masters gloried in conquering the land, the new masters in enlightening the mind. And yet people call the latter period a “dark age.”

Alcuin and his friends created a unique and fun literary culture inside their school in Charlemagne's capital city, Aachen. They constantly attracted foreign scholars and produced new ones. Many of the men who passed through this school went on to established literary schools of their own to replicate what was going on in Aachen throughout Christendom.

The “Carolingian Renaissance,” as this movement is often called, continued under Charlemagne's successors. In the local Germanic language, Carolingian scholars produced three biblical paraphrases, namely, the Book of Genesis, a Life of Christ called the Heliand, and a Gospel Harmony by St. Otfrid, summing to over 18,000 lines of poetry altogether (longer than Homer’s Iliad). The Gospel Harmony, in particular, also adopted an innovative style: instead of imitating Vergil, it imitated Christian hymnody, featuring accentual rhythm and end rhymes, to make it easy for people to memorize and sing parts of it in the local language. A truly novel way of bringing epic literature to the masses.

The Carolingian scholars did even more in Latin. In Latin “war poetry,” Angilbert produced The Paderborn Epic. Abbo Cernus produced The Wars of the City of Paris. Ermold the Black produced The Praise of Louis the Pious. Together, these summed to about 9,000 lines of poetry. In Latin hagiography, they produced the Metrical Life of Abbot Eigil of Fulda, the Life of St. Amandus, the Metrical Passion of St. Denis, the Life of St. Germain, the Song of St. Lucy, the Metrical Life of St. Erasmus of Formia, the Metrical Life of St. Cassian of Autun, and the Metrical Life of St. Agnes, together summing to about 11,500 lines of poetry.

Walafrid Strabo also produced The Vision of Wetti, which is an epic journey through hell, purgatory, and heaven, similar to Dante’s inferno, and Gerald of Bavaria produced an original fictional epic poem called Walter of Aquitaine, which is about a prison break. The latter became very popular in schools and was widely used alongside the Aeneid to teach Latin grammar, vocabulary, and metrics, but with a Christian, monotheistic backdrop instead of using polytheistic-framed literary devices like so many of the old classics.

As the Carolingian Renaissance continued, a fresh wind swept in from the far West. Irish monks in this century started following the example of St. Columbanus (an itinerant Irish monk from the 500s) and journeyed through Europe, bringing Irish learning with them. Among them was John Scotus Eriugena, who would have been another “one man renaissance” if he had worked alone like St. Boethius had in the 500s. But Eriugena did not work alone. He found a collaborative environment in the court of King Charles the Bald, and became rector of a school that more or less was the successor of Aachen Academy: the Palace Academy.

The Palace Academy was a rather mobile institution. Emperor Charles the Bald liked to travel around his kingdom, and sometimes stayed at Compiegne, sometimes Laon, sometimes Paris. He also likes to retain the Schoolmasters of the academy in his retinue, like Eriugena, who thus had to follow him around, and learned to work and teach “on the go.” Despite this hectic environment, Eriugena translated Greek works by St. Gregory of Nyssa and St. Maximus the Confessor into Latin, and also got to work translating Pseudo Dionysius, a sixth century Byzantine Church Father who was also an exceptionally original philosopher.

All of this new Greek knowledge was very influential in the West, and Eriugena himself wrote a five volume book of philosophy based in part on Pseudo Dionysius, but incorporating western ideas from St. Augustine and from Eriugena’s own head. This work of synthesis between eastern thought and western thought is widely regarded as probably the most astonishingly clear-minded philosophical work of the western world between St. Boethius (from the 500s) and St. Anselm (who arrived in the 1000s).

I have discussed the high literary culture of the Carolingian empire, which embraced France and Germany. In Spain, most Catholics lived under Muslim rule at this time, and probably the most ambitious literary effort was a translation of the Book of Psalms into Arabic poetry by a Catholic poet named Hafs ibn Albar al Quti. The last time a poetic adaptation of the whole psalter had been produced was in the 300s, with Apollinaris's Homeric Psalter. Now a similar achievement was made by a Christian in the heart of Muslim Spain.

The northern part of Spain was wracked with war as the surviving Visigoths organized themselves, invaded Muslim-controlled territory, took control of more and more cities, and then divided into several independent Christian kingdoms. It wasn't an ideal environment for scholarly output, but besides the Arabic-speaking poet mentioned above, Spain also produced St. Beatus of Liebana at this time, who wrote an influential commentary on the Book of Revelation.

Italy during the 700s and 800s was also dealing with Muslim invasions and internal divisions, but nonetheless their monastic network continued to develop its connections to Byzantium, which allowed them to keep pouring translations of Greek texts into Latin. Four big Italian translators of this period were Anastasius Bibliothecarius of Rome, Archbishop Leo of Calabria, Paul the Deacon of Naples, and John the Deacon of Naples. These four men largely worked within the still-thriving Byzantine monastic institutions that were founded in Italy in the 500s and 600s, mentioned in a previous section, and many of which were now bilingual in Greek and Latin, due to living so long in or near Latin-dominant territory.

The translations made in this period included three historical books by Theophanes the Confessor, George Syncellus, and Patriarch Nicephorus, theological treatises by St. Maximus the Confessor (different from the ones translated by Eriugena in the same century), two stories about the relics of past saints (Pope St. Clement I and St. Stephen the Protomartyr), and fourteen Saints Lives: Nicholas of Myra, Juliana, Mary of Egypt, Cyrus and John, Theophilus of Adana, the Martyrs of Mount Ararat, Peter of Alexandria, John Calybites, Bartholomew the Apostle, Basil of Caesarea, John V of Alexandria, Demetrius of Thessalonica, Maximus the Confessor, and John the Almsgiver.

Saints Lives were, I think, the most popular genre of literature produced during this period, and if I tried to list every original Saint Life produced, I think I would find it very tedious. So I’m only focusing on authors who made the extra effort of either translating a Life from one language to another, or authors who composed these stories in the style of epic narrative poetry. My list of literature authors from this period, in every region, may therefore seem smaller than it really was, but that is simply because there were so many authors of interesting stories, I choose to skip the majority. (Also, many of the Lives are anonymous, so for those ones I couldn't name their authors if I wanted to.)

In summary, the Carolingian Renaissance included a massive literary renewal in Germany and France, mirrored in England and Italy, that not only looked back to the classical past for inspiration (such as the influential translations of Greek Church Fathers and historians, and Angilbert naming himself after Homer), but also established influential literary schools that would go on to shape Europe’s future.
