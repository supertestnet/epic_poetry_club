title: "The Ottonian Renaissance | 936-1024 ad"
---

By the 900s, Italy had long been a point of contact between the Byzantine East and the Latin West, and a lense through which the light of Greek knowledge shined. It became even more so in this century, particularly in Salerno, where Byzantine medical treatises began to be studied and translated into Latin, such as portions of Paul of Aegina’s medical encyclopedia.

A medical school developed around this material, the School of Salerno. Students from all over Europe traveled to Salerno to work with tutors, buy books, and not just fund but also participate in the development of the theory and practice of medicine. Salerno was thus a place where knowing both Greek and Latin was profitable, and where working as a translator could be a lucrative source of income.

In future centuries, the Medical School evolved into one of Europe's most prestigious early universities, and it got its start in the 900s. The educational system was not organized like a university yet, but tutors from Salerno in this era already gained an international reputation for medical genius, and it is notable as a rare "secular" learning environment with as much prestige as the more common "monastic schools" of the era.

The ability to earn an income by translating in Italy was not exclusively related to the growing medical community. Scholars from Naples like John the Deacon, Leo the Archpriest, and Garioponto the Grammarian of Naples made trips to Byzantium, brought back Byzantine texts, and translated them for the edification and even the entertainment of western nobles.

Thus, one day, Leo the Archpriest found a manuscript of an ancient Greek adventure story on a Byzantine shelf. It featured a fictionalized account of Alexander the Great, where he travels to India, battles monsters, and solves mysteries. Leo thought the story might please western nobles, translated it into Latin, and found that his adaptation became a big hit.

To me, it is fascinating to note that this story already had a Latin adaptation created in ancient imperial Rome, and selections from it called Epitomes (such as the Zacher Epitome) were already popular in monastery schools as educational material. Nonetheless, Leo's new, "complete" version of the tale (with all the monster stories intact) swept through Europe like a wildfire, finding its way not just into monasteries but into noblemen's castles. It was entertaining, and very few stories written primarily for entertainment existed in Europe. (Another notable exception is the prison break story Walter of Acquitaine, mentioned in a previous section. Its preface says it was written to be an entertaining adventure story, but there is little evidence of its use except for teaching Latin grammar and vocabulary.)

Leo's version of the "Alexander Romance," as historians often call it, is also notable for highlighting a distinct personality characteristic already present in the Greek version. This version of Alexander was what we might call "half chivalrous." He was respectful to several of his enemies, showed deference to several women, offered his riches to the poor, and fought some of his enemies nobly in single combat. People of the era commonly called the "high middle ages" loved to read and write adventure stories with chivalrous protagonists. Leo the Archpriest arguably brought this tradition to western Europe in the 900s.

Another major Italian contribution to the Ottonian Renaissance happened in Rome. Due to Muslim raids in the southern Italian region of Calabria, many Greek-speaking monks fled to Rome, and the pope decided to house them in the Latin-speaking Abbey of Saint Boniface. It was renamed as the Abbey of Saints Boniface and Alexius (Alexius was a saint beloved in Calabria) and became bilingual: prayers were said in Latin on some days and in Greek on others, and abbots alternated between ones from the Latin-speaking side and ones from the Greek-speaking side.

This remarkable arrangement made the institution a translation hub. The southern monks brought Greek books with them and worked side by side with Latin scholars, who decided to translate their books. Translations included parts of the Rule of Saint Basil, two books about holy relics (The Relics of St. John Chrysostom, also called the Sermon of Cosmas Vestitor, and one called The Relics of St. Athanasius), a "calendar" known as the Greek Synaxarion which contained "short" hagiographies to be recited on various days of the year, and ten longer hagiographies: the Lives of Pope St. Clement I, St. Fantinus, St. Alexius, St. John Chrysostom, St. Catherine of Alexandria, St. Peter of Alexandria, St. Blaise, St. Eustratius and his Four Companions, St. Febronia, and St. Athanasius of Naples.

Thus Italy continued pouring Greek knowledge into Europe which spread through the monastic "knowledge network" established in previous centuries. Another notable translation happened further north. In a previous section, I mentioned that Emperor Otto I invited Israel the Grammarian of England to teach at his Palace School, and how Israel was particularly enamored with the Byzantine-inspired philosophy of John Scotus Eriugena. As John had translated Pseudo Dionysius into Latin, so his successors at the Palace School translated commentaries on Pseudo Dionysius into Latin, including the Scholia of John of Scythopolis and Maximus the Confessor.

The growing connections between Byzantium and Western Europe were sealed through marital alliances. Some scholars think that Princess Anna of Constantinople moved to France in this era and married Louis the Blind, who produced a son Charles Constantine, Count of Vienna. The evidence for that is uncertain, but much more certain is that Princess Theophanu of Byzantium arrived in Germany at this time to marry Emperor Otto II, and brought a large retinue with her that included Byzantine scholars and diplomats.

They founded Burtscheid Abbey in Germany, which for many years was an outpost of the Byzantine monastic tradition in the heart of the Latin speaking west. Particularly influential was John Philagathos, who taught Greek at the Ottonian court and ensured that the son born of Theophanu and Otto II was schooled in the Byzantine tradition as well as the Latin one. He would become Emperor Otto III, whose bilingual erudition was the era's most evident fruit of the deep alliance between East and West.

Perhaps the most astonishing personality produced by the Ottonian Renaissance was Gerbert of Aurillac, a French priest who was a friend of Otto III and was particularly interested in science. He traveled widely in the various Spanish Christian kingdoms of this time, which successfully invaded and captured Muslim-held cities and discovered libraries of advanced scientific knowledge in their Arab-language volumes.

Southern Spain had undergone a scientific revolution while under Muslim control, largely driven by Byzantine texts that eastern Islamic scholars had themselves discovered in conquered cities, and disseminated west. Muslim scholars translated these texts and developed the science of Byzantium and ancient Greece, especially in mathematics and astronomy, and wrote down their new discoveries and advancements. In the late 900s, Spanish Christians began translating these texts into Latin, and Gerbert of Aurillac was at the forefront of this movement.

He introduced Arabic numerals to the rest of western Europe, which allowed for much quicker and more precise calculation in mathematics than the old system based on Roman numerals. He also studied and built astrolabes, which were three dimensional astronomical charts used for predicting solar and lunar eclipses. He taught scholars in tenth century France how to use them, and early texts measuring the heavens using angles of 360 degrees, with subdivisions of minutes and seconds, already at this time. Gerbert's love of truth, wherever found, earned him the admiration of his peers, and he ended up as pope, and served on the throne of St. Peter as Pope Sylvester II.

Some of the major poets produced by the Ottonian Renaissance were Theodulus, Roswitha of Gandersheim, and Flodoard of Rheims. Theodulus is probably a pseudonym, but the poet is only known from one poem, the Eclogue of Theodulus. This is a fascinating piece of literature in which a Western shepherdess meets a Greek shepherd at a river, and a heavenly character named Wisdom judges an ad-hoc poetry contest between the two. On his turns, the shepherd summarizes stories from Greek mythology, and on her turns, the shepherdess summarizes comparable stories from the bible. At the end of the poem, Wisdom pronounces that the shepherdess wins the contest. The piece became one of the most popular poems of the middle ages, and in future centuries, a performance of the skit was required at several medieval universities.

Roswitha was a nun known for writing six plays, modeled on the ancient Latin playwright Terence, but Roswitha's plays have Christian themes, and mostly female protagonists. She also wrote epic narrative poems, including the Deeds of Otto, the Martyrdom of St. Pelagius (which is about a Muslim persecution in Spain), and Maria, which is western Europe's first poem entirely dedicated to narrating the life story of the mother of Jesus.

Flodoard wrote a major poem called The Triumphs of Christ, which is divided into 27 books. The first three books narrate the stories of the New Testament and the deaths of the Apostles. The next ten books narrate the spread of Christianity through the Mediterranean world. The last fourteen books narrate the history of Christianity in Italy, up through Flodoard’s own time. After a strong beginning, the poem largely becomes a versified chronicle, a kind of list of popes and the major events of their reigns.

I personally find the chronicle format boring, and while the steady beat of a verse chronicle is helpful in retaining my attention (Flodoard probably wrote this historical narrative “as” a poem precisely to make it more catchy), I much prefer the more narrative, story-telling manner of the earlier portions of the work. The full poem is over 20,000 lines long, making it the longest surviving poem between the 400s and the 1100s, and an incredible achievement on that score alone.

Another notable poet of this era is Airbertach mac Cosse, an Irish poet who wrote a book called The Psalter of Quatrains, containing 150 poems that narrate the history of the world from Creation to Judgement Day. Among the Byzantines, John Geometres wrote a poem called The Life of St. Pantaleon, which is a rare example of a surviving Eastern verse hagiography. His contemporary Theodosios the Deacon also wrote The Capture of Crete, which is a "war epic" about a successful invasion of the previously Muslim-controlled Mediterranean island of Crete. The earliest versions of the Tale of Digines Akritas also date to this era. That poem is about a Byzantine warrior who battles Muslim raiders and fantastic beasts, and is another example of the growing European taste for secular adventure stories.

Overall, the Ottonian Renaissance saw a renewed focus on science and bilingualism, with deepening connections between Byzantium and Western Europe. It also saw the development of two prestigious secular learning centers (Otto's Palace School and the Medical School of Salerno). The nobility of the era began seeking entertaining literature, including secular adventure stories that added to the more common genres of hagiography and war epics. Some people say the period called the "high middle ages" began in the late 1000s, with the development of the medieval university system. Historians, consider marking the beginning of that era one century prior, with the schools, science, saints, and stories of the Ottonian Renaissance.
