The Holy Roman Empire's "Ottonian Dynasty" was followed by the "Salian Dynasty," known for advancing the development of Europe's nascent secular school system. But before I get to that, I want to talk about what was happening in England at this time. We last left England during the Alfredian Renaissance, with Wulfstan Cantor giving new life to the music and poetry of all Europe. He is considered a major figure of the Winchester School, which was probably England’s finest literary school in this period. It continued to flourish in the 1000s, with scholars like Aelfric of Eynsham producing an Old English translation of the first six books of the bible.

Other English scholars of this period were even more influential: Eilmar of Malmesbury was a scientific prodigy centuries ahead of his time, who observed the cyclical nature of Halley’s Comet, studied the flight of birds, experimented with winged gliders, and intuited (after breaking his legs) that a glider would be much easier to steer with a birdlike tail.

Walcher of Malvern was another English astronomer of this era, who set up a personal observatory, and wrote texts about the discoveries he made using an astrolabe to research the night sky. He helped bridge continental European science, still ongoing under the Salian Renaissance, to England.

A third English scientist of this time was Adelard of Bath, who traveled in foreign lands such as Palestine to study mathematics under Islamic scholars, and brought back texts on the Arabic numeral system, which he translated into Latin. He helped introduce the decimal zero into European mathematics, where Arabic numerals were already being studied, and zero simplified many calculations by easing the transition to the “place value” system still used today.

Perhaps the two greatest poets in England at this time were Guy of Amiens, who accompanied William the Conqueror into England at this time and sang his military victories in the Song of the Battle of Hastings, and Reginald of Canterbury, who wrote a poem nearly 4,000 lines long about the life of St. Malchus – the longest verse hagiography ever composed up to that time. This period also marks the earliest evidence of a teacher being hired by students in the city of Oxford for group lectures. That student body, and the set of teachers they hired, would gradually evolve into Oxford University.

In Italy, the Medical School of Salerno, mentioned in a previous section, continued to grow as the premier example of a European secular school. Byzantine medical textbooks translated into Latin at this time included Nemesius of Emesa’s Book on Human Nature, On Pulses by Philaretus, The Medical Arts by Galen, and On Urine by Theophilus Protospatharios. New works were also written by the physicians there and disseminated across Europe, and the school was patronized by Princess Sichelgaita of Salerno. She was married to Robert Guiscard, the Lord of Sicily, whose campaigns to conquer Sicily and southern Italy from Byzantines and Muslims were sung in William of Apulia’s Deeds of Robert Guiscard.

The translators who drove the intellectual engine of the medical school included Saint Alfanus I of Salerno, Constantine the African, and possibly Leo Marsicanus of Ostia, a Greek translator who chronicled the rise of the Salerno school system and its deepening connections with the Abbey of Monte Cassino. That abbey became a translation hub for all these imported medical texts, which embraced not just Byzantine medicinal arts, but Arab ones too.

Still in Italy, at about this time a collection of manuscripts was found near Bologna, Italy, containing a Latin text by a king named Justinian. He was a sixth century Byzantine emperor, mentioned in a previous section, who wrote a summary of his law code called the Digest, and these manuscripts contained its provisions. The University of Bologna was founded at this time, in large part to study this newly discovered material, and students carried knowledge of its legal ideas to the domains of all the European kings. Europe had yet another secular school.

The Abbey of Saints Boniface and Alexius, mentioned in a previous section, continued pouring out translations of Greek hagiographical material in this period, including Lives of St. Elias the Troglodyte, St. Irene, St. John the Almsgiver (this new translation was based on different manuscripts than the already-popular one from the 800s), St. John Calybites (this was mostly a revision of the translation from the 800s to give it more rhetorical flourish), St. Michael of Chios, Saints Theopompus and Synesius, and Barlaam and Josaphat. The latter was technically a Christianized biography of the Buddha, and, under the name Josaphat, he was mistakenly venerated as a Christian saint for centuries. Scholars in the 1800s noticed the mistake and he was purged from Christian saint books in the 1900s.

In Germany, court poetry continued to flourish under the Holy Roman Emperors of the period, such as Wipo of Burgundy’s Tetralogy of Henry III (a mix of praise of the king and educational material), the Song of the Saxon War (which is an anonymous epic about imperial victories), and the Ruodlieb, an adventure poem that narrates a young medieval knight’s journey to adulthood. Also worth mentioning here are the Vienna Genesis, which is a poetic retelling of the Book of Genesis in the local Germanic tongue, and the Cambridge Songs, which are a collection of minstrel songs from this period. The latter collection is partly in Latin, partly in German, and all collected by a traveling English scholar, whose manuscripts ended up at Cambridge University in future centuries.

This era featured a unique blend of Latin poetry and poems in the developing local languages. Latin language poets from France included Sigebert of Gembloux, who wrote a metrical Life of St. Lucy and an epic called The Passion of the Theban Saints. Warner of Rouen wrote a satirical “mock epic” called the Moriuht about a man who pretends to be a traveling scholar but is really a fraud. Bishop Adalberon of Laon wrote a satirical poem addressed to the French King Robert II, which praises him at first, and then criticizes some of the monastic reforms the king oversaw. Fulcoius of Beauvais wrote a poem structured as a dialog between a man and the Holy Spirit, who, over the course of seven books, guides the doubtful, questioning man through biblical history. He also wrote a book called Neither that narrates the lives of four saints in a Vergilian poetic style. The Life of St. Alexi is a verse hagiography by an anonymous author, The Life of St. Arnulf is similar and was written by Letselin of Crépy-en-Valois, and the Life of St. Ghislain is similar and was written by a monk who only signed his name as W. Monachus. Also worth mentioning here is the Ecbasis Captivi, or Escape of a Captive, which is a cute story about anthropomorphic animals including a calf who escapes from a wolf and a sick lion who is helped by a clever fox.

One thing that gets historians really excited about this era is that the earliest surviving French “Chansons de Geste” (Songs of Heroism) come from this period. They are part of the long Christian tradition of epic poetry about secular heroes, and these ones are often fictional, and almost always embellished even when based on real history. The earliest known are the “Song of Roland” and “Gormond and Isembart,” both of which date to this era.

In future centuries, these heroic tales would be widely associated with the taverns in the towns of medieval universities. Among students, slipping away from class to drink and listen to (or even participate in) performances of such works was common, and many students even composed adaptations or “remixes” of these poems. The Song of Roland, in particular, is the only surviving “complete” Chanson de Geste from this century, and tells a tragic story about a defeat Charlemagne experienced while fighting the Muslims in Spain, and the heroic knight, Roland, who sadly died on that day.

Overall, the Salian Renaissance was characterized by the growth of secular schools, a recovery of ancient legal and medical knowledge, and left a legacy of epic poetry that continues to inspire people to this very day. Europe began to be a powerhouse of science, and its existing spiritual institutions of learning (as well as new, secular ones) had a lasting impact on the future direction of the continent.
