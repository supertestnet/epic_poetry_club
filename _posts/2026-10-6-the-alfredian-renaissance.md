title: "The Alfredian Renaissance | 878-996 ad"

Throughout the 800s, England was wracked by Viking invasions, and sadly, many of the invaders loved to burn libraries. Much ancient knowledge was possibly lost forever when they destroyed the Abbey of Lindisfarne in 793 ad, along with its neighbors, which had a great library. Monks constantly fled south and west as Vikings ravaged the north and east. They brought treasured texts of their finest authors with them, such as Cynewulf the Poet, but we have lost the context that might otherwise have told us the life stories of their literary geniuses.

Some of the surviving material includes a verse adaptation of the Book of Daniel in Old English, two epic “saints lives” (aka “hagiographies”) in Old English called St. Elene and St. Juliana, and a narrative poem on the Life of Christ called the Exeter Christ Book.

This initial Viking Invasion era became more stabilized when King Alfred the Great appeared. In addition to uniting several of the battered kingdoms of England and halting the spread of the Vikings, he and his successors (especially King Aethelstan) tried to re-establish the scholarly heritage of England. Alfred settled on a policy of preserving and encouraging study of the above-mentioned works, which were carried to his court by fleeing Northerners. He also summoned foreign, Carolingian scholars to teach the liberal arts, just as Charlemagne had summoned English scholars to do the same thing in the previous century. Among them were Grimbald of Saint-Bertin and John of Saxony.

One native scholar of the time, named Asser, wrote a biography of King Alfred in which he discusses what court life was like during this time. He describes a duty of constantly reading something scholarly aloud in Alfred’s presence, regardless of time of day or other royal duty at hand. Whether Alfred was eating or debating military strategy, someone had to be nearby with a scholarly book open to continue reading to him as soon as his attention could be obtained again. The king also kept a personal notebook with him to jot down sayings that struck him for further inquiry later. He insisted that all his noblemen learn to read or step down, and desired schools to be opened so that all the free children of England could acquire literacy if they wanted it. 

Alfred’s court was also filled with poets. Asser’s biography says he loved to listen to Anglo Saxon poetry, and many poems from this era have survived which mirror similar works from the Carolingian counterparts of the Alfredian scholars. Solomon and Saturn is a poetry battle in which performers would role play as the biblical King Solomon and a fictional Greek philosopher named after the Greek god Saturn, who test one another’s wisdom by answering one another’s riddles and unpacking the meaning of ancient sayings. A similar poem is called The Soul and the Body, in which two performers would roleplay a corpse and its ghost as they debate one another about what awaits them in the afterlife.

Scholars of this era also translated many Latin books of philosophy and theology into Old English, including St. Boethius’s Consolation of Philosophy, Pope St. Gregory’s Pastoral Care, and three biblical books: Judith, Psalms, and Genesis. The Genesis translation is distinct from the one discussed in connection with the Northumbrian Renaissance. Scholars call that one Genesis A. Scholars call this newer translation Genesis B, and it was translated from the Carolingian Genesis poem mentioned in connection with the Carolingian Renaissance.

All three biblical books were adapted as Old English alliterative verse, and in addition, Alfredian scholars also produced a prose version of the first fifty psalms, allegedly translated from Latin by King Alfred himself. While producing the prose Consolation of Philosophy, which contains 39 poems that summarize the philosopher’s points as rhythmic songs, the Alfredian scholars produced a unique adaptation called The Lays of Boethius, which are in a similar alliterative verse format as their various biblical adaptations, but the Lays expand upon Boethius’s original songs to combine mythological narratives, philosophy, and Christian mysticism.

Three of the great luminaries produced by this movement were Israel the Grammarian, Wulfstan Cantor, and St. Dunstan. The latter established a literary school in the city Glastonbury where surviving manuscripts (such as the Glastonbury Classbook) indicate that his students studied and imitated the poetry of the ancient Roman poet Ovid. I suppose no renaissance would earn its name unless its scholars started schools that looked to the literature of the ancient past in order to blaze a trail toward the future, and St. Dunstan shows that the Alfredian renaissance did this too.

Wulfstan Cantor produced what is possibly the crowning literary achievement of the Alfredian Renaissance: The Metrical Life of St. Swithun. At about 3,400 lines, this epic narrative was, when composed, the largest surviving “Saint Life” ever produced in England. It has significantly more epic content than the ancient “secular” record holder, Beowulf, which has about 3,200 lines. Up to Wulfstan’s time, the only surviving epic hagiographies of similar length were The Metrical Life of St. Martin by Paulinus of Perigueux (a French poet from the 400s) and The Life of St. Germain by Heiric of Auxerre (a German Carolingian poet from the 800s).

It is not only the length that merits attention. The style is also a unique example of a form called Latin Hermeneutic Style, pioneered by Anglo Saxon poets and featuring grammatically perfect sentences of incredible length, jammed with obscure vocabulary and loan words. Some modern readers might find the language too ornate and dislike the frequent need to consult a dictionary, but in its time, this style was considered supreme, and many still appreciate it today. (Another hermeneutic “verse hagiography” worth mentioning here is the Compendium of the Life of St. Wilfrid, which was written by Wulfstan’s contemporary poet Frithegoth of Canterbury. That poem is only ~1,400 lines, which is still substantial, but far from what Wulfstan produced.)

Note that I say this poem was “possibly” the crowning literary achievement of the Alfredian Renaissance. The other major candidate, from my perspective, is the Anglo Saxon Metrical Psalter from the late 800s, and which I already mentioned. That magnum opus is much longer than Wulfstan’s piece, but displays similar skill, and therefore producting it required greater dedication to the craft. Moreover, it was in the local language, which was more typical of Alfredian literature, and perhaps more useful to Alfred’s overall project or bringing literacy to the masses.

But Wulfstan’s work has several things going for it: first, from a literary perspective, it tells a consistent story with a main character, which, at least for me, makes it a “funner” read than the psalter. Second, by being in Latin, the Life of St. Swithun was copied and appreciated all over Europe. It showed that England was once again an intellectual powerhouse that could stand toe to toe with the geniuses elsewhere in Europe. The Saxon Psalter, sadly, stayed local.

Israel the Grammarian is widely considered the brightest mind of the tenth century. He learned Greek and was invited to the court of Emperor Otto I to contribute to the Greek-influenced renaissance happening there, to be discussed in the next section. He was a particular fan of the philosophy of John Scotus Eriugena of the previous century. Israel wrote commentaries and critiques of John’s philosophical theories as well as on other philosophers whose influence was rapidly growing in that time, such as Porphyry.

Israel’s expertise in grammar earned him a spot as a teacher at the Palace Academy of Otto I. As was the custom ever since Emperor Charles the Bald (mentioned in a previous section), Otto retained the teachers in his Palace Academy in a sort of traveling retinue.

Perhaps as a result of this hectic environment, the Palace Academy did not always have a headmaster. One might die during their travels, and appointing a successor was not necessarily a priority. Thus, when Manno of Laon died (he was the successor of Eriugena in the 800s), we hear of no replacement, and the school itself effectively died with him. Emperor Otto I effectively revived it, and made Israel a teacher there, thus bringing the learning of England to central Europe once again.

Overall, the Alfredian Renaissance produced some of the brightest minds and poets of the tenth century, beginning with the old tradition of Anglo Saxon poetry and ending with Latin and even Greek language scholarship that became the boast of all Europe.
