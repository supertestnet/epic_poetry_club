Walter of Aquitaine is a poem by Gerald of Bavaria, a scholar of the Carolingian Renaissance. It is a unique adaptation of an old Germanic myth, recast in Vergilian hexameters, with a Christianized main character. It might be termed the "Carolingian Odyssey" in that, like the Odyssey, it is a secular adventure story, characteristic of the poetic culture that produced it, which saw two use cases: entertainment and education (both books were widely used in schools of their respective eras to teach grammar and vocabulary).

One of the things I find interesting about the story is the appearance of a bear near the beginning and near the end. The first time a bear appears is in section 2.4, where Hagano says he had a dream where a bear attacked the king, taking off its leg, whereupon Hagano tried to fight it, and lost an eye and some teeth. This foreshadows the end of the story, where Walter does all of those things to both men. The other time a bear appears is in section 3.6: right before narrating how the king and Hagano actually lost those body parts, the poet dedicates seven lines to a Homeric simile wherein Walter fights his opponents like a bear hemmed in by hounds. So the bear imagery comes full circle, and hearkens back to the foreshadowing from before.

This translation was created in several stages. First, I divided the poem up into sections of roughly 40 lines apiece, which I call sections. Then, I fed each section into Google Search's AI (based on Gemini), and asked it to translate each section idiomatically. Then I combined all the sections, read through the text, modified a few lines for readability, and decided it was good enough to post. Below is the result, and I hope you enjoy it.

# Text

## Preface.

Section 0.0.  
  
Almighty Father, lover of supreme virtue,
And the Son of equal right, and the nurturing Spirit of them both,
Three in persons, but one in true deity,
Who, living life, will hold all things without end,
Save now and forever the supreme pontiff,
The worthy Erckambald, who gleams with an illustrious name,
So that he may grow inwardly, filled with the holy breath,  
Whereby he may be an unfeigned remedy to many for all time.  
Holy prelate of God, receive now the gifts of your servant,  
Which, with abundant care, a fragile sinner,  
Vile in name, Gerald, has determined to bring forth for you,  
Who is indeed a secure and faithful foster child to you in his heart.  
I continually beg this from the all-thundering Lord in prayers,  
That you may achieve in deeds what I utter in words.  
May the Father on high, who governs heaven and earth, grant it.  
Servant of the highest God, do not despise the words of this little book.  
It sings not of the nurturing things of God, but echoes the wondrous deeds of a young warrior,  
Walter by name, hewn down through many battles.  
It is meant more for amusement than for petitioning the Lord,  
But, once read, it shortens the tedious spans of a long day.  
May you be a happy, holy priest, through many seasons,  
And may Gerald be a dear brother in your mind.  

## Book One.  

Section 1.0.  
  
The third part of the world, brothers, is called Europe,  
Divided into various peoples by customs, languages, and names,  
And separated by worship, as well as by religion.  
Among these, the people of Pannonia are known to reside,  
Whom, however, we also commonly call the Huns.  
This people, strong in courage, flourished in arms.  
They not only subdued the surrounding regions,  
But, passing even through the shores of the ocean's coast,  
They granted treaties to suppliants and laid low the rebellious.  
They are said to have ruled for more than a thousand years.  
Attila held that kingdom as king at a certain time,  
Untiring in renewing ancient triumphs for himself.  
Moving his camp, he ordered an advance upon the Franks,  
Whose king, Gibicho, was thriving on his high throne.  
He was rejoicing in a newly born offspring, of whom I will speak later,  
For he had begotten a male child, whom he named Gunther.  
Swiftly flying rumor struck the ears of the terrified king,  
Saying that an enemy host was crossing the Danube,  
Surpassing in number the stars and the sands of the river.  
He, trusting neither in arms nor in the strength of his people,  
Called a council, asking what ought to be done.  
All agreed that they should beg for a treaty  
And join right hands with right hands, if by chance they would offer them.  
Then, having given hostages, they should pay the demanded tribute.  
For this would be better than to lose at once both life and kingdom,  
And their children and their wives likewise.  
  
Section 1.1.  
  
Noble Hagano was a young warrior at this time,  
Of excellent disposition, coming from the stock of Troy.  
Him—because Gunther had not yet reached the age  
To be able to sustain his tender life without a mother—  
They decreed to send to the king along with an immense treasure.  
Without delay, ambassadors carrying the tribute and the youth  
Arrived, and they asked for peace and confirmed the alliance.  
At that time, Burgundy stood with mighty sceptres,  
Whose sovereignty Heriricus happened to hold.  
He had only an only daughter, Hiltgunt by name,  
Flourishing indeed in nobility and the crown of her beauty.  
She, as heir, was meant to reside in her father's hall  
And enjoy what had long been amassed, if by chance it were permitted.  
For the Avars, having concluded a firm peace with the Franks,  
Turned away indeed from the border of their kingdom,  
But Attila soon turned his swift reins toward this place,  
Nor did the remaining satraps delay in following his tracks.  
They marched matched in number, and in a long column.  
The shaken earth groaned beneath the galloping of the horses,  
And the terrified air thundered above with the clashing of shields.  
An iron forest gleamed, reddening through all the fields,  
Just as, early in the morning, striking the seas,  
The beautiful sun shines bright in the furthest parts of the world.  
And now the host had crossed the deep rivers Saone and Rhone,  
And the entire vanguard went about plundering.  
Heriricus happened to be staying at Chalon, and behold,  
Raising his eyes, a watchman cried out:  
"What kind of cloud rises from the thick dust?  
A hostile force is coming, close all the gates now!"  
  
Section 1.2.  
  
At once, the prince himself knew what the Franks  
Had done, and he thus addressed all the elders:  
"If a nation so brave, to whom we cannot compare ourselves,  
Has yielded to Pannonia, with what courage do you think we can  
Engage them hand-to-hand and defend our sweet homeland?  
It is better that they make a pact and take tribute.  
I have an only daughter, whom to hand over for the kingdom  
I do not hesitate. Let those proceed who may confirm the alliance."  
The ambassadors went, completely stripped of their swords.  
They made known to the enemy what the king's command ordered,  
And begged them to cease devouring the land. Leader Attila,  
As was his custom, received them graciously and said:  
"I desire more to offer treaties than to bring battle upon the common people.  
Indeed, the Huns prefer to rule in peace, but with weapons  
They strike against their will those whom they see to be rebellious.  
Let the king come to us to give his right hand and take ours in return."  
The prince went out, carrying away countless  
Treasures, and he struck a pact and left his daughter behind.  
The most beautiful gem of her parents went into exile.  
After he completed the pact and established the tribute,  
Attila moved his columns into the western regions.  
For Alphere then held the realms of the Aquitanians.  
They say he had an offspring of the male sex,  
Walter by name, shining in the first bloom of youth.  
For kings Heriricus and Alphere had given an oath  
Between themselves that they would unite their children  
As soon as the prime time for marriage should come to them.  
When this man learned that these nations had been subdued,  
He began to tremble with immense terror of heart,  
Nor was there any longer hope of being defended by fierce arms.  
"Why should we delay," he said, "if we are unable to wage war?  
Burgundy and France give us an example.  
We cannot be blamed if we equal ourselves to such as these.  
I will send ambassadors and order them to strike a treaty,  
And, in exchange for a hostage, I will offer my beloved son,  
And even now I will pay the future tribute to the Huns."  
But why delay further? He completed his word with deeds.  
Then the Avars, laden at last with many riches,  
Having taken the hostages Hagano, the maiden Hiltgunt,  
And also Walter, returned with joyful hearts.  
Attila, entering Pannonia and being received in his city,  
Showed great kindness to the exiled children,  
And ordered them to be nurtured just like his own foster children.  
  
Section 1.3.  
  
The queen was commanded to take care of the maiden,  
But both of the youths he ordered to be always present  
In his own sight, and he imbued them with the arts,  
Especially with the sports to be practiced in time of war.  
They, growing alike in the genius of their minds and in age,  
Surpassed the strong in vigor and the wise in spirit,  
Until they soon bravely excelled all the Huns.  
Attila then made them the foremost of his soldiery,  
And not without merit, since, whenever he waged  
War, they shone through illustrious triumphs.  
As a result, the prince loved them both exceedingly.  
The captive maiden also, by the favor of the supreme God,  
Pleased the eyes of the queen and increased her love,  
Abounding in excellent manners and industry in her works.  
At last, she was made the keen-eyed guardian of all the treasures,  
And little was wanting for her to rule as queen herself.  
For whatever she wished regarding affairs, she accomplished in deeds.  
Meanwhile, Gibicho died, and Gunther succeeded  
To the kingdom himself, and straightaway he broke the treaties  
Of the Pannonians and refused to submit to the tribute.  
As soon as the exile Hagano learned of this,  
He contrived his flight by night and hastened to his lord.  
Walter, however, had gone forth ahead of the Huns to battle,  
And wherever he went, success soon accompanied him.  
Observing that Hagano had escaped, Ospirin, the royal spouse,  
Suggested such words as these to her lord:  
"Let the wisdom of the king, I pray, foresee and beware,  
Lest the pillar of your empire should by chance collapse,  
That is, lest your friend Walter should depart,  
In whom a great strength of this power resides.  
For I fear lest, fleeing, he may perhaps imitate Hagano.  
Therefore, consider now my reasoning.  
As soon as he returns, speak these words to him:  
'In our service you have often suffered great labors,  
And therefore know that our favor has loved you  
Exceedingly, above all your friends.  
I wish you to recognize this more by deeds than by words.  
Choose for yourself a bride from the satraps of the Pannonians,  
And do not care to consider your own lack of wealth.  
I will indeed greatly enrich you in land and household,  
Nor will anyone who gives you his daughter in marriage feel shame after the fact.'  
If you fulfill this, you will be able to secure him."  
  
Section 1.4.  
  
The speech pleased the king, and preparation began to be made.  
Walter returned, to whom the prince revealed these things,  
Urging him to take a wife. Nevertheless, Walter,  
Already planning in advance what he would later complete with deeds,  
Spoke in opposition to these enticing suggestions:  
"You are indeed too kind, in that you notice the cause  
Of my modest service. But that you regard the sluggishness of my mind  
With such favor, I could never have deserved.  
I only ask that you receive the words of your faithful servant.  
If I take a bride according to the commands of my lord,  
I shall be bound first of all by cares and the love of a girl,  
And shall often be held back from the service of the king.  
I shall be forced to build houses and attend to the cultivation of the land,  
And this will hinder me from being present before my lord's eyes  
And from bestowing my accustomed care upon the kingdom of the Huns.  
For whoever has once tasted pleasure, thereafter  
Is accustomed to bear labors with greater intolerance.  
Nothing is so sweet to me as to always remain in the faithful  
Service of my lord. Therefore, I pray, permit me to lead my life  
Now without the bond of marriage!  
If you command me late in the evening or in the middle of the night,  
I will go secure and prepared for whatever you order.  
In wars, no anxieties will persuade me to yield,  
Nor will a son or a wife hold me back and prompt me to flee.  
I entreat you, best father, by your own life,  
And now by the unconquered nation of the Pannonians.  
You must not urge me further to partake in a wedding ceremony."  
Overcome by these prayers, the king abandoned all his persuasion,  
Hoping that Walter would never depart by flight.  
Meanwhile, a most certain rumor had come to the leader  
That a certain nation, which had recently been subdued, was resisting.  
Moreover, it was prepared to bring war upon the Huns without delay.  
Therefore, the management of affairs was turned over to Walter.  
He soon reviewed the entire army in due order  
And comforted the hearts of his warriors,  
Exhorting them to always remember past triumphs,  
And promising to lay low these rebels with their accustomed valor  
And to impose terror upon the foreign lands.  
Without delay, the whole army rose up and followed.  
  
Section 1.5.  
  
Behold, he descried the place of battle, and he distributed  
His marshaled line across the broad plains and fields.  
And now, within a weapon's throw, each opposing vanguard  
Came to a halt. Then from all sides a shout was raised  
To the heavens. The trumpets sounded their horrific roar,  
And straightaway dense spears flew from this side and that.  
Ash and cornel wood mingled together in the game,  
And the brandished spearpoint gleamed like a lightning bolt.  
Just as thick snow is scattered in the season of the north wind,  
Even so they unleashed their savage arrows.  
At last, when all the javelins from both armies  
Were spent, every hand turned to the sword-hilt.  
They drew their flashing blades and swung their shields into place.  
The lines finally clashed together and renewed the fight.  
The breasts of one side’s horses were shattered against the other’s,  
And a certain portion of the men was laid low by the hard shield-boss.  
Walter, however, raged in the midst of the battle-line,  
Pushing forward and mowing down everything in his path with his weapons.  
When the enemy beheld him causing such great slaughter,  
They feared him as if they were looking upon death itself present before them.  
And whatever place, whether to the right or to the left,  
Walter made for, they all straightaway turned their backs  
To retreat with reversed shields and loosened reins.  
Then, imitating their leader, the great nation of the Pannonians  
Rose up more fiercely. Then they grew bolder and increased the slaughter,  
Laying low those who stood against them and trampling those who fled,  
Until they achieved a full triumph. Such were the fortunes of war.  
Then the leader rushed upon the slain and despoiled them all,  
And at last he recalled the columns with his hollow horn,  
And he was the first to bind his brow with festive foliage,  
Crowning his temples with the victorious laurel before the common soldiers.  
After him came the standard-bearers, followed by the rest of the youths.  
And now they returned adorned with the garland of triumph,  
And having entered their homeland, each man settled himself in his own  
Abode. But Walter hastened straightaway to the throne.  
Behold, the palatine servants ran down from the citadel,  
Joyful at the sight of him, and held his horse  
Until the illustrious man descended from his high saddle.  
  
Section 1.6.  
  
They asked if things had fallen out well.  
He, telling them some small details, entered the hall,  
For he was weary, and made his way to the king's chamber.  
There he found Hiltgunt sitting all alone.  
After embraces and sweet kisses, he said to her:  
"Bring a drink here quickly, because I am weary and panting."  
She straightaway filled a precious cup with pure wine  
And offered it to the man, who took it. He made the sign of the cross  
And pressed the maiden’s hand with his own. But she  
Stood by and silently watched her lord's face,  
And Walter, drinking, offered the empty vessel to her,  
For both knew that a betrothal had been made between them,  
And he teased his dear girl with this speech:  
"We have been exiled together now for so long a time,  
Not ignorant of what our fated parents planned  
Between themselves regarding our future.  
How long must we keep our very mouths silent about these things?"  
The maiden, thinking her betrothed spoke this in irony,  
Kept silent for a moment, but afterward returned these words:  
"Why do you feign with your tongue what you condemn from the bottom of your heart?  
Why do you persuade with your mouth what you reject with all your soul,  
As if it were a huge shame to marry such a bride?"  
The wise man answered in return and brought forth these words:  
"Far be what you mention! Turn your understanding the right way!  
Know that I have spoken nothing with a feigned mind.  
Believe not that any lie or obscurity interposes here!  
For no one is present here with us, except we two.  
If I could be sure that you are ready to grant me a willing mind  
And, despite everything, to keep faith to our secret vows,  
I would wish to open to you all the mysteries of my heart."  
At last the maiden, bending at the man's knees, spoke out:  
"To whatever you call me, my lord, I will follow eagerly,  
Nor would I prefer to put anything before your pleasing commands."  
He then said: "I am weary at last of our exile,  
And I often remember the borders of the homeland we left behind.  
Therefore I desire to make haste by means of a hidden flight.  
Indeed, I might have been able to do it many days ago,  
Had I not grieved that Hiltgunt would remain alone."  
The young maiden added these words from the bottom of her heart:  
"Your will is mine; for these things alone do I burn.  
Let my lord command. For his love, I am ready with all my heart  
To suffer either prosperity or adversity."  
  
Section 1.7.  
  
Walter then spoke thus into the maiden's ear:  
"The royal power has indeed made you the guardian over its treasures.  
Therefore, be mindful and mark these words of mine.  
First, take the king's helmet and his triple-woven tunic.  
I mean his coat of mail, which bears the mark of the master smiths.  
Then, stow away two average-sized chests.  
Fill them with so many Pannonian arm-bands  
That you can scarcely lift one to the bottom of your chest.  
Then, have four pairs of traveling boots made for me according to custom,  
And provide just as many for yourself. Place them in the vessels.  
In this way, the chests may perhaps be filled to the very brim.  
Furthermore, secretly ask the smiths for barbed fishhooks.  
Let our provisions be fish as well as birds,  
For I am compelled to be both fisherman and fowler.  
Accomplish each of these tasks cautiously within a week.  
You are familiar with what a traveler might happen to need.  
Now I will reveal the manner in which we can make our escape.  
When the sun has completed seven more circuits,  
I will prepare a joyous feast at immense expense  
For the king, the queen, the satraps, captains, and servants.  
Then, with all my ingenuity, I will strive to bury them in drink,  
Until none of them can further perceive what we are doing.  
Meanwhile, however, you must use the wine sparingly,  
And take care barely to quench your thirst at the table.  
If anyone rises, run back to your typical chores.  
But when the impact of the drink finally overcomes them all,  
Then let us hasten together to seek the western regions."  
Mindful of his words, the maiden fulfilled his commands. And behold,  
The appointed day of the feast arrived, and Walter  
Himself prepared the food at great expense.  
Indeed, luxury abounded in the midst of the table.  
The king entered the hall, which was enclosed on all sides with tapestries.  
The high-minded hero, greeting him in his accustomed manner,  
Led him to his throne, which was adorned with fine linen and purple.  
He sat down, and ordered his captains, two on each side,  
To sit beside him. The attendant seated the others.  
A hundred companions soon took their places at the couches,  
And each banqueter, tasting the diverse dainties, sweated from his exertion.  
  
Section 1.8.  
  
Then, when the first dishes were removed, others were brought in to be eaten,  
And an exquisite mixture foamed amidst the gold.  
Each vessel of gold stood upon a fine linen cloth,  
And spiced wine adorned the mixing bowls.  
The appearance and sweetness of the drink enticed each one to drain it.  
Walter exhorted them all to wine and food.  
After hunger was banished by the feast and the table was cleared,  
The aforementioned hero, cheerfully addressing his lord,  
Said: "I beg you, let your favor shine forth in this,  
That you first make yourself merry, and then the others."  
Then, with that word, he handed him a bowl crafted with art,  
Whose sequence of carvings related the deeds of the ancients.  
The king, receiving it, emptied it at a single draft,  
And straightaway ordered all the others to imitate him.  
Swiftly the cup-bearers ran up and quickly ran back again,  
Giving full cups and taking back empty ones.  
All vied with one another at the urgings of their host and the king.  
Boiling drunkenness ruled in the entire hall.  
Their soaked palates poured out stutters instead of eloquence.  
You could see mighty heroes stagger on their feet.  
In this manner Walter protracted the gifts of Bacchus  
Into the late night, and he held back those wishing to depart,  
Until, overwhelmed by the force of the drink and weighed down by sleep,  
They were all stretched out everywhere on the ground throughout the porticoes.  
Even if Walter had wished to set the walls ablaze with fire,  
No one still there could have discerned the cause.  
At last he called his beloved woman to himself,  
Commanding her to bring out quickly the things that had been prepared.  
And he himself led from the stables the champion of horses,  
Whom, on account of his courage, he had named Lion.  
The steed stood and fiercely champed the foaming bit.  
After he arrayed him in his usual trappings, behold,  
He suspended the chests full of treasure from each side,  
And packed meager provisions for the long journey.  
Then he handed the trailing reins to the maiden’s right hand.  
He himself, clothed in his coat of mail like a giant,  
Placed the red crests upon his head along with his helmet,  
Enclosed his huge calves in golden greaves,  
Girded his left thigh with a two-edged sword,  
And girded his right with another, according to the custom of the Pannonians.  
The second one, however, inflicts wounds from only one edge.  
  
Section 1.9.  
  
Then, seizing his spear in his right hand and his shield in his left,  
He anxiously began to depart from that hated land.  
The woman led the horse, which carried many talents of wealth,  
And in her hands she likewise held a hazel rod,  
With which a fisherman casts his hook into the wave,  
So that a fish, desiring food, might swallow the hook.  
For the mighty hero was heavily weighed down on all sides by weapons,  
And at all times he expected a fight to come upon him.  
Indeed, they hastened to travel through the entire night. But when  
The reddening sun first showed his light to the earth,  
They made sure to hide in the woods and sought out shady retreats.  
Then fear troubled them, overwhelming them even in safe places,  
And terror so struck the maiden’s heart  
That she dreaded every whisper of the breeze and the wind,  
Fearing the birds or branches brushing against one another.  
On one side pressed the hatred of exile, and on the other, the love of the fatherland.  
They fled far from villages, leaving behind the beautiful plowed fields.  
They followed winding pathways through the uncultivated mountains,  
Changing direction with trembling steps through out-of-the-way places.  
Meanwhile, the people of the city, dissolved in sleep and wine,  
Lay silent in their beds until the middle of the following day.  
But after they arose, everyone looked for their leader,  
To give him thanks and greet him with festive praise.  
Even King Attila, clasping his head with both hands,  
Came out of his bedchamber, and in his pain called out  
For Walter, perhaps to complain to him of his headache.  
The servants answered that they themselves were unable  
To find the man, but the prince hoped that he  
Was still held fast in quiet sleep  
And had chosen a hidden spot for his slumber.  
After Ospirin realized that Hiltgunt was missing  
And had not brought out her robes according to her usual custom,  
She was full of sadness, and cried out to her lord with immense laments:  
"Oh, detestable food which we took yesterday!  
Oh, wine which has brought ruin to all of Pannonia!  
What I, long foreseeing, said to my lord the king,  
This day confirms, a day we cannot overcome.  
Behold, today the pillar of your empire is known  
To have fallen. Behold, our strength and renowned valor have gone far away.  
Walter, the light of Pannonia, has departed from here,  
And he has led away my dear foster child Hiltgunt as well."  
Now the prince was kindled with wild and exceeding anger,  
And their grieving hearts exchanged their former joy for sorrow.  
He tore his royal robe from his shoulders all the way to the bottom,  
And turned his sorrowful mind now here, now there.  
  
Section 1.10.  
  
And just as sand is stirred up by Aeolian tempests,  
Even so the king fluctuated on all sides with internal anxieties,  
And his shifting facial expressions, mirroring his shifting heart,  
Betrayed on the outside whatever he was enduring within,  
For his rage would not permit him to utter a single word.  
Indeed, on that day he loathed both food and drink,  
Nor could his anxiety allow his limbs any peaceful rest.  
For when black night finally stripped all things of their color,  
He collapsed onto his bed, but could not close his eyes,  
Propping himself now on his right side, now on his left.  
Then, just as if pierced through the chest by a sharp javelin,  
He throbbed and tossed his head now here, now there,  
And at times, starting up frantically, he sat upon his bed.  
Finding no comfort in this, he finally rose and ran about the city,  
And returning to his couch, he felt it and departed from it at once.  
In such a manner did Attila spend a sleepless night.  
But the fleeing companions, traveling through the friendly silence,  
Hastened to leave that dire land far behind their backs.  
Hardly had the next day broken when the king, calling his nobles together,  
Said: "Oh, if anyone would bring back to me the fleeing Walter,  
Bound fast like a worthless hound,  
I would straightaway clothe him in gold refined multiple times.  
Indeed, as he stood, I would weigh him down on all sides with it,  
And while he still lived, I would completely fill his path with talents!"  
But no lord in that vast region was found,  
Neither captain, nor count, nor soldier, nor servant.  
However much anyone desired to display his own strength,  
Win everlasting praise by his valor,  
Or strive do that while stuffing his purses with treasure,  
None presumed to pursue Walter when he was incensed in arms,  
Or to face the man with a drawn sword.  
For his valor was well known. They too had experienced how great  
A slaughter the victor could inflict without taking a wound.  
Nor was the king able to persuade a single one of his men  
Who was willing to accept the promised talents on this condition.  
Walter, fleeing, as I said, traveled by night,  
And by day, seeking out woodlands and dense thickets,  
He caught wild birds by his skill, summoning them with his art,  
Trapping them now with birdlime, now within a split twig.  
But when he came to where rivers flowed in winding curves,  
He cast in his hook, snatching his prey from beneath the swirling water,  
To drive away the plague of hunger by enduring this labor.  
And during the entire time of their flight, the praiseworthy hero,  
The man Walter, completely abstained from the embrace of the maiden.  
  
End of Book One.  

## Book Two.  

Section 2.0.  
  
Behold, forty times the sun had completed its circuit  
Since he had departed from the Pannonian city.  
On the very day that brought this number to a close,  
He reached the river just as evening was half spent,  
Namely the Rhine, where its current flows toward the city  
Named Worms, brilliant as a royal seat.  
There, to pay his fare, he gave the fish caught earlier,  
And as soon as he was ferried across, he walked on rapidly, panting.  
After the dawning day had scattered the black shadows,  
The ferryman arose and went into the aforesaid city,  
And to the royal cook, who was master over the rest,  
He delivered the fish which that traveler had given.  
When the cook had seasoned them with spices and served them  
To King Gunther, the king, marvelling, spoke from his high seat:  
"France has never shown me fish of such a kind.  
I believe they are from foreign lands.  
Tell me at once, what countryman brought them?"  
And he, answering, related what the boatman had given him.  
The ruler commanded that same man to be summoned.  
Then, when he arrived, he was questioned about the matter.  
He spoke these words and laid out the story in order:  
"Yesterday evening I was sitting on the bank of the Rhine,  
And I spotted a traveler approaching in haste,  
Looking fully prepared in every limb for battle,  
For he was completely clad in bronze, noble king,  
And as he walked, he carried a shield and a gleaming spear.  
Indeed, he looked like a valiant warrior, and though he bore  
A massive burden, he still marched with a brisk stride.  
A maiden, adorned with an incredible radiance of beauty,  
Followed behind him, treading close upon his heels.  
She herself led a sturdy horse by the reins,  
Which bore two sizeable chests upon its back.  
These, whenever the steed tossed its high neck  
And proudly sought to curve its bounding legs,  
Made a clattering sound, as if one were striking gold with gems.  
This man gave me the fish here as a payment."  
When Hagano heard this, for he was sitting at the table,  
He spoke out joyfully from the bottom of his heart:  
"Rejoice with me, I beg you, for I recognize these traits!  
My comrade Walter has returned from the Huns."  
  
Section 2.1.  
  
Gunther, the prince, made arrogant by this news,  
Cried aloud, and soon the whole court shouted back to him.  
"Rejoice with me, I command you,” he said, “that I have lived to see this!  
The treasure which Gibicho sent to the eastern king,  
The Almighty has now sent back here into my kingdom."  
Thus he spoke, and kicked the table away as he leapt up.  
Then he ordered his horse to be led out and equipped with a carved saddle,  
And from all the people he chose twelve men  
Remarkable for strength, and thoroughly proven in spirit.  
Among these he commanded Hagano to go along with him.  
He, mindful of ancient loyalty and of his former comrade,  
Strove to turn his lord away from the enterprise he had begun.  
The king, however, urged on regardless and spoke in reply:  
"Do not delay, men! Gird your strong bodies with iron.  
Let the plated armor now cover your backs.  
Shall this man carry away so much treasure from the shores of the Franks?"  
Equipped with weapons, for the king’s command urged them on,  
They marched out of the gates, longing to see you, Walter,  
And hoping to rob a defenseless man of his riches.  
Hagano still strove by all means to stop them,  
But the unhappy king refused to change his mind about what he had begun.  
Meanwhile, the great-hearted man, continuing on from the river,  
Had come into the forest even then called the Vosges.  
For it is a massive wood, vast, containing many dens  
Of wild beasts, and accustomed to echo with hounds and horns.  
In a secluded spot there are two neighboring mountains,  
Between which lies a pleasant, though narrow, cave,  
Formed not by a hollow in the earth, but by the crest of the crags,  
A station perfectly well-suited for bloodthirsty robbers.  
This nook bore green and delicate grass.  
"Here," said the youth as soon as he saw it, "let us go here.  
It pleases me to rest my weary body in this camp."  
For after he had departed in flight from the territory of the Avars,  
He had tasted the rest of sleep in no other way  
Than by leaning upon his shield, scarcely ever closing his eyes.  
Then, at last, laying down his heavy weapons,  
He stretched out in the maiden's lap, saying: "Look around carefully,  
Hiltgund, and if you see a dark cloud of dust arise,  
Warn me to get up with a gentle touch.  
Even if you see a huge battle-host approaching,  
Be careful not to rouse me suddenly from sleep, my dear!  
For from afar you are able to cast your clear vision.  
Diligently scout the whole region all around!"  
  
Section 2.2.  
  
Thus he spoke, and he closed his own shining eyes.
And now for a long time he enjoyed his greatly desired rest.
But when Gunther saw the tracks in the dust,
He urged his swift steed on with cruel spurs,
And exulting in spirit, he spoke thus in vain to the winds:
"Hasten, men! Right now you will capture him as he goes.
Never today shall he escape. He will leave behind the stolen treasures!"
But the renowned Hagano soon returned these words in reply:
"One thing only I tell you, bravest of kings.
If you had seen Walter fight as many times
As I have, and as many times raging with fresh slaughter,
Never, perhaps, would you think he could be despoiled so easily.  
I have seen the Pannonian battle lines when they stirred up wars  
Against the northern or the southern regions.  
There, Walter, gleaming with his own valor,  
Moved about, hated by his enemies and marveled at by his allies.  
Whoever clashed with him soon saw the Underworld.  
O king and companions, believe an expert. How mightily  
He rises behind his shield! With what a whirlwind he hurls his spear!"  
But while Gunther, weighed down by an unsound mind,  
Could by no means be turned back, they drew near the camp.  
And Hiltgund, looking out from afar from the mountain peak,  
Perceived them coming by the dust that was raised,  
And with a gentle touch she warned Walter to wake.  
He, lifting his head, scanned to see if anyone was approaching.  
She reported that, in the distance, a certain battle host was speeding toward them.  
He, wiping his cleared eyes to purge them of the film of sleep,  
Gradually clad his stiff limbs in iron,  
And gathered up again his heavy shield and his spear.  
Then, leaping up, he pierced the empty air with his blade,  
And wielded his weapons in a swift rehearsal for the bitter battle.  
Close at hand, behold, the woman saw the gleaming spears,  
And, completely struck with dread, she said: "The Huns are here!"  
Then, falling to the earth, she spoke these words in sorrow:  
"I beseech you, my lord, let my neck be severed by your sword,  
So that I, who have not merited to be joined to you in the pledged marriage bed,  
May never suffer the embrace of any other flesh."  
  
Section 2.3.  
  
Then the youth said: "Shall innocent blood stain me?  
And how by any means is my sword able to lay low  
Enemies, if it does not now spare so faithful a friend?  
Far be it from me to do what you ask. Lay aside your heart's terror!  
He who has often rescued me from various dangers,  
He is able, I believe, to confound our enemies."  
Thus he spoke, and lifting his eyes, he said to her:  
"No Avars are present here, but Frankish rascals,  
Dwellers of this region, and"—lo, he caught sight  
Of Hagano's helmet, and, recognizing it, he added with a laugh:  
"And here is my old comrade and companion, Hagano."  
With this word spoken, the hero went to the entrance of his site,  
And to the woman, who stood further back, he said this beforehand:  
"Before this gate I now throw out a proud boast.  
From here, no returning Frank shall presume to tell his wife  
That he carried away any of this vast treasure with impunity."  
He had not yet finished his speech when, behold,  
He collapsed to the ground and begged forgiveness for speaking such things.  
After he arose, scanning the men all the more carefully, he said:  
"Of these men whom I see, with the exception of Hagano,  
I fear none. For he has already learned my ways through battles,  
And he, being quite shrewd, also possesses the art of war.  
If, by God's will, I can disrupt his maneuvers alone,  
Then I shall be saved from the battle for you, my bride Hiltgund," he said.  
But when Hagano saw that Walter was entrenched  
In such a stronghold, he soon advised the proud ruler with these words:  
"O lord, desist from provoking this man in war!  
First, let men go forward to ask him about everything,  
His lineage, his country, his name, and the place he left behind,  
Or if, perhaps, he may seek peace, offering us the treasure without bloodshed.  
Through his response we can know the man,  
And if it is Walter who is staying there,  
He is wise. Perhaps he will yield out of respect for your honor."  
The king ordered a man named Camalo to go,  
Whom renowned France had sent to the city of Metz  
As governor, and who had arrived there bringing gifts  
One day before the prince found out about these matters.  
Giving his horse the reins, he flew, and very like the swift east wind,  
He traversed the space of the plain and approached the youth,  
And thus accosted him as he stood in his way: "Tell me, man, who  
You are, or where you come from, and where you intend to go?"  
  
Section 2.4.  
  
The great-hearted hero replied, speaking these words:  
"Did you come of your own accord or did someone send you here?  
I would like to know." Camalo then returned with a proud mouth:  
"Know that King Gunther, powerful in this land,  
Has sent me to go and ask about your circumstances."  
Having listened to this, the youth offered this response:  
"I completely fail to see what need there may be to scrutinize  
A traveler's circumstances. Yet I do not hesitate to disclose them.  
I am called Walter, born of the Aquitanians.  
By my father, as a small boy, for the sake of a hostage,  
I was given to the Huns. There I lived, and now I have departed,  
Desiring to see again my native land and my beloved people."  
The messenger replied to this: "The aforementioned ruler commands you through me  
To surrender the horse with the chests, and the maiden.  
If you do this promptly, he will grant you your life and your limbs."  
Walter confidently brought forth these words in reply:  
"I have never heard a more foolish sophistry.  
Lo, you mention that a prince, I know not who,  
Promises what he does not hold, nor perhaps will hold.  
Is he God, that he can rightfully grant me  
My life? Has he touched me with his hands? Has he thrust me into prison,  
Or twisted my palms behind my back with chains?  
And yet, listen. If he releases me from combat,  
For I see he is clad in iron, and is thus here for battle,  
Then I will send him one hundred arm-rings made of true red metal,  
By which to honor the name and status of the king."  
With this answer received, that man departed,  
And, when received by the leaders, he related Walter’s offer.  
Then Hagano said to the king: "Accept the offered treasure!  
With this, father, you are able to reward those accompanying you.  
Recall now to restrain your hand from battle!  
Walter is unknown to you, as is his supreme valor.  
And a vision portended to me this past night,  
That if we join battle, all things will turn out unfavorably for us.  
For it seemed to me that you were wrestling with a bear,  
Which, after a long conflict, bitingly tore away from you  
An entire leg, with the ham, all the way up to the thigh.  
And soon it attacked me as I, wielding my weapons, came to assist you.  
Then it tore out one of my eyes along with my teeth."  
  
Section 2.5.  
  
The proud king, noticing these things, shouted:  
"As I see, you yourself imitate your father Agathias.  
He too carried a thoroughly terrified mind within his cold breast,  
And used many words to cast aspersions on battle."  
Then the hero would have justly conceived a great anger,  
If only it was lawful to be angry against one’s lord.  
"Lo," he said, "let all things depend on your weapons.  
He whom you seek is in sight. Let everyone fight.  
You all stand close at hand, nor is anyone currently hindered by fear.  
Permit me to merely observe the outcome and I will abstain from the spoils."  
Having said this, he soon he sought a neighboring hill,  
And, dismounting from his horse, he sat down and watched from there.  
After this, Gunther commanded Camalo, saying:  
"Go, and command that the whole treasure be surrendered to me.  
But if he hesitates, I know you are a brave and bold man.  
Clash with him, and soon despoil him as one conquered in war!"  
Camalo, the governor of Metz, went forward.  
A yellow helmet gleamed on his head, and armor on his breast.  
Then, shouting from afar, he said: "Hey, friend, listen!  
Send all the treasure to the king of the Franks  
If you wish to have life or safety any longer!"  
The most valiant hero kept silent for a short while at his word,  
Waiting for the fierce enemy to approach closer.  
Speeding forward, the messenger repeated that speech:  
"Send all the treasure to the king of the Franks!"  
Then the steadfast youth brought forth this reply:  
"What do you seek, or what do you urge me to send back, insolent man?  
Did I steal these things from King Gunther?  
Or did he give me anything for my own gain,  
That he should justly compel me to pay so great a recompense?  
As I traveled, did I do any damage to your region,  
Through which I might justly be despoiled by you?  
If your kind bears such envy toward everyone  
That it permits no one passing by to tread upon its soil,  
Behold, I will buy my passage. I will send two hundred  
Arm rings to the king. Let him grant peace now, and set warfare aside."  
After Camalo perceived these things with a ferocious heart,  
He said: "You will give a greater gift than that when you open the chests.  
For now, I wish to end all discussion.  
Either give up what we seek or you will pour out your life with your blood."  
  
Section 2.6.  
  
Thus he spoke, and, gathering his triple-layered shield upon his arm,  
He brandished his gleaming spear-shaft, and strove with all his might  
To hurl it. But the youth, being very cautious, avoided the blow.  
The flying spear dealt the earth a harmless wound.  
Walter at last said: "If this is how it pleases you, let us play!"  
And even as he spoke, he sent his spear across. Then it  
Passed through the left side of the shield-boss, and behold,  
It struck the hand with which Camalo had begun to draw his sword,  
Pinning it to his thigh, and piercing through the back of his horse as well.  
Without delay, as soon as the steed felt the wound, it raged and,  
Bucking its back, attempted to throw its rider.  
And perhaps it would have done so, if the fixed lance were not holding him.  
Meanwhile Camalo let go of his shield and, grasping the spear  
With his left hand, he scrambled to wrench his right hand free.  
Perceiving this right away, the highly renowned hero ran up  
And, pressing down with his foot, drove his sword in up to the hilt.  
Then, drawing it out, he pulled the spear from the wound at the same time.  
The horse and its master then fell in the selfsame hour.  
And when, by chance, Camalo's nephew caught sight of this,  
The son of his very brother, Kimo by nickname,  
Who, according to some, was called Scaramund by name,  
He groaned, and in deep sorrow accosted them all with tears:  
"Alas, this turn of events concerns me above all others.  
Now I shall either die together with him or avenge my dear friend."  
For the narrowness of the place compelled them to clash only one on one,  
Nor was anyone able to run to the aid of another.  
The unhappy Scaramund flew forward, already doomed to die,  
Brandishing in his hand two spears with broad iron heads.  
When he saw that Walter was moved by no terror  
And stood firm, staying in that very spot,  
He spoke thus, gnashing his teeth and tossing the horse-tail crest  
Of his helmet: "In what is your confidence? In what is your hope?  
I do not now seek your treasure or any of your goods.  
I only demand your life on behalf of my slain kinsman!"  
The other then replied: "If I am proven to be the first who sought  
This battle, or if I have deserved any of this, so that I might  
Justly suffer such things, let your spear pierce me without delay."  
Not yet had he finished his speech when, behold, Scaramund  
Hurled one of his two spears at him,  
And then the other just as quick. Of these, the highly renowned hero  
Avoided one, and smacked the second down with his shield-boss.  
  
Section 2.7.  
  
Then, drawing the edge of his sharp sword, Scaramund  
Rushed at the youth, desiring to cleave his brow,  
And, though his unbridled horse carried him closer to him,  
He was unable to deal his head a balanced blow,  
But struck his hilt against his helmet. Ringing out, it gave a sharp  
Clink, and at the same time sent sparks into the air.  
But he could not turn his proud steed around  
Before Walter had driven a thrust of his spear-point under his chin  
And lifted him up, dying, from his high saddle.  
Severing the head of the pleading man with his own blade,  
He caused the blood of the kinsmen to flow together.  
When the arrogant Gunther saw that this man had perished,  
He exhorted his raging companions to renew the battle:  
"Let us attack him, and not allow him to catch his breath  
Until he grows weary and fails. After that, he will be bound,  
And he shall return the treasures and pay the penalty for this bloodshed."  
Behold, Werinhard was the third to go out and provoke a fight.  
He was a descendant born from a long lineage,  
O renowned Pandarus. He was your kinsman and a lover of the craft you mastered,  
You who once, when commanded to shatter a treaty,  
Were the first to hurl a weapon into the midst of the Achaians.  
This man, scorning the spear, carried a quiver and a bow,  
Disturbing Walter by launching arrows from afar in unequal combat.  
Against him, however, that manly hero  
Stood firm, opposing him with the curve of his seven-layered shield,  
Using his foresight to repeatedly dodge the oncoming blows.  
For now he leaped aside, now he inclined his shield to the south  
And deflected the missiles, so that not one touched him.  
After the descendant of Pandarus saw that he had spent his arrows  
In vain. In his anger, he soon drew his sword,  
And, flying forward at last, he hurled these words from his mouth:  
"Though you have dodged these wind-swift shots, you clever man,  
Now you may feel the blow of my armed right hand!"  
Walter, addressing him with a laughing heart, replied:  
"I have waited long enough for combat to be waged  
With proper gravitas. Hurry, there will be no delay on my part."  
He had spoken, and straining with his whole body, he hurled  
His spear. The flying lance laid open the horse's breast.  
The quadruped reared up straight, lashed the air with its hooves,  
And, throwing its rider, fell down upon him.  
The youth ran up and by force wrested his sword from him.  
  
Section 2.8.  
  
With his helmet dashed away, the hero grasped his white hair,  
And spoke thus to the man who stitched many prayers together:  
"Not long ago, you spewed such words through the air."  
Thus he spoke, and left him a torso, his neck severed.  
But the three corpses he beheld did not terrify the mad  
Gunther. He commanded the next in turn to hasten to his death.  
Behold, Ekiurid, born on the Saxon shores,  
Was the fourth to try his hand at battle. He had fled there as an exile  
For the slaying of a certain chieftain.  
A chestnut horse with a variety of spots bore him.  
When he saw that Walter was ready for combat,  
He said: "Tell me whether you live in a solid body,  
Or whether you deceive us, accursed man, through phantom shapes of the air.  
Indeed, you seem to me a faun accustomed to the woodlands."  
And he, rising up with a loud laugh, gave this answer:  
"Your Celtic tongue proves you were sired by that race  
To whom nature gave the gift of surpassing others in mockery.  
But if I lay my hands on you as you draw closer,  
You will scarcely be able to tell to the Saxons later  
That you saw a faun's phantom here in the Vosges."  
"I will certainly test what you are," Ekiurid said, and instantly  
He hurled his heavy, iron-tipped cornel-wood spear. It flashed forth,  
Launched from its twisted thong, but the hard shield-boss shattered it.  
Walter gave answer in his turn by launching his own spear-point:  
"The sylvan faun sends these gifts across to you.  
See whether our weapon is more piercing."  
The lance split the wood covered in bull's hide  
And, tearing through his tunic, lodged in his lung.  
The unhappy Ekiurid collapsed and vomited a river  
Of blood. Behold, fleeing death, he ran straight into it.  
The youth chased his horse into the field behind him.  
The fifth to fight was Hadaward. Deluded by his puffed-up ego,  
He demanded Gunther himself to bring him his shield.  
Advancing, he left his spear for his companions to hold,  
Boldly but vainly trusting to his sword alone.  
  
Section 2.9.  
  
Then, when he saw that fallen corpses completely  
Blocked the path, and that his horse was unable to pass,  
He leapt down, preparing to go on foot. Valiant in his armor stood  
Walter, and praised the man who offered him an equal  
Condition for fighting. Hadaward then said to him:  
"O serpent, skilled in wiles and conscious of deceit,  
Accustomed to hide your limbs within a scaly covering,  
And coiled snakelike into a single loop,  
You avoid so many weapons without even a slight wound from a blow,  
And rudely elude the venomous arrows!  
Do you think that this stroke as well will be dodged by cunning?  
A stroke which my right hand, at point blank, sends with a steady balance?  
For this hand sends no small dart, nor a trifling wound.  
Hear my advice, lay down your painted shield!  
I seek it as my spoil, and the king’s pledge guarantees it too.  
Truly, I do not want you to damage it, for it is pleasing to my eyes.  
Moreover, even if you should snatch the sweet light of life from me,  
There are many more companions and blood-kinsmen present here,  
Who, even if you make like a bird and take to flight,  
Will never suffer you to depart from us unharmed."  
The warrior, however, was not at all terrified. He brought forth these words in reply:  
"About the rest I keep silent. My care is to defend my shield.  
Believe me, I am its debtor for its good services.  
It used to oppose itself regularly to my enemies,  
And in place of my own wounds, it received wounds for me.  
You see for yourself how opportune it has been for me today.  
Were it absent, you would perhaps not be speaking with Walter.  
O right hand, take care to drive back the enemy with your utmost strength,  
Lest he tear you away from the rampart of this wall!  
Left hand, strive to hold fast to the pin of the shield-boss,  
And wrap your fingers around the ivory as if fixed with glue!  
Do not lose your strength here, you who have carried such weights  
Through so vast a distance of travel from the high strongholds of the Avars!"  
The other then replied: "If you refuse to do it willingly, you shall do it unwillingly.  
And not only the shield, but the horse with the maiden and the gold  
You will surrender. Then at last you will pay for your crimes with torments."  
Thus he spoke, and tore his well-known sword from its scabbard.  
Born in different parts of the world, they clashed  
Together. The Vosges marveled at their lightning flashes and blows.  
They, sublime in spirit and grand in their armor,  
The one trusting in his sword, the other fierce and towering with his spear,  
Joined battle together with great and powerful force.  
Not so loudly does the dark holm-oak resound when struck by axes  
As their helmets rang out and their shields echoed back.  
  
Section 2.10.  
  
The Franks marvelled that the hero Walter did not tire,  
For no rest or breathing room was granted to him.  
Now thinking he could strike with impunity, the man of Worms  
Leapt forward, and rose up fiercely with his sword held high,  
Believing that with this blow he would end the duel.  
But the foresighted youth intercepted the striker by driving  
His spear-point forward, and forced him to drop his blade before he knew it.  
The sword glinted far off in the brambles,  
And when he saw himself despoiled of it,  
He wished to quicken his flight and make for the bushes.  
The son of Alphere, relying on his swift feet and youthful vigor,  
Pursued him, saying: "Where are you fleeing? Take your shield with you!"  
Thus he spoke, and, swiftly raising his spear with both hands,  
He struck. The man fell. His huge shield crashed loudly upon him.  
Nor did the youth delay. He pressed his foot upon his neck, and, tearing  
The shield away with his spear, he pinned him to the earth.  
The man, rolling his eyes, exhaled his spirit into the air.  
Patafrid was the sixth to fight. Hagano’s own sister  
Had given him birth. When his uncle saw him step forward,  
He tried to turn him back from there with shouts and prayers,  
Crying out: "Where are you rushing? Behold how death  
Grins at you! Stop! Behold how the Fates are gathering  
Their final threads! O dear nephew, your own mind deceives you.  
Cease! You are clearly no match for Walter’s strength."  
Yet the unhappy youth, continuing on, scorned all these words,  
For he burned in his veins, desiring to capture glory.  
Saddened, Hagano drew long sighs from his breast  
And poured out these words from the bottom of his heart:  
"O whirlpool of the world, insatiable hunger for possession,  
Abyss of avarice, root of all evils!  
O that you would swallow only cruel metal, that dire element,  
With other riches, and leave men alone unharmed!  
But now, blowing through men with your perverse power,  
You inflame them, and no one is content with his own things. Behold,  
They do not tremble to run into a shameful death for the sake of profit.  
The more they hold, the more the thirst for having burns.  
They possess themselves of strangers’ goods, now by force, now by stealth,  
And, what stirs up tears and renews groans the more,  
They thrust heaven-born souls down into the furnace of Erebus.  
  
Section 2.11.  
  
Behold, I cannot call back my beloved nephew,  
For he has been goaded on by you, O savage desire.  
Lo, he hastens blind to taste an unspeakable death,  
And for the sake of cheap glory he desires to go down to the shades.  
Alas for me, dear nephew! What message, ruined boy, do you send to your mother?  
Who, dear one, will comfort your recently wedded bride,  
To whom you, snatched from hope, have not even given the playful joy of a child?  
What madness possesses you? From where has this dementia come?"  
Thus he spoke, and bedewed his breast with welling tears.  
Then, with choking sobs, he uttered a long "farewell, beautiful boy."  
Walter, although far off, noticed that his companion was sorrowful,  
And the outcry reached his ears at the same time.  
Therefore, he addressed the charging horseman in this way:  
"Accept my advice, most renowned youth,  
And save yourself to enjoy a better fate.  
Stop, for your burning confidence deceives you!  
See the deaths of so many heroes and yield from the duel,  
Lest, by meeting your end, you make more enemies for me."  
"Why do you care about my death, tyrant?" he answered.  
"Now you must fight, not make speeches."  
He spoke, and he aimed his knotty spear after his words.  
But the hero parried, deflecting it with his own spear-point.  
Carried past by its flight and driven by the might of the raging youth,  
It entered the camp and stuck fast before the maiden's feet.  
She, struck with terror, uttered a womanly cry.  
But after the faint blood returned to her heart,  
She looked up a little to watch and see whether the hero was still alive.  
Then the brave man ordered the Frank to depart from the war again,  
But he, raging, bared his sword and, rushed at him,  
Seeking him out and driving a blow down from above.  
The son of Alphere at last shook his well-fitted shield,  
And gnashing his teeth in the manner of a foaming boar, he kept silent.  
The other, wishing to strike, exposed his whole body as he leaned forward  
Into the blow, but Walter, bending beneath his covering,  
Sheltered himself and drew his body back. Then, behold,  
The foolish youth was baffled by his own stroke, and fell forward.  
It would have been the end, except that, resting his knees upon the earth,  
The warrior crouched low and used his shield to protect himself from the steel.  
  
Section 2.12.  
  
As one of them rose up, the other roused himself at the same time,  
And all too quickly, trembling, he thrust his shield out in front of him,  
And prepared in vain to renew the contest. But at him  
The son of Alphere quickly lunged with his sword, dropping his spear,  
And with a massive strike he sheared away the middle section of his shield,  
Cutting through his chain-mail coat and baring his flanks.  
The unhappy Patafrid fell, staring at his own entrails,  
And gave his body to the woodland beasts, his soul to Orcus.  
Promising to avenge him, Gerwit advanced,  
Who, carried on a strong horse, flew over the entire  
Mound of corpses which had blocked the narrow path ahead.  
And just as the warrior had cut off the neck of the fallen boy,  
He arrived and brandished a two-edged battle-axe at his face,  
For weapons of this very kind belonged to the Franks back then.  
The agile hero threw up his shield and foiled the stroke,  
And, leaping backward, he snatched up his friendly spear,  
And laid down his blood-stained sword upon the green sedge.  
Here indeed you might see a terrifying battle between men.  
To be sure, there was no talking amidst the weapons of war,  
So completely were their minds intent on the adversarial duel.  
The one raged so that vengeance might cleanse his slaughtered comrades.  
The other strove with every effort to defend his life,  
And, if fortune should grant it, to hold the palm of victory.  
This one struck, that one guarded. The one attacked, the other parried.  
Chance and valor were mingled together in their striving.  
Yet the long spear kept the enemy, who was armed with a shorter weapon,  
At a distance, though he still wheeled his horse around  
And desired to trick the weary man.  
Now weighed down more and more by a mass of fury,  
Walter lifted the bottom of Gerwit’s shield,  
And driving his blade across, the iron penetrated through his groin into his thigh.  
Falling backward, he let out a black cry,  
And lamenting his destruction, beat the earth with his heels.  
Him too he left a torso with a severed neck.  
This man had previously been a count in the lands of Worms.  
Then, for the first time, perhaps, the Franks began to hesitate,  
And with great entreaties they begged their lord to withdraw from the fight.  
The wretched king raged, and blindly spoke out:  
"I beg you, brave men and hearts often proven,  
Let this turn of events bring not fear, but anger to everyone!  
For how could I return to Worms so completely inglorious?  
Let each man claim my mind as his own. Behold, I am prepared  
To die now rather than enter Worms with such deeds in remembrance.  
Shall this victor march to his homeland without bloodshed?  
Up to this point you burned to rob the man of his metals.  
Now burn, men, to cleanse the blood that has been poured out,  
So that death may wipe away death, and blood may wipe away blood,  
And the wound of the slayer may comfort the slaughter of our comrades!"  
  
Section 2.13.  
  
With these words, the mad king inflamed their spirits and made  
Them all at once unmindful of life and safety.  
And, just like in games, each man strove to outrun the other  
To his death. But the narrowness, as I said before,  
Compelled them to enter the warzone only two at a time.

And hung his crests, torn from his helmet, upon a tree.  
Then, catching the breeze, he wiped away his sweat, panting.  
Behold, suddenly the champion Randolf, outstripping the rest  
On his swift steed, approached him insolently,  
And straightaway attacked him beneath the breast with an iron-tipped pike.  
And if the work of Wayland, with its hardened rings,  
Had not withstood it, the thick wood would have penetrated his flanks.  
He, however, struck with sudden fear in his heart,  
Recovered his wits and threw up the defense of his shield.  
Yet there had been no chance to pick up his helmet again.  
Then the Frank, having hurled his spear, bared his sword,  
And, striking the top of the Aquitanean's head, shaved away  
Two locks of hair, but fate did not permit him to graze  
Even the surface of the skin. Then again he brandished another blow,  
And, headlong in his passion, he drove his blade into the direct barrier  
Of the shield, but could not pluck it out with all his strength.  
Then the son of Alphere, flinging himself backward like a lightning flash,  
Cast the Frank down to the grass with mighty force,  
And, standing over him, trampled his breast, saying:  
"Behold, in exchange for the baldness of my head, I despoil you of yours,  
Lest this boast of yours about me should be made to your bride."  
Hardly had he spoken these words when he truncated the neck of the pleading man.  
But Helmnod succeeded as the ninth in the fight.  
He bore a trident attached to a triple-stranded rope,  
Which his companions held fast, standing behind his back.  
The plan was that, while the launched point was lodged  
In the shield, they should all strive to pull together at once,  
So that even thus they might bear down the raging man.  
And under this hope they set for themselves a certain triumph.  
Without delay, the leader, pouring all his strength into his arms,  
Sent the trident against his adversary, crying out with a loud voice  
And declaring: "Beneath this iron, behold your doom, baldhead!"  
  
Section 2.14.  
  
Then, piercing the air, it flashed like a javelin,  
A type of serpent which drops from a high tree  
With such a whirlwind that it overcomes all obstacles.  
Why do I delay? It split the shield-boss and settled upon the buckler.  
The Franks raised a shout and the woodland echoed back,  
And, straining hard, they pulled the rope all together and in turn,  
Nor did the prince hesitate to join in this labor.  
Rivers of sweat poured from the limbs of all.  
But yet amidst this the hero stood like an oak tree  
Which equally seeks the stars with its foliage and hell with its roots,  
Motionless in spite of all the crashing winds.  
The enemies strove and encouraged one another man by man,  
So that, if they could not hurl him down to the very ground,  
They might at least strive to wrench away the defense of his shield,  
Which being removed, he would easily be captured alive by them.  
I shall now declare the remaining names of those who were pulling:  
The ninth was Eleuthir, called by the surname Helmnod.  
The very city of Strasbourg gave Trogus as the tenth.  
The mighty city of Speyer brought forth Tanastus as the eleventh.  
In place of Hagano, the king filled the twelfth spot.  
These four against one with supreme effort  
Contended equally with a great and varied tumult.  
Meanwhile, the vain labor instilled anger into Walter,  
And he who already long ago had bared his brow of its helmet,  
Trusting at once in his spear and his bronze tunic,  
Let go of his shield. Then he attacked Eleuthir first.  
Cleaving his helmet, he scattered his brains, and,  
Cutting through his very neck, laid open his chest, while the failing  
Heart, throbbing, quickly left behind its life and its warmth.  
Next he attacked Trogus, who was still clinging to the accursed rope.  
  
Section 2.15.  
  
He, suddenly terrified by the death of his falling comrade  
And by the horrifying sight of his enemy, began to attempt  
A sharp flight. In vain he wished, that having left them behind,  
He might gather up his weapons, and so renew the struggle,  
For all who were about to pull the rope had laid down  
Their spears along with their shields. But as much as the great hero  
Had proven stronger, so much was he swifter. Having caught up  
With him in his run, he cut through his calves with his blade,  
And, having slowed him thus, he got ahead of him and carried off his  
Shield. But Trogus, although faint from his wound,  
Still burned in mind, and looked round for a huge rock,  
Which, seizing suddenly, he hurled at his straining enemy,  
And split his own shield from the top straight down to the bottom.  
But the leather covering on top held the broken wood together.  
Then, dropping quickly to his knee, he cleared a green space  
And, blazing in spirit, he terrified the air with his brandishing,  
And even if he could not show his valor in deeds,  
Yet by his heart and his tongue he revealed a manly character.  
Then, seeing that his death doomed spirit was in grief, he boldly spoke:  
"O if only my shield, a friend even in moments like this, were now at hand!  
It was not illustrious valor but chance which brought you victory  
Over me, to raise your sword here over my shield!"  
Then the hero, smiling again, said, "I am coming now,"  
And, flying forward as he ran, he cut off the striker's right hand.  
But when the champion aimed a second blow from above  
And was about to make an opening for the departing soul,  
Behold, Tanastus arrived, took up his weapons with the king,  
And protected his comrade from a wound by interposing his buckler.  
Incensed by this, Walter turned his wrath upon him  
And tore his shoulder from its joint.  
Then, drawing the iron through his side, he poured out his vitals.  
"Farewell!" murmured Tanastus with his lips as he fell forward.  
As he collapsed, Trogus disdained to offer up prayers,  
But inflamed his conqueror with bitter insults,  
Whether from strength of mind, or due to despair. Thereupon  
The son of Alphere said: "Die, and carry this down to hell,  
Telling your comrades you have avenged them."  
With these words, he cast a golden necklace around his neck.  
Behold, at the same moment the slain friends rolled in the dust,  
Striking the befouled ground with twitching blows.  
  
End of Book Two.  

## Book Three.  

Section 3.0.  
  
The unhappy king sighed when he saw this, and abandoning  
All his pride, he mounted the back of his caparisoned horse,  
And flew swiftly to the sorrowful Hagano.  
He strove with every kind of prayer to move him  
To go with him and renew the battle. But he replied:  
"My shameful ancestral lineage forbids me to fight,  
And cold blood robs me of my courage in arms.  
For my father used to waste away whenever he saw weapons,  
And the fearful man used many words to cast aspersions on battle.  
Since you boasted of these things, O king, among your companions,  
Our assistance has surely proved worthless to you."  
Nonetheless, begging him continuously though he refused,  
The king tried to win back the averted man with these words:  
"I beg you by the gods above, lay aside the anger you harbor.  
Shake off the wrath brought on by my own fault,  
Which, if life remains and I return home with you,  
I will wash away with many rich favors bestowed upon you.  
Are you not ashamed, with so many comrades and kinsmen slain,  
To hide your manhood? Indeed, as it seems to me,  
Words have more power to move your mind than heinous deeds.  
More justly would your heart rage against that savage tyrant,  
Who alone today has defiled the crown of the world.  
We suffer no small loss from the slaughter of our men,  
But France will never overcome so great a disgrace.  
Those who once feared us will now hiss and say,  
'Oh, the shame of it! The entire army of the Franks  
Was slaughtered with impunity by a single, nameless man!'"  
Hagano still hesitated, and in his heart he kept turning over  
The loyalty he had pledged to Walter, while recalling  
The entire sequence of events in the unfolding tragedy.  
Yet the unhappy king pressed him even more suppliantly.  
Moved by the intensity of his humble pleading,  
Hagano blushed before his lord's face, and reflected on the honor  
Of his own valor, which might perhaps be cheapened  
If in any way he spared himself in this affair.  
At last he broke his silence, and answered thus in a clear voice:  
  
Section 3.1.  
  
"Where do you call me, my lord? Where shall I follow you, illustrious prince?  
Your confidence promises an impossible task to your heart.  
Who has ever been proven to be so foolish  
As to leap willingly into an open abyss?  
For I know Walter to be so fierce on the battlefield  
That, posted as he is in such a fortress and position,  
He scorns a massive troop as if it were a single little man.  
And even if France had sent all her horsemen and foot soldiers here together,  
He would have done to them just as he did to these.  
But because I see you grieve more from shame

Which, however, either does not appear anywhere, or does so only unwillingly.  
For because of my dear nephew, I confess to you, my lord,

Behold, I will go into certain danger for your sake, O king.  
But know that I shall withdraw entirely from fighting here.  
Let us move aside and afford him room to leave,

Until he securely deserts his narrow fortress,  
Thinking that we have gone. When he treads the open plains,  
Let us rise up and follow behind his startled back.  
Thus we shall be strong enough to attempt some deed of valor.  
In these uncertain matters, this is my most certain hope.  
Then you can fight, O king, if your mind is set on war.  
Indeed, he will never flee from the two of us,  
But it will be necessary for us either to fly or to fight fiercely."  
The ruler praised the counsel and embraced him,  
And caressed the man with a kiss. And behold, they withdrew,  
And looked around for a place well-suited for an ambush.  
Then, dismounting, they tethered their animals in the lush grass.  
Meanwhile, Phoebus was bending toward the western shores,  
Marking his final tracks through familiar Thule,  
And left the Iberians behind his back along with the Scots.  
After he had gradually warmed the ocean waves,  
Hesperus turned his horns toward the Ausonian lands.  
Then the wise hero began to debate within himself,  
Whether to stay in safety in the dense silence of the fortress,  
Or to commit himself to the vast fields of the wilderness.  
He seethed with immense waves of cares, and, as to what  
He should do now, he cleverly investigated that with sharp scrutiny.  
For he was suspicious of Hagano alone, and of that  
Kiss the king gave him when they embraced.  
  
Section 3.2.  
  
He had fully considered what intent might be in the mind  
Of the enemy, and whether they wished to return to the city they left,  
So that, having perhaps gathered more allies through the night,  
They might prepare at early dawn to renew the heinous battle,  
Or whether, alone, they would lay an ambush and lurk closer by.  
Moreover, the forest with its unknown paths terrified him at this crossroads,  
Lest he should happen upon rough places thick with thorns,  
Or indeed with wild beasts, and lose his bride to them.  
Having thus foreseen and weighed these matters, he spoke:  
"Behold, however things turn out, I shall lie down here  
Until the spinning sphere restores the beloved light,  
Lest that proud king should say I escaped the borders of my homeland  
By fleeing through the shadows in the manner of a thief."  
He spoke, and behold, he fortified the narrow approach with a rampart,  
Cutting down thorns and brambles on every side.  
When this was done, he turned toward the severed torsos with a bitter  
Groan, and prostrated his body toward the eastern quarter.  
Then, placing his head upon each and every one of them,  
While holding his naked sword, he prayed with these words:  
"To the Maker of the universe, and the Ruler of all creation,  
Without whose permission or final command nothing  
Endures, I give thanks that He has defended me from the hostile  
Weapons and the insults of the enemy throng.  
And I pray with a contrite heart to the merciful Lord,  
Who wishes not to destroy sinners but to destroy their sins,  
To grant that I may see these men in the heavenly dwelling."  
When he had made an end of praying, he rose up at once,  
And, turning the six horses around, he bound them in order  
With twisted twigs. These alone remained, since two  
Had been destroyed by weapons and King Gunther had driven off three.  
Having arranged these things, he loosened his armor and, sweating  
Profusely, relieved his body of its massive weight.  
Then comforting his sorrowful bride with cheerful speech,  
He soon took food and refreshed his weary limbs,  
For he was exceedingly tired, and, reclining upon his shield,  
He commanded the maiden to keep watch during his first sleep.  
He intended to take up the morning watch himself,  
Which was the more suspected hour. Then, at last, he found rest.  
Sitting at his head, she kept watch as was her custom,  
And kept her drowsy eyes open with song.  
  
Section 3.3.

He rose without delay and bade the maiden sleep on.  
Then, snatching up his spear, he briskly leaned upon it for support.

Going about among the horses. Soon he approached the rampart to listen closely,  
Hoping that light and shape might be restored to the world.  
Meanwhile Lucifer [the morning star], the herald of dawn, was climbing Olympus  
Proclaiming: "The island of Taprobane beholds the bright sun."  
It was the hour when the icy morning star bedews the earth.  
The young man set about stripping the slain of their weapons  
And their armor's gear, leaving behind the tunics and other clothes.  
Only the armlets, the sword-belts with their studs, and the swords,  
As well as the corselets and helmets, did he strip from them.

He placed her upon a fifth, while he himself mounted a sixth.

Or the rattling bridles of arrogant warriors,

After he saw that everything was silent, he sent the laden

For her fragile sex forced her heart to tremble,  
Looked back behind her and saw two men descending  
From a certain hill, moving rapidly and without restraint.

"The long-delayed end is now at hand. Fly, my lord, they are closing in!"

"In vain would my right hand have routed so many foes,  
If honor should now fade at last, and dishonor take its place.  
It is better to seek a glorious death through battlewounds  
Than to escape alone and wander aimlessly after losing everything.  
Yet the blessings of safety are not entirely to be despaired of  
By one who has looked upon greater perils before.  
Go, take the reins of gold-bearing Leo,  
And, moving more swiftly, seek shelter in the nearby grove!  
But I prefer to make my stand on the slope of the mountain,  
Greet those who approach, and await the outcome."  
  
Section 3.4.  
  
The noble maiden complied with the words of him who commanded.  
Swiftly he gathered his shield and shook his spear,  
Testing his skills in the armor of another knight.  
The king, madly rushing at him with an accompanying attendant,  
Challenged him from afar with an exceedingly arrogant speech:  
"Fierce enemy,” he said, “your efforts will delude you! Behold, your hiding places  
Are gone now. Like a common hound, you used to bark  
From inside them, gnashing your rabid teeth.  
Lo, if you will, fight out in the open field,  
To see if your end can by any chance be made equal

And therefore you despise flight or surrender."

But, as if deaf to him, he turned elsewhere, saying:  
"To you is my speech, Hagano. Halt a moment!  
What, I ask, has so suddenly changed so faithful a friend,  
That he who, when about to depart recently, seemed scarcely able  
To be torn from our embrace, but now, of his own accord,

And in the cultivation of which we spent our early years.  
Whither has vanished that glorious concord of ours,  
Which always endured at home and in the enemy's land, and knew no strife?  
Indeed, the sight of you made me forget my father,  
And my wide homeland grew cheap to me while dwelling with you.  
Do you wipe from your mind the faith so often pledged?  
I beg you, cut off this wickedness and do not provoke war,  
And let our alliance remain unshaken through the ages.  
If you agree to this, you shall depart enriched even now  
With blessings, and I will fill your shield-boss with ruddy gold."  
  
Section 3.5.  
  
Hagano, with a grim countenance, replied to this with these words,  
Thus letting loose his open rage:  
"How like you, Walter, to use force first, and only then to play the philosopher.  
You yourself cut off our bond of faith, when you saw that I was present  
And yet slaughtered so many of my comrades, and, indeed, my kinsmen.

Even if my face was hidden, you still saw my armor,

Any slower to act. All stood on foot, ready to do battle.  
Each man took his stance and cautiously braced himself  
Against the coming blow. Their martial limbs trembled beneath their shields.

The weapons of two conspired against one alone.  
Hagano, having shattered the peace, was the first to hurl  
His malicious spear with gathered strength. But as it came, flying

Threw his ashen lance, which, flying on, stuck in the bottom  
Of Walter's shield. But as soon as the hero shook it,  
The quivering iron fell right out of the hole in the wood.  
Dismayed by this omen, the Franks, with troubled hearts  
Drew their blades at once. Their grief turned to pure rage,  
And, covered by their shields, they strove to assault the Aquitanian.  
  
Section 3.6.  
  
The strenuous hero, however, drove them back with the point of his spear  
And terrified them as they rushed him, both with his glare and his weapons.  
Here King Gunther contemplated a foolish stratagem,  
Namely, to approach silently, then stealthily pick up the spear  
Which had been thrown in vain and which lay fallen on the earth.  
For the jostled-for thing lay right before the feet of the hero.

Saw him bending down, realized what he was doing,

Of death, the sheer exhaustion of battling, and the scorching heat of the sun.

And within his silent heart he suppressed these thoughts:  
"If Fortune does not change her course, these two

Assailed the king even more relentlessly with his drawn sword,  
And, having ripped away the king's shield from his right side,  
He dealt a mighty and wondrous blow, and from him  
He cut clean away the whole leg, from the back of the knee right up to the thigh.

The attendant turned deathly pale as his master fell.  
Then Alpharides, lifting his blood-stained blade a second time,  
Burned to inflict a final wound upon the fallen man.  
But Hagano, a brave hero, and unmindful of his own danger,  
Bowed his bronze-clad head and threw it in the path of the blow.  
The hero was unable to check his out-stretched hand,  
And the helmet, long-wrought and perfectly crafted,  
Received the assault and straightaway sent sparks flying high.  
Stunned by its hardness, the sword shattered.  
O grief! Then, as it snapped, parts of it gleamed in the air and in the grass.  
When the warrior saw the fragments of his ruined blade,  
He took it ill, and raged wildly with excessive fury.  
Then, losing all self-control, he took the hilt, now devoid of the iron's weight,  
And cast it away immediately, loathing the grim reminder,  
Though it excelled in both exceptional metal and art.  
And while he chanced to have his hand stretched out quite far,  
Hagano, overjoyed, lopped it off with a swift stroke.  
  
Section 3.8.  
  
Into the midst of the clash fell that brave right hand,  
Feared by many nations, peoples, and tyrants,  
Which before had shone with countless trophies.  
But the exceptional hero, knowing not how to yield even in adversity,  
Mighty in his sound mind to overcome the pains of the flesh,  
Did not despair, nor did his countenance fall.  
Rather, he thrust his wounded forearm into his shield,  
And, with his unharmed hand, he quickly snatched up his short-sword,  
Which, as we have mentioned, he had girded on his right side.  
Then he took severe vengeance upon his enemy on the spot.  
For, striking Hagano, he gouged out his right eye,  
And, slashing his temple and tearing back his lips alike,  
He dashed twice three molars out of his mouth.  
This business being done, the battle was brought to an end.  
Each man’s own injury and sickly panting persuaded them  
To lay down their arms. For who could depart from there unscathed,  
Where two great-hearted heroes, as equal in strength  
As in fervor of mind, had stood in the lightning flash of war!  
After the end arrived, their tokens marked each man.  
There lay King Gunther's foot, there lay Walter's hand,  
And also the quivering eye of Hagano.  
Thus did they divide the armlets of the Avars.  
The two sat down together, for the third one lay prostrate,  
And they wiped away the surging river of blood with flowers.  
Amidst this, Alpharides recalled the trembling maiden with a shout.  
She came and bound up each man's wounds.  
When these things were thus settled, the bridegroom commanded her:  
"Now mix the pure wine and offer it to Hagano first.  
He is a good champion, if only he keeps the laws of faith.  
Then present it to me, who have endured more than the rest.  
Last of all, I wish Gunther to drink, inasmuch as he was sluggish  
When he appeared amidst the arms of great-hearted men,  
And performed the work of Mars lukewarmly and without spirit."  
The daughter of Heriricus complied with all his words.  
But the Frank, although his breast was parching for the offered wine,  
Said, "Carry it first, maiden, to Alpharides, your bridegroom and lord,  
Since, I confess, he is stronger than I,  
And towers in arms not only over me, but over all men."  
  
Section 3.9.  
  
Here at last thorny Hagano and the Aquitanian himself,  
Unconquered in their minds, though weary throughout their whole bodies,  
Jested over their cups in a contest of mockery  
After the din of battle and various terrible blows.  
The Frank said: "From now on you will hunt stags, my friend,  
From whose hides you may enjoy an endless supply of gloves.  
But fill the right one, I advise, with soft down,  
So that you may deceive those ignorant of your plight with the appearance of a palm.  
Ha! But what say you to the fact that you seem to break the custom of your people?  
For you strap your sword onto your right thigh.  
And what about your wife? Whenever the desire for her steals upon you,  
Will you wrap your left arm around her in an awkward embrace? Try your best!  
Why do I delay? Behold, from now on, whatever you must do,  
Your left hand must do it." To him Walter returned these words:  
"I marvel that you boast so much, you one-eyed Sicambrian.  
If I hunt stags, you will have to avoid boar's meat.  
From now on, looking suspiciously askance, you will order your servants,  
Greeting crowds of heroes by staring at them sideways.  
But, mindful of our ancient faith, I will give you some advice.  
Whenever you go home and draw near your hearth now,  
Make a porridge of milk and meal mixed with lard!  
This will provide you with both sustenance and a cure."  
With these words they renewed once more their old alliance,  
And, lifting up the king together, who was groaning heavily,  
They placed him on his horse. And so, parting ways, they returned.  
The Franks went to Worms while the Aquitanian went to his homeland.  
There, welcomed with joy and great honor,  
He duly celebrated his public wedding to Hiltgund,  
And, beloved by all, after the death and passing of his father,  
He ruled his people happily for thirty years.  
What kind of wars he waged thereafter, or how many great triumphs  
He often won, behold, my blunted pen refuses to mark.  
Whoever reads this, forgive a chirping cricket.  
Do not judge its hoarse voice just yet, but its youth,  
Inasmuch as it does not yet seek the heights, having not yet left its nest.  
This is the poem of Walter. May Jesus save you.  
  
End of Book Three.
