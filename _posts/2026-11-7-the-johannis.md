The Johannis is a poem by Flavius Corippus, one of the most important poets of the Vandal Renaissance. It uses a style influenced by Vergil and Claudian to tell the story of the reconquest of Roman Africa by Emperor Justinian's General John Troglita.

Set around 535 ad, the poem details the battle preparations on both sides and dramatizes the twists and turns of war, with the Moorish enemies helped by demons and the Romans helped by God.

One thing I find notable about this epic is: it is the first "secular epic" I am aware of in which a role that is typically played by pagan deities is played by a Christian angel. Namely, in Section 1.6, an angel descends from heaven to assist John by banishing a demon that warned him not to enter Africa, and to tell him what to do next. In classical pagan epics, this would be done by some god or goddess, such as Juno or Mercury. Here, the gods and goddesses who perform cosmic interventions in pafan epics are replaced by Christian angels and demons.

A second thing I think is notable is: the classical gods and goddesses are not *entirely* absent. They are relegated to stylistic metaphors, or explicitly called out as demonic counterfeits of the true God. The Moorish god Ammon and the Roman god Jupiter are said to exist, but are demons, whereas gods like Mars and Phoebus appear several times, but only as poetic metaphors for warfare and sunlight, respectively.

A third thing I find notable is the ending. The poem ends very abruptly, in a way that reminds me of Vergil's Aeneid. The Aeneid ends in the middle of a climactic one-on-one battle, as Aeneas thrusts his sword through the breast of his opponent Turnus. The "denouement" in which he would have taken control of the city of Rome (now that its greatest warrior was defeated) is left unversed and implied. Similarly, in the final section of this poem, John hurls a spear through the breast of the enemy leader Carcasan, but the poet chose to zoom out *slightly* compared to Vergil. He declares that the Romans are routing the Moors, and ends on a final image of the remaining Moors hiding in trees as the Romans stab them from below, expressed through the metaphor of a hunter killing a bird that he has trapped in a tree using birdlime. It is *slightly* less of an abrupt ending compares to Vergil's Aeneid, but it still leaves the peaceful "denouement" implied.

This translation was created in several stages. First, I divided the poem up into about sections of roughly 40 lines apiece, which I call sections. Then, I fed each section into Google Search's AI (based on Gemini), and asked it to translate each section idiomatically. Then I combined all the sections, read through the text, modified a few lines for readability, and decided it was good enough to post. Below is the result, and I hope you enjoy it.

# Text

## Preface.   

Section 0.0.  
  
Conquerors, nobles, I have dared to speak of laurels:  
In a time of peace, I shall sing festive songs.  
It pleased me to write of great John throughout his wars,  
The deeds of the man to be read by future generations.  
Letters make all things known to the age-enduring world,  
As they recount all the battles of ancient leaders.  
Who would know of great Aeneas, who of fierce Achilles,  
Who of brave Hector, or the horses of Diomedes,  
Who of Palamedes' battle lines, or who would know Ulysses,  
If letters did not commemorate the works of old?  
The poet of Smyrna described brave Achilles,  
And learned Virgil described Aeneas in song:  
The work of John has taught me to describe battles  
And to relate all his deeds to men to come.  
John surpasses Aeneas, being better in virtue,  
But I do not sing songs worthy of Virgil.  
The greatest deeds of the leader thunder out because I am bold,  
As do the virtues of the man and his conquered wars.  
The fickle fortune of the poet wavers in a narrow strait:  
On one side is laurel favor, on the other, pale fear.  
A most rich sequence of events rouses me to song:  
I have grown warm at his deeds, though cold in talent.  
I do not sing of the excellent leader in learned verse,  
And numbness restrains my tongue within my mouth.  
What of the fact that I, an ignorant man who once spoke through the countryside,  
Now openly send songs through the peoples of the city?  
Perhaps a syllable will be misplaced in a broken verse—  
I confess it: for my Muse is rustic.  
Surely the glory of speaking praise must be allowed:  
Shall I alone be cheated of the reward by singing nothing?  
Terror driven from my breast rouses my voice even more:  
Let favor be in my mouth as praises are sent forth.  
The verses that learning denies, victory provides;  
Such great joys lift a spirit weary of songs.  
If Carthage rejoices thus in her many triumphs,  
Let there be due favor for me; let yours, I ask, be love.  
While my rustic Muse contends with the Roman Camenae (Muses),  
Fame carries our leader through the stars.  
If it pleases you that I recite the words of my first book,  
Then, deservedly obeying your commands, I sing my first songs.  
  
End of Preface.  

## Book One.   

Section 1.0.  
  
I sing of standards, leaders, wild nations, and the ruins of war,  
The ambushes, the slaughters of men, the harsh labors,  
The Libyan disasters, the enemies broken in strength,  
The famine proclaimed to the peoples, and the denial of water,  
Which threw both camps into deadly confusion with tumultuous chaos;  
I sing of peoples thrown into disorder, laid low, and subjugated,  
And of a leader marking his deeds with a great triumph:  
The Muses desire to resonate once more with the descendants of Aeneas.  
Peace, returned to Libya, stands firm as wars cease,  
And a more certain Victory shines with twin wings;  
Now Piety has looked down upon the lands from high heaven,  
While Justice and Concordia together accompany her,  
Joyfully nurturing and restoring the world, embracing it with twin arms.  
In the midst of these, sublime upon your high throne,  
O Justinian, rise up, prince, joyful in your triumphs,  
And as victor, give laws to broken tyrants:  
For your glorious footsteps trample upon all kings,  
And the joyful purple now serves the Roman Empire,  
The conquered enemy is prostrated beneath your feet,  
Fierce chains bind the nations, and thongs bind their hands  
Intertwined behind their backs with a tenacious knot;  
Savage necks are bowed down under the weight of chains.  
Even if my chest could pour forth songs through a hundred mouths,  
My understanding would not suffice, nor would all my talent  
Flying through the broad, remote ways of the world suffice for me as I sing.  
I shall sing of those things briefly: these matters are suited to the highest praise.  
Africa, worn out, was tottering under great danger.  
For wild madness had flared up with barbarian arms,  
Proud in ambushes, the sword, flames, and vast numbers of people,  
Burning all the walls of the plundered land  
And dragging away captive Africans from every part:  
Now there was no distinction—she could not spare any prophets,  
Nor grant worn-out old men any honor of a tomb. Every corpse then lay  
Pierced by swords. It was not permitted for any sons  
To place the slaughtered bodies of their fathers beneath the heaped-up earth,  
Nor was it allowed to pour out proper tears over their wounds:  
While the father is overwhelmed, the children and wife are dragged away,  
Wealth is plundered, the wicked violence of war oppresses,  
And dutiful funerals are abandoned to the deserted land.  
  
Section 1.1.  
  
The noble and the poor are swept away in a single downfall;  
Mourning resounds everywhere, terror and grim fear pass  
Through everyone, and all things are thrown into confusion by dreadful dangers.  
Who could unfold the tears, disasters, spoils, fires, deaths,  
Ambushes, groans, torment, chains, and abductions,  
Or who could count such wretched sorrows?  
Africa, a third part of the world, perishes smoking in flames.  
And now the pious prince [Justinian] turned these cares in his breast,  
Pondering and meditating on whom he wished to send to our shores  
As leader of leaders and supreme master of the army,  
Desiring to prop up so great a ruin.  
As he considered all things, John alone pleased him by his virtue  
And counsel; he appeared brave and wise.  
He alone was thought able to clash with the wild nations  
And, fierce as he was, to lay low the hostile troops.  
Indeed, the man's honor, the proofs of his glorious labor,  
And his conquered wars of a prouder kingdom [Persia] pleased the emperor:  
How he expelled the Persians, and with what a wound he laid low the Parthians  
Who trusted in their multitudes and in blocking the enemy with dense arrows;  
At that time when the wide fields of Nisibis flowed  
With the blood of Persians, and when Nabedes, second to the Parthian king,  
Having joined battle while relying on his own fierce courage,  
Lost his allied troops because John overcame them;  
And Nabedes, fleeing, driven by dread, was scarcely able  
To close his own gates, and in the middle of Nisibis' citadel  
The Roman cavalry broke through; and John, as victor,  
Struck the high gates of the Persians with his spear.  
All the brave deeds of the faithful man ran before the eyes  
Of the emperor. He weighed and turned over his labors:  
How a dense enemy had surrounded Theodosiopolis  
In a heavy siege: how he, coming swiftly through the shadows of night,  
Succored the walls of the wavering city  
And entered friendly gates through the midst of the enemy;  
And how the huge Mermeroes, terrified, retreated from those walls,  
And how, fiercer with crowded arms, he then dared to attack Daras—  
Which girded its steep fortifications with a heavenly wall—  
Where the leader [John] was ruling the standards, and tried to test the Latin phalanxes in war.  
  
Section 1.2.  
  
But after the vigilant leader rescued the first city from the enemy,  
Pursuing them as they fled from there,  
He occupied the roads ahead and protected all the fields,  
Lest the fierce enemy should plunder or harm anyone;  
And he was the first to hold the fortifications of the high wall,  
Nor did he allow any delay: for straightway, growing braver,  
He dared to clash with the enemy in the middle of the plains  
And overthrew countless troops with successful warfare,  
Along with excellent leaders and nations joined by treaty.  
He conquered and put to flight Mermeroes, the lord of the Parthians.  
Then the Persian, fearing the Roman who pursued him through the plains,  
Dropped his swords and shining trappings into the midst of the fields as he fled.  
The Median sword gleamed across all the sands,  
As did light scabbards, spears, shields, and horse-crests;  
The bodies and horses were mingled, and the weapons of the proud  
Armor-bearer of the leader lay scattered. He too would have lain  
Prostrated on the plains, if the magnanimous leader had not wished to take him alive.  
Conquered nonetheless, Mermeroes saw the steep fortifications  
And entered the walls with only a few companions.  
Then the wise Urbicius, standing in the middle of the fields, blessed the Lord—  
He whom the supreme imperial apex of the world then held  
As his chief servant and faithful minister, and had sent to those hostile shores  
To learn what the dangers of the savage war had been.  
When he saw the victorious Romans burning with zeal  
And the devastated enemies going trembling through the fields,  
Stretching forth his twin palms to heaven along with his eyes,  
He spoke thus in his joy: "Glory to You forever,  
Highest God, because I have at last deservily lived to see the Persians conquered  
Through the virtue of our John."  
The prince, frequently turning over these labors in his mind,  
Approved with timely piety that this man alone could defend oppressed Libya.  
Delaying no longer, he ordered the leader to be summoned  
From the furthest parts of the world. Calmly John left those borders and enemies  
To approach the western waters, and with swift speed  
He fulfilled the commands of the emperor within days; returning as victor,  
He soon touched the golden thresholds of the Roman gate.  
Rejoicing, he stood before the feet of the prince with a serene countenance.  
  
Section 1.3.  
  
The emperor looked upon his servant. Swiftly, John gave joyful kisses  
To his kindly feet. The emperor ordered him to relate the deeds  
Of the Eastern world in few words. Having been commanded, he poured  
Into peaceful ears the wars he had completed. Rejoicing in his foster-child,  
The imperial apex of the world wished for him to always conquer thus.  
Straightway, he sent him to defend Libya with aid.  
By the commands of the prince, ships were loaded with soldiers,  
As well as with provisions and arms; and the raw recruit, needing to be taught  
How to fight, was sent under the auspices of a master who would tame wars.  
Now, at the fixed time, a gentle wind driving the sails had made the waves ripe,  
And a favorable Thetis [the sea] invited navigation for the sailors.  
But the greatest prince, full of piety,  
Instructed the master, offering these words: "Our state  
Compensates well-deserved rewards for worthy labor  
With me as prince, and it pleases me to assist all men and make powerful in rank  
Whomever I perceive to run a good course for their lands and peoples.  
Now receive my words, gladly learn the causes, and hold them in your mind.  
Under what great dangers wretched Africa lies  
Has resounded in our ears. Piety compels me to succor her in her exhaustion.  
It has pleased our minds, bravest man,  
And it has seemed that you are able to protect Libya.  
Move the standards, swiftly board the lofty ships,  
And relieve the wretched Africans with your accustomed virtues;  
Lay low the rebellious battle-lines of the Levathae with arms,  
Compelling them, crushed by your virtue, to bend their subject necks beneath our feet.  
Maintain the ancient laws of our parents, relieve the weary, and shatter the rebels.  
This is the love of our piety: to spare the subjected;  
This is the honor of our virtue: to tame proud nations.  
Holding these commands of mine, most faithful leader, preserve them.  
Let Christ, our Lord and God, do the rest,  
Turning things for the better, and let Him guide you through all things  
With His prosperity. May we, in due form, see your honors increased  
With better titles according to your merits."  
John fell before his feet and pressed kisses upon his divine soles,  
Watering his footsteps and wetting them with tears.  
The prince, like a father seeing the leader depart,  
Felt deep grief, and piety shook his imperial mind.  
  
Section 1.4.  
  
Then seeking the fleet, the magnanimous leader encourages the cheering sailors.  
They launch the ships from the shore;  
First, the marble surface of the sea is swept by the striking oars;  
Quickly they loose the sails, they urge them on with loud shouts,  
And they loosen the heavy ropes with a harsh-sounding screech  
And expand the sheets. Now the breezes, driven by gentle gusts,  
Move the canvas: the deep hulls hide the sea,  
And the narrow waters are concealed beneath a hundred ships.  
Favorable winds grow stronger with ripe North-West currents  
And impel the vessels. The swift ships cut through the waters with bronze prows,  
The marble sea is furrowed by the rams,  
And the foaming wave murmurs beneath the long hulls.  
The fleet flies past the Thracian straits with their narrow shore,  
Where the sea divides Sestos from the fields of Abydos,  
And secure in the winds, it flies through the Sigean waves  
And skims the mournful shore of ancient Troy.  
Then they recall the glorious songs of the Smyrnaean poet [Homer]  
And point out from the high deck the places of the ancestors.  
"This was the seat of Priam, this the house of Aeneas, which lies far off,  
Enclosed by trees. Here fierce Achilles  
Had dragged Hector's corpse with his rushing chariot.  
On this shore the victor Aeneas overthrew the great Demoleos—  
Aeneas, the ancestor by whose authority the walls of lofty Rome  
And the illustrious name of her empire shine,  
Holding the wide world by the treaty of her rule."  
And they sing of all the deeds and battles of the Argive war:  
How Patroclus fell, laid low by Hector's spear,  
And how black Memnon was conquered by the wound of Achilles,  
How dutiful Aurora wept over the massive funeral of her son,  
How the warrior maiden Penthesilea fell in the midst of her troops,  
On what night Rhesus was slain,  
And how the boy Troilus, having clashed with brave Achilles,  
Fell where the victor was brought down by Apollo's arrow,  
And by what wound Paris the abductor lay dead.  
Afterward, they recount the final fires of exhausted Troy  
And the flight of Aeneas, how he then carried away in ships  
The boy, illustrious by the name of Iulus, and his father,  
His wife having been lost, running through so many azure shallows.  
The excellent Peter [John Troglita’s son] heard them recounting the battles.  
As he heard the illustrious name of the boy Iulus,  
His boyish breast burned with a new love of reading,  
Wishing to know of the wars. He is moved by a great affection:  
He thinks himself to be Ascanius; he thinks his mother is Creusa.  
  
Section 1.5.  
  
She was the daughter of a king; his mother also was the daughter of a king.  
Then it was father Aeneas, and now it is father John himself.  
Rejoicing, he ponders these things; joys thoroughly thrill his mind.  
These tales he would bring to his father, these to the servants, these to everyone  
Across the sail-flying sea—Peter, the sole joy of his great sire,  
And the second hope for Roman affairs.  
The secure fleet glides through the Aegean Sea,  
And no differently through the Adriatic waves, with favorable winds  
It cuts the deep more swiftly. Soon it reached the Sicilian shores;  
The breeze deserts the ships, and as the winds fall silent, the entire sea  
Lay still with unmoving waves. A gentler wave  
Shakes no shores. Then two-formed Scylla was silent,  
The barkings of her dogs were silent, nor did the stirring wave compel  
The mouths of her wolves to make the howling rocks echo.  
Although here the borders of the opposing lands come together  
And each shore is troubled by a narrow strait,  
Even the never-appeased Charybdis held back her motionless waves,  
Neither vomiting back nor swallowing down the liquid waters.  
The slack sails fall, and swelling with no gust of wind,  
They clung to their own masts. Then, ordering his companions  
To loosen the ropes, the leader says: "Enter the quiet harbors."  
Commanded, the swift sailors fly over all the rigging.  
Running here, one unties the sails; another gathers the sheets;  
Another encourages his cheering companions with sweet sounds and soothes them with a sharp song.  
The men give courage with their shouting; the voice itself assists  
Their labors, and provides both strength and joy to the sailors.  
Caucana lies near the Sicanian fields of Pachynus,  
Occupying a curved shoreline, whose harbors the anchor  
Of the Roman fleet then gripped with its hooked bite.  
And now Hesperus [the evening star] roughens the star-bearing waves of the sea,  
Bringing a foul night upon the lands with its darkness.  
Here the magnanimous leader, John, secure on his deck,  
Was taking his sleep, when by chance the careful master  
Of the ship himself perceived that gentle winds were rising.  
The young men run in haste through all the decks  
Preparing the rigging, and they loosen the ropes from the shore  
Without waiting for the leader's command. The sailors raise all  
The sails, spreading the canvases to the full blasts of wind.  
  
Section 1.6.  
  
Driven by the winds, the fleet was now holding the middle of the deep sea,  
And from the edge of the sky the dewy Dawn was rising,  
Bringing forth the day, when a grim phantom  
Stood before the general’s feet. It seemed to be a Moorish face,  
Kin to the shadows, terrible with its pitch-black color,  
And twisting its eyes with veiled, smoldering flames.  
Then it began to speak: "To what shores do you stretch your fleets?  
Do you really think you will cross into Libya?" Sending back his words to it,  
The general said: "You see our hulls crossing,  
And yet you ask me?" Then the grim phantom, threatening in countenance,  
Twisting its terrifying eyes with sulfurous monstrosities,  
Said: "You shall not cross." He realized in his mind that this was  
A wicked angel, cast down from high Olympus.  
Yet he himself did not fear the man's face,  
Which mimicked wild beasts. He pursued the fleeing ghost and strove to grasp it.  
But the phantom, scattering thick darkness ahead with its hand,  
Muddled the general's path with a shadow of foul gloom mixed with dust.  
But once again, calm in appearance, an elder descended from high Olympus,  
Wearing white vestments over his starry robes,  
And he stood before the eyes of John, who was looking for his weapons,  
And held back his hand, adding these words from his holy mouth:  
"Let no madness rouse your spirit to such great wrath.  
Scorn evil through your own goodness; flee the dread curses of the wicked one,  
And do not fear." To him the general said, "O best of fathers,  
And man of God, you see him fighting and striving to close  
The approaches to our pathways." The elder, then kind in spirit,  
Said this: "O blessed one, follow in our footsteps,  
And make your way with me as your guide." Having spoken thus, with a great light,  
He calmly raised the glowing fire of his torch.  
Then, indeed, every helmsman stood paralyzed, their minds frozen in doubt,  
And fleeing the storm, they turned their backs to the winds, confessing  
That the power of their skill was utterly defeated.  
The wretched men no longer knew which way to turn the ship’s prow,  
Nor could the reefed sails hold against the gusts.  
There was no use for any of them. The sailors lowered all  
The canvas, abandoning their vessels entirely to the mercy of the winds and waves.  
Scattered in all directions, they drifted across the vast sea,  
Carried wherever chance, the driving gale, and the blindness of the night  
Happened to drag them. Fortune threatened the miserable crews with savage  
Shipwreck. They believed no hope of safety was left,  
And in the face of such open peril, they despaired for their very lives.  
  
Section 1.7.  
  
The leader groaned and, sorrowful, lifted his mind to the stars,  
And with piety as his guide, as fear itself prompted,  
He sought the aid of God with welling tears;  
Prostrate and praying, he suppliantly began with these words:  
"Omnipotent father of the Word and creator of all things,  
Beginning without end, O God, all things confess You  
As author and lord; the elements tremble before their maker,  
The winds and clouds fear You, the air does military service for You,  
And by Your command the high ether now thunders  
And the great fabric of the shaken world is thrown into confusion.  
You know, highest Father, You know all things through Your foreknowledge:  
Not greedy for gold, nor by any reward of gain  
Am I driven to go to Libya, but to tear apart the war  
And to save wretched souls. This is my only desire,  
This is the whole love within my heart; to this place only the bounteous  
Command of the prince draws me. With You as sovereign, our prince  
Commands. He himself confesses that he owes You well-deserved  
Service in due order; You subject all of us to him  
And command us to serve; I have followed Your precepts.  
Look upon us favorably, Holy One, and behold our labors;  
Be calm now and dutifully succor us in so great a downfall.  
But if his own transgressions condemn the sinning John  
By Your judgment, destroy me by any other death,  
Only spare my Peter now." When this name was spoken,  
The sound caught in his mouth, the father's breast trembled,  
His legs and hands grew colder than ice and failed him,  
And all his limbs gave way. Then he poured out tears like a river  
And lifted massive groans up to the stars.  
The Lord received the tears and words of him who prayed thus,  
Accepting them: He commanded the powerful winds to grow gentle,  
And the storm fled away swiftly as the clouds thinned out into fleeces.  
The sun returned, and with a bright, gleaming torch in the sky,  
The nurturing day burst forth. The definitive command of God smoothed  
The marble waves of the sea: favorable winds arrived.  
The joyful sailors arose, throwing all things into motion  
With their voices, and stretched the canvases to the gentle winds,  
And the sheets were filled. Then from all sides they gathered  
The allied ships, and sails shone bright across the whole sea.  
  
Section 1.8.  
  
Now closer and closer they fly, and favorable breezes  
Impel the ships as they furrow the azure waters on their course.  
At last the leader looked out upon the shores of the burning land  
And recognized the untamed reins of war,  
And a portent about which there was no doubt—for real fires raged:  
The winds were rolling flames that curled at their peak,  
And flying ashes, mixed with smoke above the stars,  
Scattered thin sparks into the highest ether.  
The flame rose and now raged into the middle of the deep sea,  
Consuming all the wood of the burning land.  
The nourishing crop, ripe throughout the cultivated fields, was consumed,  
And every tree increased the fire that grew amid the foliage,  
And, burning away, dissolved itself into ashes.  
Wretched cities were overturned together with their slaughtered citizens,  
And all the fortifications blazed as the roofs were plundered.  
Not otherwise had Phaethon, with his poorly granted chariot,  
Set all things on fire from every part of the world,  
Carried away by his flame-vomiting horses, until with a supreme thunderbolt  
The omnipotent Father, pitying the lands, unyoked the panting  
Horses, quenching fire with fire.  
The leader burned to succor the wretched land,  
Groaning with more than his accustomed piety, and he moistened  
His cheeks with a copious flood of tears. His virtue blazed in its accustomed arms,  
And anger dictated that he direct his hasty steps  
Into the middle of the waves. Nature restrains him, though he wishes to,  
As does a restraint mixed with virtue, by which, ruling all things,  
Weighing illustrious matters alongside small ones, he governs what has been entrusted to him.  
Swiftly he commands them to turn the prows, brought close to the shores,  
And joyfully he approached the familiar sand.  
The sea does not wash the shores of Byzacium with equal waves,  
Since the land stands opposite. A part, gentler in its tide,  
Lies flat: a station is granted there for the curved ships,  
For the salty shallows create exceedingly quiet harbors.  
Here no force of the South Wind, when aroused, can lift the calm waves,  
Nor does the wind terrify the clear water.  
Another part of the sea is battered by waves, and as it glides back from the bank,  
The sea barks, broken against the rocks. The water, passable by way of the rocks,  
Resounds and is poured out over the black seaweed.  
  
Section 1.9.  
  
There, both the swollen Boreas [North Wind] and Eurus [East Wind], heavier with storms,  
Roll up the seas from their deepest bottom.  
Then wretched ships perish as their ropes are burst  
By the violence of the sea: the planks of the vessel often lie across harsh fields,  
Along with decaying prows scattered through the grass.  
For this reason, sailors fear and flee the dire dangers  
Of that place, and seek the safe, quiet shallows of the other part.  
On that shoreline the Roman fleets had stood  
At the time when Belisarius touched the Libyan shores,  
About to capture the Vandal wealth: which harbor, by name,  
From its contrasting side, the ancient sailors called Caput Vadorum [the Head of the Shallows].  
Arriving here, the magnanimous leader John likewise untied his sails  
With a similar virtue. Fortunate was that place,  
Where there was a safe and favorable station for the Latin fleets.  
Then the anchor, fixed by its bite,  
Held the secure ships on the shore.  
The most brave leader recognized those harbors,  
And rejoicing in the places, he pointed with his finger from the waves  
And spoke thus in joy to his companions: "When the first avenging ships  
Held these lands, I myself was the first on this shore  
To touch the soil, relying on my youthful arms:  
For I was one of the officers. When the treacherous tyrant  
Gelimer held the scepter on the Libyan shores,  
A Roman band pressed these sands with their very first step.  
Here it drank the Libyan waters. Along this border  
The arriving army then placed its trenches with its initial weapons.  
That hillock far off, closest to the neighboring waves—  
Do you see how it rises, heaped up with soft sand  
By the movement of the South Wind? There the leader Belisarius  
Placed his tents higher than all the standards,  
Around whom the officers and mixed tribunes positioned themselves.  
In this place I held camp, with my blessed brother likewise accompanying me.  
Alas, the most harsh lot of fate, enemy to human goods!  
How many joys of dutiful brothers does fierce death, coming suddenly, snatch away!  
With what raging virtue did that brother of mine overthrow the enemy,  
And with what a noble mind did he restrain his companions by ruling them,  
And how greatly did the state weep for such a man!  
The fortune of war did not snatch away my powerful brother,  
Since he had returned as victor so many times from the savage enemy.  
Alas, harsh death overcomes the dutiful! You were the image of a father to me,  
Pappus, and you were a son to me. Worthy solace for so great a misfortune  
Is it that, as a victor, you now look down upon the Stygian waves.  
  
Section 1.10.  
  
These places have made me mindful of my blessed brother  
And have moved my tears. What ancient wars  
Did that man complete! May God Himself grant me better outcomes.  
Let this place be fortunate, and more fortunate than in that year  
By the prosperity of God. Yet at this time, when these camps  
Remain unfinished during so great a crisis of war,  
From how many peoples has safety been stolen! If Victory  
Should favor my standards throughout the wars, I shall complete the begun  
Fortifications of the place, solidified with strong metal."  
Having spoken thus, he grieved that the cities lay deserted of citizens  
And the homes lay empty, and pitying the Libyan ruins,  
He groaned; he ordered the ropes to be loosed from the shore  
And yielded the canvases to the desired winds.  
After these moments, the third light of dawn brought the Tyrian [Carthaginian] walls  
To their eyes, and returned the leader to the weary city.  
Scarcely had he touched the shores, planting his first footsteps upon the soil,  
When he began ordering the battle-lines to go out into the plains;  
He instructs the officers to assemble the dense troops  
And commands them to bear the standards. Supreme grief incites his anger.  
Grieving indeed for the ruin of the land which he himself had witnessed,  
He moves the camp. Obeying his commands, the aroused youths  
Gleam, their numbness cast aside, and every soldier,  
Having taken up his sword again, seeks war. Then with curved bronze horn,  
The harsh songs of the hoarse-sounding trumpets kindle the bitter battle.  
And now the columns break forth from the nine wide gates,  
And all the walls pour out iron-clad troops.  
From this side come the cavalry, while the slow crowd of infantry moves  
Along a thousand paths, and the dry earth groans under the running of feet.  
Just as when that sovereign of a beautiful little plot of land,  
Moving his camp of bees, commands the dense swarms to go out  
And lead forth the column from the golden wax cells—  
Whether he prepares for battles, aroused by anger against an opposing leader,  
Or by chance hastens, wishing to keep wicked drones away—  
And the youths, welcoming his commands, in haste leave the hives  
Through every entrance with crowded exits and challenge the enemy with hoarse buzzings—  
Not otherwise does the soldier march out into the plains from all Carthage  
And rejoice to advance with upright standards.  
  
Section 1.11.  
  
From this side, a dense crop of soldiers bristles with bronze-clad companies:  
These men carry quivers and bows; on the broad shoulders of those men  
Shining armor resounds. Spears and shields flash bright,  
As do heavy breastplates and the crests standing upon their helmets.  
Then a fresh cloud of dust rolls up in a thick gloom,  
The trampling hoof beats the unyielding earth,  
And the smoke of the lifted sand envelops the air.  
The leader himself, encouraging the columns among the foremost,  
Is carried on his horse and kindles the officers in their accustomed armor,  
While he reminds them of old battles fought in the Persian lands.  
In no other way could he inflame the spirits of the men,  
If he did not praise the work of war. Just so did Jupiter,  
As the ancient poets say in pagan song,  
While savage Phlegra stood in the midst of the Giants' tumult,  
Warn the hosts of heaven what the fates intended:  
By what bolt of thunder he could lay low the earth-born creatures,  
With what spear-point Mars could pierce and scatter their limbs,  
Which ones Pallas would turn into mountains at the sight of the Gorgon,  
What dooms the Bow-holder [Apollo] would bring with his crowded arrows,  
And which ones swift Diana would pierce with her hurled weapon.  
And now, hastening through the extended fields, the army  
Pursued its march through Byzacium, where the Antonian Camp is—  
A place named after the ancestors. Here John soon  
Pitch-tented his camp, and the envoys of the tyrant arrived.  
The excellent leader commands these men to be called inside the tents  
And orders them to deliver the messages of the savage tyrant.  
Then, one to whom belonged eloquence in the Roman tongue,  
Maccus, having been ordered to speak, says: "The magnanimous leader  
Of the fierce Levathae nation, the hero Guenfeius, and Antalas,  
Command us to bring these words to you. Surely you, John,  
Whom the Massylian band knew in the time of the unjust Solomon [a previous Roman general],  
You who were once a general closest to our shores  
And a protector of the neighboring sands of the sea—  
Have you not heard that the Solomonic troops fell in equal numbers  
In heavy warfare? Or with how great a slaughter the Roman band’s corpses  
Filled the rivers? Or how many of your men lay destroyed on the plains?  
Or of the massive destruction of your own leader in those wars?  
Do you dare to bring violence against unconquered nations?  
Is it not known to you how great the Laguatan chief is in war, whom alone  
Ancient, everlasting fame sings of? He whose ancestors long ago  
Maximian thoroughly felt in battle—Maximian, who held the Roman  
Scepters throughout the world as prince of Latium. Do you, with your small army,  
You who are about to perish, dare even to look upon my troops?  
  
Section 1.12.  
  
Or will you be able to endure the hands, and will you look upon the faces  
Of my men on the plains, O Roman leader?  
Rather turn your step, or carry back your taken-up  
Standards, and beware of death. But if you think you can engage  
In battle with me, if it pleases you to go beneath the shadows  
And your final day calls you, why do you weary your standards?  
Send back words to me telling what your certain intention is,  
And I will come wherever you wish, nor will I delay the fates.  
These messages that most brave leader gave.  
Send back whatever answers please your mind."  
Then, calm in his gravity and unmoved against the enemy,  
The leader said: "There is no need for me now to return answers  
To a savage enemy. On fixed days these messages  
Of the savage tyrant must be brought to maturity by me.  
Afterward I will give you my commands." Having spoken thus from his mouth,  
He ordered them to be held under private guard,  
Preparing brave deeds. Who could have hoped that safety  
Remained for those men? How great is the patience of the great  
Leader, how great his piety and his virtue in governing!  
Barbarian hearts swell, kindled by insane madness;  
He acts mercifully, guiding with Latin gravity.  
He did not wish to avenge himself with the immediate death of the proud,  
But wished to save the humble and lift up the broken.  
Thus Roman virtue remains and always will remain:  
It chastises and saves, and promises pardon after anger.  
When Lucifer [the morning star] brought forth his gleaming fires with his rays,  
Carried up from the waters of the Ocean, then the leader commanded all  
The camps to move and the dense phalanxes to go out.  
Sounding the signals, the wild trumpet groans its horrendous songs  
From its hollow bronze, and expels sweet sleep from the breast.  
The allies give courage—a shout mixes through the tents—  
And all encourage one another. The servants then tear away the pitched  
Canvases from the ground, lead the wing-footed horses from the high stalls  
Adorned with trappings, and take up all their weapons again.  
  
Section 1.13.  
  
But when he began to advance with the troops assembled in order  
And to show the victorious eagles and battle-lines on the plains,  
Thus properly dispensing the cares of his anxious breast,  
He himself warns the leaders, recounting and teaching the causes:  
"O Roman band, O most faithful hope of our affairs,  
O virtue and glory of the world, and supreme solace,  
The faith of the empire and the palm of our labor!  
Although you possess a certain confidence that belongs to our race,  
Yet I myself, mindful of ambushes, frauds, and trickeries,  
Am singing of things to be feared and unfolding what must be done.  
Harsh battles have never been free from wicked deceits:  
The Moorish battle-line has always waged wars through ambushes,  
And trusting in hidden weapons, it has couched in wait;  
Fallacy alone preserves the Massylian strength  
And makes the fearful fight, while rocks provide hiding places  
On the highest mountains, or rivers with high banks,  
Or where the gray olive spreads out its grove,  
Or the strength of a tall tree with its leafy crown  
Enables their battle-lines to crouch in wait on hidden plains.  
With these frauds the Moor strives to join battle,  
So that, coming swiftly upon an unsuspecting enemy, he may terrify them  
And oppress them while wavering, relying on both his numbers and the location,  
And trusting in his tamed horses. Then, a lone warrior is sent by craft  
Into the level plains to provoke battles  
And, fleeing, to draw out the enemies when they are sighted.  
He, in light course, brandishes javelins with prefixed iron tips,  
And does not cease to turn his tamed steed in a curved circle.  
But when the enemy has hastened forward, he flees by artifice,  
So that he may skillfully scatter his neat wings,  
While a numerous band follows, and thinks itself to be victorious,  
And the column diffuses itself across the whole countryside.  
With these ambushes the unmartial Moor acts, feigning combats,  
Until he leads the hostile column, enclosed in besieged valleys,  
Right into the midst of the enemy.  
Then that open deceit lets loose its savage reins  
And summons the hidden troops from every side.  
He who is driven by sharp fear flees at the first tumult.  
Elated, the Moor oppresses him under a savage wound:  
Fear itself makes the Moor fearless. But if by chance the men  
Stand firm with steadfast strength, no band will follow  
Those who dare to fight, but turning, they bend back the soft  
Necks of their four-footed beasts: thus they abandon the wars.  
  
Section 1.14.  
  
Thus the fleeing battle-line falls, thus the steadfast one will resist.  
For fortune will oppress the fearful and will assist the cautious  
And the daring alike: for she often revisits many men,  
And how many have taken the palm of victory from dangers themselves!  
As it becomes leaders to be, cautious, brave, and fierce,  
Display, each of you, your strengths in the crisis of war.  
Let that labor be in your minds. Arrange the battle-lines by troops  
And let all the standards advance with the companies set in order.  
Let this be especially the precaution of your highest labor,  
Maintain these vigilant duties: by these you conquer the enemies.  
Let each tribune precede the camp in his own order,  
And from time to time let the leaders search the suspected valleys  
And provide safe paths. The entire army  
Will be secure by these means: nor does the enemy catch the cautious man unawares,  
When he is fortified by his own men. But if the Moorish battle-line,  
Accustomed to hidden frauds, prepares for war,  
Let a light messenger, carried on a horse, bring word to our ears beforehand  
And summon the cautious cohorts to hasten forward.  
Observe this, leaders, and hope for true safety."  
The leader had spoken these words, when the broad column follows him  
With auspicious shouts: they praise, they applaud, they show favor,  
And they exult in their minds and joyfully execute his commands.  
  
End of Book One.  

## Book Two.  

Section 2.0.  
  
Meanwhile, the Moorish devastator is driven from all shores  
And, trembling, is trampled by the oncoming enemy.  
Terrified and fleeing, he deserts the besieged cities  
And, troubled by fear, runs together to the high mountains.  
Then he fortified his dire huts and placed them in the woods.  
Both the hollow valleys and sloping hills are filled  
with innumerable peoples,  
And they cover the plains and bending rivers far and wide.  
The bordering land lay hidden beneath the dense  
columns,  
And the mountains and leafy roofs in the high woods  
lie hidden under the hanging hut. From there, every kind  
Of wild beast fled, revering the hunting spears of the fierce Moor,  
Nor could the wretched creatures escape such great plagues,  
But lie with weapons pierced in their upturned breasts.  
No gentle bird was permitted on the high branches  
Or in the midst of the foliage to suspend their sweet nests.  
On all the trees, they tie reeds into a piled roof,  
And there is no peak now vacant on the mountain:  
Just as when the earth, covered by poured-out frost,  
Lies hidden under a cloud, and the plains and mountains, trees, and everything  
Grow white, and the very air is compressed by its own movements;  
The most certain face of things is snatched away by frequent  
Rains, and the stars are recognized by no signs.  
What poet could set in order for me so many peoples, nations, and battles  
With a new art? You, Justinian, by favoring,  
teach all things:  
Mix the Muse with charming sweetness.  
May she temper the songs which falter with unaccustomed words,  
Names of a barbarian tongue barking so fiercely.  
Antalas, prince of the Moors, once subject to Roman  
Princes, pleasing to leaders and faithful to masters, first enters the war,  
Driven by his brother's death: and moving his harsh right hand into battles,  
He raised it, roaring furiously, whereby, turbulent, he impelled  
All the bronze-clad nations toward the rewards of war.  
In the Libyan borders, having undertaken peace,  
He was faithful and had completed ten full years.  
Alas, what a war the indiscretion of an ignorant leader  
Moved, and made the extinguished flames grow warm again!  
Then the fury of ambivalent Mars planted its seedlings.  
The crime of perfidy or the seeds of so great a death  
Anger gave. It moved the bitter peoples of Libya  
Nore sharply, and disturbed the whole world with slaughters.  
  
Section 2.1.  
  
Now the Frexes follow, thickest in number with their kindred squadrons,  
And praise the ruler with a proud neck,  
A brave people, harsh in their men, and savage in the tumult  
Of war, whether they go on foot through the plains, daringly through the enemy,  
Or strike the flanks of a roaring horse with spurs.  
From there, the light squadrons of cavalry, with Sidifan accompanying,  
Bear arms. Fierce, he flies through the middle of the column,  
Trusting in his horse, and, sharp, he moves and strikes the armed phalanxes,  
And, untamed, runs about into the wide fields.  
From here, the cohorts of the Sinusdisae fly through the wars,  
And the grim Siluacae, and Naffur in savage arms,  
And fierce Silcadenit, who in the high woods prepare wars.  
They are to be feared for their deceits in out-of-the-way places, terrifying the enemy  
By the deception of their location, and, raging,  
They are carried forward in blind daring.  
Those who inhabit the mountains of Gurubus and the spiteful valleys,  
The hills of Mercury, and Ifera with its dense woods;  
With whom the heavy Autiliten, no softer than his father's daring deeds,  
Accompanied as commander and companion in war, and, faithful to no one,  
He loosened the huge reins of crimes along the way,  
Burning and plundering, fiercely slaughtering, and dragging away;  
The Siluaizan and the wandering Macares, who on high mountains,  
Steep and with dense woods, build their dreadful huts,  
Secure in the shadow of an opposing cliff;  
And those whom the Vadara weaves into its fountains with river waves,  
The light Silzactae and Caunes, where, from the height of the mountain,  
The river courses through the grasses with curving banks,  
The flowing Vadara [River], which pours out into the flat fields;  
The nations whom Agalumnus [the mountain] nourishes came to the battles,  
Which is seen to lift its lofty peak in the midst of the clouds,  
And Macubius, sustaining the stars of the wide sky;  
And those whom the most wretched [land] nurtures with heavy stalks,  
Grazing the barley of the thorny earth;  
Sascar also, summoned, assembles from distant shores,  
The Astrices, the [corrupted text], and the Imaclas.  
The dreadful Zersilis sends in those whom it nourished in narrow plains,  
And soon the polluted Gallica [sends] sorrowful men;  
Nor do the ill-fated fields cease to send peoples,  
Whom the nurse took up from the Talantean fields,  
And Tillibaris, and Marta, the mother of evil, who stretches out  
Her sands joined to the sea. The Roman hand would have avoided  
Seeing those sorrowful fields, if the fates had permitted it.  
The Fates, often envious of good things, snap the lethal threads.  
So it pleased you, Highest Father, whose judgments are performed.  
  
Section 2.2.  
  
A messenger, carried across into the furthest shores of Libya,  
Summons the untamed nations to foreign battles.  
The Laguatan chief, never conquered, assembles with innumerable  
Thousands, and flying about, terrifies the whole world.  
The fierce Austur follows him, trusting in the strength  
Of his horn-footed steeds with loosened reins, and brave in arms  
And in a number surpassing measure. For the warlike Austur,  
Anxious to commit to doubtful battles on the plains,  
Arranges walls and ditches with bound camels  
And places his varied livestock as a dense walling ring,  
So that he may entangle the fighting enemy with obstacles  
And press them hard when they are hesitant. Running then, the fierce Laguatan chief  
Slaughters, laying low the columns within the narrow walls,  
And safely approaches the fields, and follows and presses hard  
With fresh slaughter, and as a devastator rages against the wretched column.  
The ram is a machine of unspeakable war to them,  
And they pitch their adorned tents with arranged standards,  
A dreadful race and harsh in its men, and bold with triumphs  
Innumerable, which at no time ceases from wars,  
Impious, cruel: he fears no ruins,  
But it was permitted to have feared [corrupted text]:  
May it repent them to have raged for a long time. For the brave Laguatan chief,  
Once laid low by a wound through the wide fields,  
[corrupted text], and spared spoils and wars.  
Lerna was their fierce leader, and the priest of Gurzil.  
To this god the nations report that horn-bearing Ammon is his father,  
And a grim heifer his mother: so great is the madness in their blind  
Minds! Ah, how the deities deceive the wretched nations!  
The Ifurac came, skilled in hostile arms:  
Here as a foot soldier he leaps forward, distinguished with shield and spears,  
And powerful with his blade, while he aims at the fierce enemy.  
The Muctunian band descended from hot shores,  
Which inhabits the deserted parts of Tripolis; and malignant [city] Gadabis  
Sends men from its citadel, and bristling with savage walls,  
The wicked vanguard of Digdiga gave a force to the neighboring banks.  
  
Section 2.3.  
  
Then the peoples assemble who sweep the waters with Velanidean boats,  
Who skim across the sea with skill,  
Throwing curved hooks for trembling fish.  
The Barcaeans, wont to rage with their usual fury,  
Have deserted their own lands and begin to seek ours.  
To these, fury supplies arms and war,  
And a breed of fierce warriors, with menacing shields and swords,  
Not fastened to their sides in the usual way, but a ring goes around  
To encircle their arms, embracing them in a modest disc,  
And they fit scabbards to hang from it on their bare upper arms.  
Nor do the Moors adorn their arms with the sleeves of a tunic;  
Belts, inset with no studs, are fastened;  
And, ungirded, they drive their fierce squadrons into battle,  
And they carry two spears with very strong iron.  
A rough cloak, hanging down over their bound limbs,  
Lies lowered from their shoulders; then a linen  
Robe binds the grim head, supported by a tenacious knot,  
And raw Moorish rawhide is trodden under a black sole.  
Africa has endured so many Marmaridan nations in wars.  
Who would think the wretched land could survive? That is not enough,  
O heavenly ones! Now bold from the opposite shores,  
From the fronting side, that leader rises, roaring of the ruins  
Which the Roman hand previously gave to him through the wounds of war:  
From there the fierce man now gathers such great anger.  
Innumerable nations accompany him as he goes:  
Those who inhabit the Rock of Gemini, the rough country of Zerquilis,  
And the unspeakable mountains of sad Nauusus,  
And the deserted places which the dreadful land  
Of unspeakable Arzugis nurtures—so the ancients call it by name—  
The Aurasitan band descended from the lofty shores.  
That vanguard cannot come into battle on foot,  
But the horseman fights very powerfully. Then a double lance  
Fastens strong juniper wood with sharp iron,  
And a rough, light shield often lies on the hard back  
Or falls suspended at the side, but the flashing  
Blade itself, bound tight, hangs down from the left shoulder.  
And the plowing Moor, who cuts harvests twice a year at warm Vada,  
And grazes barley with a double stalk,  
Alas, rages in the dry sand of glowing Phoebus.  
How great is the love of plunder! The burning ardor is endured,  
And the hunger of the lands, the heat, and thirst are suffered  
For the love of fierce war and the desire for shameful profit.  
  
Section 2.4.  
  
Now the brave army, hastening, saw that the dense enemies  
Were on the highest mountains and on the hills,  
And that the places smoking with flames and the hidden woods,  
With a thick column, showed no mountain peak.  
All the roofs lie hidden. A savage voice from all sides  
Sounds indistinguishably through the breezes. You would think the groves  
And sounding woods were moved by an approaching excessive storm,  
Breaking the waves of the sea clashed against the shore.  
From here the shout of young men, and from there the roaring of horses  
Grows fiery, raging. Mothers shake the heavens with trembling  
Wails. Then the earth boils with a fearful tumult.  
Behold, a fleeing column of shepherds comes from the valleys  
At the arrival of the enemy. The bleating of flocks through the meadows  
Sounds in harmony, and the dust rises up with thick sand.  
Already the courser was running through the plains with loose reins,  
And was the first to snatch plunder from the driven herds.  
Behold, an enemy comes to meet them from the savage gorges,  
Scattered, and he is seen to advance, arranged in no squadron,  
Nor does he fervently address the foes facing him,  
But only rages with a shout. Then, driven from the woods,  
The vanguard seeks its cloaks with frequent movements  
And calls the crowds for help in the manner of the Moors.  
Therefore the Frexes fly together swiftly to the first contests  
In light warfare, and, fleeing, only protect the herds:  
For a steady battle line is composed with no weapons.  
Now, however, the war is begun with shed blood.  
For that powerful leader Geisirith had gone before the column  
By the commander’s order, and wise Amantius together with him,  
To test the hostile places, to search through the valleys,  
And to clear the ways in the usual manner. Now on a high mound  
The Roman battle lines halt, and they await the unspeakable nations  
With their eyes, taking in with anxious minds the sights  
Which they intend to report to the great commander.  
They are unable to tolerate the tumults,  
Nor to survey the dense places. Who could perceive  
So many thousands of men? Just as when the locusts fall down in the starlight,  
Spreading through the Libyan fields while the South wind blows  
At the very end of spring, or when the South-Southwest wind from the high sky  
Orders it to go headlong into the sea, snatched by a great whirlwind.  
The hearts of the hesitant farmers bristle with dread,  
Lest the dreadful plague destroy the crops,  
Or lay waste to the tender fruits and green gardens,  
Or harm the flowering olive tree on its soft branches.  
  
Section 2.5.  
  
And now drawing back their step, the Roman youths went,  
Bearing responses to the commander. The nearest enemy surrounds them  
With his characteristic swiftness. Now the harsh columns were coming up.  
The dense enemies assemble from the mountains,  
From all the gorges they pour out into all the plains—  
The Austur and the Munctunian band, which is carried along blazing  
In untamed years. The brave Laguatan chief follows.  
And now, sent across toward the hostile battle lines, the crowd  
Was running with innumerable horses, and disturbs and presses hard,  
And more and more it is packed into a dense column  
And overcomes the men. The plain itself is choked by spears,  
And the earth is shaken by a huge tumult.  
Just as when the winds drive the running clouds  
With a strong murmur, til the rain-cloud blazes with frequent fires,  
And the storm coming on the fronting face  
Flows in with a vast weight of driven drops  
Of unspeakable hail: and, subdued by rains and winds,  
Lowering his sorrowful face, the already cold traveler  
Chatters with his teeth, and, thus defeated, turns his back,  
And runs to the woods and roofs, seeking safe places.  
Not otherwise the Roman horseman was already departing, defeated,  
With the heavy enemy pressing hard. Although their glorious valor rising up  
Might seek brave deeds, yet the excessive enemy, assembling,  
Overwhelms them. Neither can the men scatter their weapons  
Nor their slow darts with their hands. The sword alone scarcely suffices  
To drive back the men in war; shields scarcely suffice to stand  
Against wounds. Then a sorrowful shout strikes  
The heavens; the opposing shields of the men groan back  
As weapons are received. Life is shaken by unspeakable dangers.  
Then the Roman battle line endeavors to withdraw gradually from the battle  
And struggles to gain the fronting hills.  
Meanwhile, a swift messenger shook the calm ears,  
Disturbing the camp with a huge tumult,  
Reporting that the fierce, innumerable enemies  
Had descended from the woods and mountains,  
That the unspeakable battle lines had filled the plains,  
And the places were beset by peoples and flashing arms,  
And already the leaders were succumbing in so great a fight.  
  
Section 2.6.  
  
Straightaway every horseman runs down into the open plain  
For the love of their fellow citizens, and burning passion and fear  
For their commander was urging them all. He drives the backs of his men,  
Inspiring respect with his voice as he urges them: meanwhile, with his spear, he also strikes  
Those whom he sees loitering. He commands the peoples to depart from the camp  
And dictates that they must run to help their comrades.  
And when he led out the light wings onto the open plains  
And went before the leaders, he rebukes those lingering through the camp  
And terrifies them with the sound of the harsh bronze.  
The dreadful trumpet, frequently summoning, groans with a trembling song.  
The battle line follows closely, assembled in crowded companies.  
The hoof saddens the starry plains with dust,  
And the beauty of the sun grows pale and it draws in  
Its flashing rays, and the sky is disturbed by the confronting sand.  
Just as when the heavy East winds rise headlong from the Aeolian  
Mountains and rage, and the storms sweep  
The sandy shore, then disturb the air with sorrowful  
Blasts, tearing the clouds with opposing winds:  
Thus the Roman battle line with dense crowds disturbs  
The plains, and the dust quickly overwhelms the distant enemies.  
The cautious Melangus saw them coming from the fronting cliff,  
And disturbing his cohorts with a huge shout,  
He gave the barbarian signals. Gradually they begin to leave  
The plains and hide their forces safely throughout the camp.  
But the brave commander, arriving and surrounded by his army,  
Perceives the enemies standing on the highest mountains,  
And, rejoicing, he takes up his unharmed, exulting men.  
From here, that band which had waged fierce and sorrowful wars  
With savage dangers, reports on the spiteful gorges.  
He himself commands the men to place ditches and a camp  
Not far from the enemy facing them, but exceeding close.  
Swiftly they execute the commands, and through the level plain  
They soon pitch white tents with snowy sails.  
Here the most brave Roman band, having pitched camp,  
Runs about at varied work. A part stores their high arms  
In places, weighs their quivers, and unstrings their bows,  
And they support the lofty sails on high poles.  
  
Section 2.7.  
  
In order they fix their spears in the soft meadows  
And lean their shields in the accustomed way against the spears throughout the fields,  
And they fit their heavy breastplates and helmets upon skins  
Placed underneath, and they store away sling-pellets and other weapons.  
Another part eagerly gathers the deserving swift-footed horses,  
Bringing them reliable fodder. That man flies, skilled in preparing  
Feasts, and placing bronze cauldrons down, he feeds the flames.  
This man draws cold spring water from a well, and through the grasses  
That one prepares curved couches, and sets down the dishes,  
Fitting everything in its place for the feasts and cleansing them with flowing water.  
Meanwhile, the great commander was revolving huge cares beneath his breast,  
Thinking diverse thoughts, concerning both the nations and the locations,  
And at the same time the captives. War had mixed them in with the African  
Peoples and had subdued them, wretched, as plunder was being snatched.  
He boils with anxiety and turns things over in his mind again and again,  
Hesitant as to what he should do. Already now his supreme valor  
Prepares to commit to battles. Yet piety dictates to spare them,  
Indeed, lest he shatter the captives with any lack of discrimination,  
On whose behalf he moves his arms, his vigilance wanders,  
Flying between both options. His mind decides and then objects.  
Within his breast a battle rages: piety runs to meet anger.  
And just as a farmer, seeing the fields bear crops  
Intermixed with briars, takes care with a grieving breast  
Diligently to tear out the unlucky briars from the fields,  
But, as Ceres herself moves him, he trembles and weighs the loss.  
For the ill-rooted thorns may destroy the barley,  
Or the dour weeds may not properly perish apart from the crops:  
Thus the father was weighing alternating cares in his breast,  
Testing all things in a balance, to see by what weight his mind might incline  
Or what it should perform. Neither does it please him to stand still, nor is conversation sweet to him  
While seated: he redoubles his sighs with a silent tongue.  
While he is driven by cares, while his suspended breast pants,  
He rises and prefers to walk about in the midst of the camp,  
And he goes and returns along the paths, fatiguing his mind and body.  
Burning with equal cares, there accompanies him as he goes  
The magnanimous honor of a leader, exceedingly benign,  
And the glory of counsel. This is Ricinarius, who clings to his side.  
Him he has as a faithful companion in savage dangers,  
And as a relief, a man of both valor and a discerning mind.  
With Mars, he too conquered in supreme triumphs,  
And he rejoiced to have endured so many excessive labors  
Of war together with him, and to have come equally to the highest crown.  
And at that time, therefore, as a companion not unequal to his panting cares,  
He went along and calmly lightened them with alternating speech.  
  
Section 2.8.  
  
"My judgment wavers on every side in these narrow dangers,"  
The leader says, "and my breast pants with cares,  
While it turns over what hope of deliverance there might be in these doubtful matters.  
A victory that brings the greatest losses of property is no victory.  
If those things perish on whose behalf I came to battle,  
What will be the profit of the victor? Thus all things weary me,  
Intermixed with evils. From one side they threaten sad loss,  
From the other they wish to delay praise, and they snatch away won triumphs  
From our hands because of the demands of piety.  
The captive Africans will perish together with the tribes,  
If we decide to fight. What is the glory of our deed,  
If we betray the wretched? No enemy is truly conquered then.  
Counsel me how I should move in the crisis of this harsh war,  
And report what must be done." Calmly the hero Ricinarius  
Uttered these things and spoke with a measured, level voice:  
"Managing all things with a just moderation  
Is the highest virtue; this alone pleases, this alone can conquer the nations  
And pacify them. It is better, greatest leader,  
That you conquer your untamed valor with holy piety.  
Whatever you perform next, the victory belongs to our party.  
Such is the order of things, so all things teach. For now it is necessary  
To send legates to the proud tyrant with the promise of peace.  
Let them recall the captives. Let that raging Austur  
Depart from our shores, or that of the savage tribe, the Laguatans.  
Let them, forced to bear the yoke of the prince in the accustomed way,  
Carry it with a subjected neck. If they yield,  
All things will become safe. Guiltless, you will pardon the tribes,  
And victory will be calmly given to our affairs.  
But if by chance the swollen rebels stand with a proud neck,  
Then they will have to be overcome by arms. Nor is it ever a matter of regret  
To have acted for the wretched: if they fall in war, John  
Will bear no sins. That uninjured piety will remain  
Certain in your mind." The just counsels of the faithful man  
Pleased the leader, and the father, released from his cares,  
Is now at leisure: yet his breast is broken by alternating anxiety.  
Maturely he acts on the counsel; he then orders a swift armor-bearer  
To carry commands to the arrogant tyrant,  
And instructs him with this speech: "Carry my words to the rebel.  
  
Section 2.9.  
  
With these warnings of ours, batter his proud ears.  
While the nations are in error, Rome extends her piety,  
And forgives all offenses, wiping the slate clean.  
She does not hasten to destroy any nation in battle,  
If they repent of their warfare, and seek to be federated  
As subjects, and ask for pardon and peace for themselves.  
For the prince, acting with clemency, prefers all things to be his  
In such a way that he may save, hold, and rule everyone,  
Relieving the submissive and breaking the proud with his valor.  
Let not a restless terror now hold your breast  
Bound by your sins. For all things are beneficial  
If you return, and we willingly grant you pardon and peace  
Of our own accord. So many peoples will learn what Roman power is,  
And what the piety and valor of ruling are.  
What fortune holds you in misery? Were you not always our faithful servant,  
Accustomed to rejoice in our triumphs,  
And then be properly submissive? What evil fates draw you, proud man,  
Into fierce wars? At last, leave behind the sorrowful deeds  
Which, turbulent, you practice in these wretched lands.  
What does it profit you, unhappy man, to challenge Roman standards?  
Or what do you prepare, to cast down wretched men with such great ruin?  
Do you truly think that any nations at any time in history  
Could have conquered Roman hands? We tame the Parthian realms,  
We subdue the Lazi, the Ulmi, the Franks, and the Getas;  
And whatever fierce nations are scattered across the wide world beneath the heavenly  
Axis serve in our courts,  
And under a pious prince they carry out commands, glad to have abandoned war,  
And they bend their necks to a sweet yoke.  
Receive these words quickly, saving both the nations and your own people.  
We do not send these commands of our safety as though we were unwarlike,  
Nor do we flee battles and beg for peace.  
It is piety which sustains the world, which fears for the captives and for you,  
Looking out for you wretched men and your safety.  
This piety, great to me, orders me to spare a camp doomed to perish:  
On account of the captives, I will deign to grant pardon.  
But if, harsh man, you dare to oppose my words,  
Prepare for tomorrow's wars: girding your ditches with a fortification,  
Pour out your accustomed livestock, construct walls ready to be seized,  
Arrange the bleating flocks and pregnant goats  
And the shouting billies with grim faces in the midst.  
  
Section 2.10.  
  
There is no need to shatter hollow towers with a brought-up battering ram.  
Rather, by driving it away, we will strip the enemy bare,  
And every ram will plunder among your sheep,  
And we will set out our luncheons upon the well-demolished walls.  
That sculpted idol Gurzil, by whose protection they chant  
That prosperity must be given to your camp, shall be split apart  
As his wood is scattered, and he will be thrown openly into the blazing fires.  
When the nations have been routed, he will be sought on that plain  
And on all the mountains. Whatever crime Moorish  
Impiety has committed, our hand will then expiate in a just war,  
Laying low warm carcasses on the plain,  
With their heads snatched away. You will learn what our battles can achieve  
Far more, while the sword avenges the wretched Africans  
And the doomed columns fall in the scattered ditches."  
He had scarcely spoken these words: the messenger was treading the mountain paths.  
Reaching the camp opposite them, he passes into the middle of the tents,  
Unhesitating, and seeks from there the seat of the fierce tyrant.  
Damp night restores the shining stars to the sky  
And the wandering stars to the pole. For Cynthia [the moon], with her horn  
Now empty, and offering no lights to the dark earth,  
Had been submerged in the sea: she had thinned the fires of the month.  
But the camps of the leaders did not feel the darkness of the night:  
Dense lights shine out bright from hearths across the fields,  
And through the high mountains the bordering land glows  
With fires, and the dense woods radiate into the clouds.  
Who could distinguish the heaven from the earth on that  
Night, discerning which had been a shining star, and which a flame?  
Everything was light. From one side the earth gleams with flames,  
From the other the sky with stars. As often as a spark ran,  
Flying with fire, it was thought to be a falling star in the night.  
Whoever from the opposite fields saw the tents  
Unaware, trembled, believing that new stars  
Were unusually rising in the skies. The sailor wandered on the glassy  
Plains, and in no way was he able to recognize the signs then,  
And turning back his stern, he refused to hold his course.  
Armed, the soldier and the Moor were dragging out a sleepless,  
Watchful night. Running about on all sides, the soldier circuits  
The ditches, and with an astonished ear explores every sound,  
Guarding against everything, lest anyone should attempt ambushes in the custom  
Of the night, and bring battles into a hesitant camp.  
  
Section 2.11.  
  
They run about and guard the tents in their turns,  
And in turns they enjoy calm sleep within their iron-clad breasts.  
Bending down their necks, they rest them upon their chests.  
These fit shields to their necks, others fit quivers  
Placed underneath, and they hold spears and bows. Sitting,  
Leaning on their swords, they bend their faces down onto their chests.  
Somnus [Sleep] scarcely touches their eyes with his upturned horn,  
Fleeing from them: again and again they let their heads droop further and further,  
Nor have they the strength to raise them up, and they close and twitch their eyes,  
And as they fall forward, the crowns of their heads are wobbling on their anxious breasts.  
While they watch, take their turns, and labor in their rounds,  
The rest of the youths throughout the secure camp already enjoy  
Sleep with a calm breast. Only the wicked deeds of war  
Urge their minds: dreams disturb their anxious souls  
And show diverse shapes in the night.  
As soon as a soldier relaxes his limbs in tender slumber,  
Somnus quickly rushes in on wet wings with fresh dew,  
To blow upon his breast from a poured-out horn;  
Then the soldier departs, and seizes the enemy camp from high mountains.  
In body he sleeps inert, yet in mind he labors,  
Waging wars, and pulling his enemy down, he conquers or wounds him,  
Or cautiously avoids coming wounds with a shield  
Placed in front. Now he is conflicted in mind and limb;  
Now he stirs both at once. He seems to himself to be fighting  
In the woods, for his languid limbs are empty in sleep.  
Often their eyes see savage war clash,  
Their closed eyes see the contest. He goes bristling with anger,  
And wishes to move his hands: the tired limbs of the man languish in slumber.  
How many times the errant right hand, having flourished its sword,  
Strikes a comrade's breast because he thinks he is striking an opponent!  
And the men laughed, recounting their battles, in their sleep.  
Likewise, the Moorish army in the midst of the woods,  
Already forewarned by savage fates, is driven into out-of-the-way tracks.  
In bitter dreams it sees its camp seized and already sighs.  
It languishes and can neither weep for its plundered camels  
Nor rise as an avenger, but sees its routed columns run  
Through all the plains and lift their right hands in vain,  
While others are laid low across trackless places by diverse fates.  
Each one wishes to run, but limbs languish with fear  
And collapse with the heaviness of sleep. The fierce enemy terrifies  
Everyone, redoubling a savage sword into each man's vitals.  
While one man prepares to avoid the blows of a violent wound,  
He strikes his wife's breast with his head in a jerking motion.  
She is snatched away then all the more. For a proud plunderer, dragging her by the hair,  
Bears her away from the safe woods.  
Thus each one seeing for himself dreams that shook their breasts  
Felt horror. Recounting their dreadful rest during the night  
They are terrified by what they saw, but rejoice that it was false;  
And time granted a brief respite to the wretched men.  
  
End of Book Two.  

## Book Three.  

Section 3.0.  
  
But cares weighed heavily on the heart of the unconquered general.  
Anxious for his men, he allowed no sleep to steal over his limbs,  
And keeping his eyes wide open, denying them sweet slumber,  
He stayed awake all night, assigning duties throughout the middle of the camp.  
Surrounding him in a dense formation, his officers  
likewise held a council on matters of the highest urgency,  
Dragging out the night with speech in varied debate.  
They spoke of the joys of military life and its grim hardships,  
And of everything the Latin army was achieving as it conquered the world,  
Now recounting in order the battles that had been successfully joined,  
Now recalling with fresh grief the wars that had gone badly.  
While they sang of these diverse things, the leader happened to speak thus:  
"What a state Africa was in back then upon our arrival,  
My comrades, when she was greatest under cruel realms,  
Until God’s vengeance was sent to rightly destroy the grim tyrant  
And the Vandal race in the hundredth year!  
With what violence the unspeakable Gelimer had harassed the Africans  
And brought guilt-ridden ruin, at that time when the great leader  
Belisarius brought the Sidonian citadels under our yoke,  
Presenting the captured tyrant to our emperors in the City!  
With what swift speed so many wars were finished with supreme  
Effort, and what worthy fortune followed our men!  
The army marched into shadows cast by thick  
trees, and the scorching heat of the swift sun  
Did not harm our soldiers, though it burned with the heat of summer,  
Even though it was autumn. And from the chariot of glowing Phoebus,  
Grim battles were fought on the burning sand.  
Nor was it any less joyful once the tyrant was captured and peace made.  
I left Libya fully inhabited and cultivated,  
And upon my departure, she remained in her proper,  
Improved state, as I remember well: she was fertile, overflowing  
With crops, and she brought back the fruit of the light-pouring olive  
And the glad juices of swelling Bacchus.  
Deep peace was on the land. What is this great madness of war?  
What insane fury has blazed up in these wretched fields?  
What Bellona moves them, lashing countless peoples with her unmerited  
Scourge? Or what Fury approaches,  
Mingling Phaethon's fires among the nations,  
And swallowing everything up like this in a mighty, cascading ruin?  
Come, speak, whoever is here to explain these times we ask about.”  
  
Section 3.1.  
  
Gentius, an excellent man, responding with his voice to the master,  
Himself a master, said: "O venerable one, worthy of highest favor,  
Supreme leader of commanders, pillar of a tottering land,  
Hope of Libya, favor of our people, and strength of our triumph,  
What the impious origin of this rising war was  
Is hidden from us, completely concealed in deep hiding places.  
Your tribune Caecilides, who comes from that region,  
If ordered to relate the critical moments of the war's beginning,  
Can explain everything: for as a citizen he knows all  
That has been committed in his own homeland—the peoples, the places,  
The author of the evil, and whether it is the wrath of a past time."  
Liberatus was commanded to speak with calm words.  
He quickly obeyed, speaking thus in a clear voice:  
"I strive, supreme leader, to narrate the causes of our misfortunes  
And to obey your commands. Yet while I attempt to speak,  
A harmful flame rises up, icy blood disturbs my heart,  
And the story barely makes its way past my faltering tongue.  
You command me to endure my own hardships all over again,  
While I relate the savage war that Africa has suffered.  
But because the lofty orders of my ruling master compel me,  
Be far from here, O grief: you are now conquered by a great daring.  
Commands must be obeyed, and followed with humble awe.  
In the beginning, Africa had already felt twin plagues;  
Now, to be pitied, she feels twin ruins all over again.  
Within our own borders arose the impiety  
Which has grown throughout the world. Guenfan is the grim origin for us wretches,  
Along with the birth of the fierce Antala. For in the ancient time,  
Peace was most secure in all the lands of Libya.  
Africa, now to be pitied, rejoiced, adorned with new crowns.  
The farmers wove wreaths from the golden ears of grain,  
And Bacchus grew red on the familiar vine,  
While shining Peace adorned the glad land with her own olive.  
This flourished for up to thirty years from Antala's  
Birth, and the greatest part of our world shone with power,  
Just as Lucifer [the morning star], gleaming across the whole sky,  
Conquers the flames of the stars with a superior flame.  
What the Guenfeian offspring brought forth in our lands,  
Supreme Father, reserve for their own heads and their race!  
  
Section 3.2.  
  
As a small child, he soon touched the breasts with fierce lips.  
While Megaera was already flashing with a terrible warning,  
A fearsome rumor flew about, singing of things to come. For the father himself  
Sought out the feigned temples of Ammon. Then, asking about the dreadful fates  
Of his wicked offspring, he slaughtered in a profane manner  
Dread sacrifices to Jupiter, and seeking from there the grim altars of Apollo,  
He begged for answers at the tripods and laurels of Phoebus.  
The most sorrowful blood was poured upon the horrific altars,  
And a ribbon-bound priestess slaughtered livestock of every kind,  
Swaying the fates; first, she searched for the fibers in the torn-out entrails,  
Inspecting them. A very long sequence lay revealed,  
And she placed the ominous organs upon the perpetual flames.  
Then she was swept away fiercely, suddenly seized by a terrible fury,  
And she plunged the knives deep into her own entrails,  
Driving them into herself. Abundant blood flowed from her body,  
And she redoubled the iron blade with wound after wound.  
She raised her hair high, then twisted her flashing eyes,  
Leaping up, whirling in bounds, driven through the malicious twistings  
Of her own body. A fiery redness stained her face.  
Struck by the mark of the divinity, her neck and hair tossed wildly,  
Falling down over both her shoulders. Her deep chest resounded  
With hoarse breaths. Murmurs mingled, then doubtful voices,  
And deep sighs agitated her full breast.  
Not otherwise does Vulcan, rising to the first fires,  
Artfully move the swollen winds to clash within the drawn bellows,  
And exciting the flames, he sharpens the sounding East Winds,  
Renewing the exhausted storms in the furnace.  
Then, singing of evil fates, she responded with hostile words:  
"The fates, Guenfan, draw on Vandal and Libyan ruins alike,  
And they loosen the yoke and reins from the Moors.  
As your Antala grows, all fury and wrath  
Will disturb the wretched world with a horrible torch.  
Flaming Tisiphone begins to rage with twisted water-snakes,  
And she has raised her stiff hair from her head  
Into the empty air. Drenched in black poisons,  
Her lips are wet; they defile the weather with eyes and three-forked tongues,  
And her temples bristle with dreadful gore.  
I see rivers running down from the mountains with Vandal blood.  
Behold, they burn the cities of Libya with fires!  
Behold, they seize the plunder and lock away what has been plundered!  
  
Section 3.3.  
  
Why, however, O heavenly ones, do you prepare to disturb so much?  
All things are mixed together. What does it benefit for the Moors to increase?  
Behold, they perish all over again. What did it help to have earned ruin  
By indomitable valor in so short a time?  
This child will rise up, and he will destroy many nations along with their name,  
And the times are swept away by a varied fate.  
For weary Africa will ask her own Maker,  
The God whom she herself worships, whom it is right for the worthy to know.  
Then the greatest author of the Roman name, alas,  
Sends the wealth of the East toward our region, which now we await.  
Look, he terrifies the whole world with his fleets.  
Now the wild boy suddenly trembles with a sudden paleness  
At the arrival of the fleet; panting, he already bears his yokes.  
Great weights tame him; he burns to burst the chains  
That he bears on his unworthy neck, and with his fetters broken,  
Behold, he prepares to wage war once more. A gathering  
Comes to him, look, and the boy runs to subvert the world.  
What does it help for nations doomed to perish to rise up here so greatly  
From the setting of the world? Why does he rise to such a height,  
Only to fall again? Presumptuous, he departs from our fields  
Burdened with spoils, and returning at last,  
Alas, he overflows the fields that are soaked with our blood!"  
As she spoke such things, fury twisted her face back over her shoulder  
With a shattered neck; and suddenly trembling,  
She fell silent and sank to the ground with a vast weight,  
And a faltering murmur ran through her exhausted lips.  
Just so, when a pipe draws down water-bearing channels  
In its hollow bronze, while the liquid stream runs and pours forth,  
Sounding hoarsely; if it pleases the quick master to stop the flowing conduits with art,  
He blocks the open path of the river with a twisted barrier,  
The flow of waters is cut off by the closed obstructions,  
The river flows back toward its source,  
And the remaining streamlet runs through with its exhausted wave.  
Lifted up by these strange responses, the race both rejoices and keeps silent,  
For that people was of humble station. Yet all hope and fear  
Converge on that boy; they guard and cherish the sacred omen,  
And they rejoice in the promised gift of the fates to them.  
The seventeenth year had taken him in his growth,  
When the boy moved his arms to unholy thefts.  
Just like Cacus, about to be deprived of light by Hercules' arms,  
He enters upon his journey of the night, a fearsome sight. He dragged away backwards  
The leader and father of the flock, and raised it upon his neck.  
Striving hard, he carries it in a fury and proceeds into his cave.  
  
Section 3.4.  
  
Then, pressing down, he strangles it with a clenched fist around its tight throat.  
The ram falls prostrate before the feet of the panting youth,  
The sole hope of the wool-bearing flock. Then, unsheathing his sword,  
He strips off the tight-fitting hide with great effort.  
The naked entrails lie exposed. The whole beast is cut  
Into deep pieces, and he transfixes the quivering limbs on spits.  
The carcass is roasted terribly over a scorching flame.  
Panting, he fearlessly devours it half-burned right from the midst of the fires,  
And consumed it all in his fierce, gaping maw.  
From that very time, the unspeakable boy begins to mature in savage thefts  
And to feed those who favored him with his hidden plunder.  
Next, he gathers men, and urging them to go silently into the deep darkness  
For unholy thefts, he trains them in his craft; now as a robber he drags off entire sheepfolds,  
And now herds of cattle. He prepares to hide his plunder in the high  
Mountains, seeking hiding places among concealed rocks  
And safe locations. Crouching low in a captured valley,  
He dares, while lying in ambush, to test the Vandal phalanxes.  
How many leaders, how many enemies he had slaughtered with the sword,  
Hemming them in by siege within the narrow passes!  
And drawing along the barbarian squadrons under a perverse deity,  
The impious youth prostrated them with his spear upon the wide plains!  
By now, time was pressing the wretched Vandals with bitter fates,  
And hastening on, it had already broken their kingdom in its hundredth year.  
Then first the Frexes began to burn cities in our lands,  
To lay waste to homes, to overrun the plains,  
And they dared to engage in hostile battles.  
Then the panting Naffur moves his forces from there,  
Gathers men from all the common folk, and presses on from the neighboring region.  
An impious lot drives us through terror to abandon our lands  
And our sweet hearths. The fierce plunderer rages: now there is no place  
Where life is safe or certain. Driven by hostile fates, we are hard-pressed.  
At the time of the Vandal kingdom's fall, our joys  
Perished along with it. It was then our lot to weep for our overturned homes, wretches that we were,  
And, as unworthy exiles, to search for safe lands.  
Hildimer then waged battles under ill-fated standards,  
Unaccustomed to hand-to-hand combat. By no strength of his own  
Could he win, nor was he able to clash with such great forces.  
The threads of his life are snapped by an adverse fate  
In the midst of the mountains, where an unfavorable downfall terrifies:  
Often angry Fortune protects the guilty while destroying the pious.  
  
Section 3.5.  
  
The standards had already halted, proud upon the ridges and in the woods:  
From above, from the crags, their battle line had hemmed in the enemy,  
Nor did those hard-pressed have any hope of flight, nor could they trust their numbers,  
Nor was there any chance to hope for life, nor could they make a stand against them  
Any longer; so heavily was that band delayed by the treacherous rocks.  
The impious race was shielded by broken stones,  
And the walled enemy was surrounded by cleft valleys.  
In the midst of the woods and groves is a malicious place,  
Vast and fortified on all sides by sheer cliffs.  
Though it is steep itself and rises prominent with its highest peak,  
And though the rocks are covered by dense willow-thickets,  
Along the ridges of the starry mountain, fields with a flat summit  
Spread out; no path lies open, and scarcely a single trail, broken on all sides  
By twisting paths, reaches the heights of the lofty field.  
The trackless ways are enclosed by a dense forest.  
When Hildimer saw the ditches hedged in by this fortification,  
And that the ground for battle stood on a precipice with no path remaining,  
He feared to risk so savage a danger.  
Then he himself ordered the dense lines to halt under their own standards,  
Doubtful where he should advance against the enclosed enemy.  
All things turn out contrary when the fates are adverse.  
Phoebus, pressing the middle of the sky with his torch,  
Was now entirely burning; a scorching heat blazed in their dry throats,  
And the excessive warmth deeply troubled the wretched men.  
By chance, thirst led certain men down toward cool waters.  
The men went back behind the lines, for the river at that time  
Was further away. Servants were carrying skins filled from the springs,  
Spotted in a hollow valley not far from there.  
As soon as the first Vandal, reaching those ill-fated waters,  
Praised the quenching of his thirst, then the rest of the crowd  
Gathered in a run toward the streams; thus the fates were drawing them on,  
Thus harmful Fortune had condemned the columns to death.  
Look, a standard-bearer above, daring to believe the signal was to change,  
Turned his reins back down from the highest mountains:  
The army followed him with their packed weapons.  
  
Section 3.6.  
  
The pathless breaks lie open while they strive to grasp the crags.  
They turn back behind the lines. The enemy believed they were fleeing  
And rages down from the top of the mountain. That downfall terrified  
Even the commanders. The standards are snatched backward,  
Overturned in flight. There was no plain where a free horse could run  
And cross over swiftly with loosened reins.  
Through cliffs and crags they fall in fear, and over the steep  
Rocks of the mountain, they now plunge down by their own weight.  
The fates press the wretches, and the running enemy terrifies them:  
He burns with fury and pursues. From all sides a vast  
Crowd falls, and they impale themselves through their chests  
On their own weapons. Others come headlong onto the spears of those falling,  
And the weight of their fellow citizens drives others back.  
Just so, thrown into equal confusion, the dense columns of men  
Rush down from the high mountains. The brave horse, tossed in the whirlwind  
Of its swift course, clashes together with its rider and weapons,  
And crushes its master beneath its vast weight:  
Just as when coming hail strips the gray olive tree,  
Shaking the fruits from the tree as its crown is buffeted,  
Then, cast down headlong to the earth by the weight of the rainstorm,  
The tender branch is likewise shaken off, shattered by the hail.  
It was not enemy valor that oppressed the men, but envious  
Fates, which were hastening to destroy so great a nation.  
From here the shattered army returns, and overthrows the trembling king,  
Weary with the number of his years and terrified by the disaster,  
And gave the scepter from that moment to a savage tyrant.  
Then the Emperor grieved that the treaty of the kingdom had been broken,  
Then Rome sought Libya with her accustomed triumphs.  
But yet, in a brief time of disaster, quite a lot of things  
Perished. All places felt the grim war.  
For Africa was then hard-pressed between twin plagues:  
On one side burning war oppresses, on the other the plundering tyrant.  
Fortune withdraws the protection of sweet safety from the wretches  
And threatens death in alternating warfare.  
Whom should they flee? Where should they fall? There is plunder everywhere,  
There is terror everywhere for the men. Thus throughout those savage pillages  
Libya, the greatest of lands, was entirely stripped bare,  
Just as a wrecked ship perishes, alas, in treacherous winds.  
Pitying these plagues of the Carthaginians, the pious Emperor took them away  
In his accustomed manner, bringing the highest consolation to weary Africans,  
And as victor he cast down both evils,  
Honoring the Sidonian fathers with better titles.  
  
Section 3.7.  
  
Your hand snatched the wretched Africans from the very jaws of death  
And dragged the cruel yoke away from their weary necks.  
Africa rose up, lifted high by your triumphs:  
You granted joys after mourning to a friendly land,  
While you crush the tribes, while you conquer the whole world.  
The fierce leaders of the Moors trembled, subdued by your valor.  
Then each one, fearing your battles, eagerly hastened to endure  
Of his own accord the reins and laws of the Emperor. Flourishing,  
Our land felt these joys, set free throughout ten full years,  
And, although they were rebels because the fates impelled them,  
The enemy fell before he could carry off his plunder.  
Africa did not consent in mind to war at that time  
While you kept watch, father. Leucada witnessed the battles  
And your valor. The fields grow fat with blood,  
They grow white with bones; struck by the plowshare are heads torn  
From shoulders, and trunks scattered through the grass  
By your sword. It is known to all what you achieved in that  
Warfare, by the favor of God. Who could have marked the fields  
With such great trophies? You adorned them, great man, bringing honor,  
And you often increased the triumphs of Solomon.  
Behold, Laudas attempted war at that same time  
And suffered for it: yet before he even saw the open plains,  
He trembled to see the Romans running through the midst of the woods.  
But Stutzas began a war from our own faction.  
This was madness, this was wrath, this was an unspeakable care  
Added to our holy affairs: civil war was reborn.  
Then Carthage felt fierce pillaging, the treaty being broken,  
And unspeakable danger in an unjust war.  
But yet he was conquered in a swift moment and yielded:  
Membressa saw him clash upon her fields,  
And saw him seek that same flight, when the greatest victor,  
Belisarius, routed the enemy with modest forces.  
Victory saw you too through the midst of the battles,  
And as a brave man, you slaughtered columns in the breached camp  
With your heavy sword, and you slew men with a similar valor,  
While Germanus scattered the fierce and conquered tyrant.  
Cellas Vatari watched you with wondrous admiration,  
And Autenti had seen you slaughtering the savage enemy.  
  
Section 3.8.  
  
Then a most rich repose was made for our land.  
No war, no rapacious plunderer, no greedy soldier  
Entered the rustic homes; no household goods were touched,  
And everything was full of good things: Libya had a secure peace throughout her world.  
Then Ceres was fertile, then the vine-tendril was glad with grapes,  
And the mottled tree shone with jewel-like olives.  
The soldier, innocent of wrongdoing, rejoiced in his own quarters.  
The farmer in every quarter had begun to plant his new shoots,  
And yoking the steers brought to the plows,  
He sowed the fields exulting, and sang calmly from the mountain.  
The glad traveler even dared to sing by the light of the moon.  
Rich in resources was that fertile peace. Everywhere,  
Every merchant sang. Sweet songs and light voices  
Resounded through the carefree lands. Exulting on one side sang the plowman,  
On the other the traveler rejoiced. Therefore the Muses would soothe  
And relieve the hearts of men with varied songs.  
The land was full of freedom at this time, but only briefly.  
Envious are the threads of the fates for this wretched world.  
Why, Lachesis, do you hold the destinies of men hanging by a slender thread?  
You push them lightly: and now the world is broken,  
Which terror, moving all things, would press upon with strong chains of bronze and iron,  
But wrath would not break;  
And now that an improved Africa had recovered new strength,  
A plague began to devastate the race of men and a failing world.  
Arriving in these shores of ours, it raged.  
Never was so grim an image of death heard of,  
Not when the world was raw in its novelty, nor in the time of Pyrrha.  
For the death-bearing year, throwing the wretches into confusion with monsters,  
Had mixed the spirits of the dead with the gods above, and men saw themselves  
Receive wounds from divine arrows,  
And then they saw varied plagues rise up from the deepest earth  
And fierce visions. By now there was no terror at bitter  
Deaths; no one closed their eyes fearing destruction,  
Whatever age-group collapsed. The human race was deprived  
Of bitter tears, and there were no mourning eyes,  
While each person feared for himself. No one then rendered any  
Service of a funeral; mourning did not sound through the world,  
No groom wept for his bride, no bride for her husband,  
No parent grieved for her children, nor a child for a parent.  
O hearts wickedly failing to weep anywhere for a new death!  
A public mourning was merited, but eyes in no household  
Poured forth tears: for death was a common sight to every eye.  
  
Section 3.9.  
  
By now the empty cities were inactive, devoid of Libyan citizens;  
And scarcely a single person, occasionally wandering through many houses,  
Would seek the riches of a parent through a long lawsuit.  
Now full of wealth, an undeserving man became the heir  
Of a thousand relatives. Unknown to his ancestors, he carried off  
The inheritances, the crops, the silver and clothing alike, and the yellow metal,  
And filled his estates with such great wealth.  
His unsatiated chest was piled high with great treasures,  
For destitute passion is never satisfied for the greedy.  
They grew warm with new marriages, and they receive  
Wealthy widows; a virgin was sought by no husbands:  
A wife is sought for the money of her deceased husband,  
A small dowry is given to virgins; thus in this dire time  
No woman cared to offer marital mourning.  
From this, all the forums stand open, and grim lawsuits  
Begin to arise. Discord rages throughout the whole world,  
Stirring up savage quarrels. Piety has completely withdrawn.  
No one followed justice with a remorseful mind.  
It is because of this that the Almighty Father, urged by wrath,  
Added scourges to the wretches with no time delayed,  
And, being angry, took away the lawsuits from all.  
He raises up a rising enemy from our borders.  
One man was grieving his brother's death with a shaken mind,  
But there was no strength by which he could rise up.  
While, however, he kept a hidden war within his breast,  
He felt that a wretched fate had diminished the countless hands  
Of his soldiers. Plague, a friend to Mars, did not harm the bitter  
Tribes: fearing it, however, the Moor was careful  
Lest he catch the evil contagion of the plundered land.  
But when the plague was driven away, the fervor of Mars began  
To succeed it and compelled unspeakable battle lines to war.  
He sent to the furthest sands of thirsting Libya,  
Where once Phaethon, cast down by the lightning bolt,  
Had dried the earth with excessive sun, and he instructed  
The malicious peoples and filled their ears with our disasters.  
And now fierce tribes pour into the Libyan region.  
Everywhere a raging robber begins to run through our lands  
And to lay waste to homes and hurl fire into the dwellings  
And, burning with rage, to set fire to the cities with flames.  
  
Section 3.10.  
  
When Solomon saw such a great war rising,  
He assembled the Roman battle lines driven from all directions,  
Hastening to clash with his adverse fates.  
Straightaway a page is dispatched on an ill-fated course,  
And behold, rushing in flight, forced by an unjust war,  
Comes the leader of the Moors, the friend of wretched Solomon,  
A man always exceedingly faithful to the Roman cause:  
Cusina, vast in resources, bringing the Mastraciani with him.  
Bold Pelagius, who was then the leader of Tripoli, also comes:  
But dragging the harsh and pagan Mecales with him to the wars,  
He advanced with forces that were not auspicious;  
Unaware of their guile, he received the treacherous Ifuraces.  
The mind remains dark whenever you, Fortune, threaten.  
Alas, pitiable Solomon, you ought to have joined these  
To your camp. Trapped by shifted destinies, it always drives  
The opposite result: no one will be able to avoid what is at hand.  
And now the final day had arrived at our shores:  
Now threatening fate was hastening to destroy Libya.  
He joins battle, mingling the fight in the midst of the woods,  
Fearless and trusting in his men. Already he had conquered the enemy,  
And now the fleeing enemy line had begun to turn its back,  
Driven by fear, and now burning with rage he himself runs through the enemy  
And follows the overturned squadrons through trackless places.  
Then, suddenly, loyalty was shattered. Fortune twisted  
Her face backward. Lachesis then snapped the threads of life,  
And wounded Victory drew back her upturned wings.  
Then the grim fates found their own servant.  
The ill-starred Guntarith terrified our column:  
He completely succeeded in overturning the Roman forces.  
A threatening Fortune had not conquered him, nor a pressing enemy,  
Nor had sharp fear driven him, but turning with a malicious mind,  
He withdraws his standards and feigns flight. When the whole cohort  
Saw him trembling, it follows and deserts its commander in the midst of the plains,  
While he was still mingling in battle among the ditches.  
This harsh lot then gave new wrath to the enemy  
And valor at the same time, but to our men death, fear,  
And an obscene flight, which is accustomed to destroy nations with a shameful death.  
The enemy closes in, stronger now with packed weapons, raging bitterly.  
While that very tumult was hanging over them,  
Alas, blameless Solomon fell, his chest transfixed by harsh spears.  
Then the entire order is overturned.  
  
Section 3.11.  
  
Raging in the manner of Mars, no one remained a friend  
To our cause now, but every friendly band, seizing plunder,  
Ran through the battlefields. Then the sorrowful plowman wept,  
Fleeing as the enemy carried off his unyoked steers.  
Then all homes perished, then all household goods.  
Nor did the poor man alone succumb, shattered by that downfall:  
The rich man was plunged into ruin equally with the poor.  
After Solomon’s time of wealth, freedom was made a prey for pillage,  
Nor was there any part secure from the malicious war.  
Everywhere the raging robber burned cities and fields.  
Nor did the crop or tree, dissolved by blazing fire,  
Perish alone: he devoured the herds that were free from the plague,  
And all Africa was trodden underfoot by her Moorish masters.  
Oh, the grief! No battle line could make a stand in the fields now,  
Nor could the soldier defend the enclosed walls:  
With God showing his wrath, all things were given over to pillage.  
The treacherous Stutzas also ran through the battlefields once more,  
With his master Antala dominating, and the tyrant earned the right  
To run about plundering all things under a Moorish lord.  
  
End of Book Three.  

## Book Four.  

Section 4.0.  
  
The leader lies dead—a tyrant whose very name is unspeakable to utter.  
A harsher grief now swells from my innermost bones and marrow,  
Shattering my peace. My mind is too numb and frozen to recount  
The slaughter of so many captains, the downfall of my comrades,  
And my own ruin. Our treacherous soldiers forced our panicked ranks  
To surrender their standards to the enemy. It would take too long to tell of our destruction,  
But let me record the crimes of this war in unvarnished words.  
Himerius was the commander and defender of the besieged city,  
Guarding the locked walls and high towers with his troops.  
Deceit dragged the wretched men right out from within the walls  
And delivered them into Moorish servitude. For while he sat in the city,  
A flattering letter written in ill-fated verse was delivered to him.  
It was penned under the commander's name, and a Sinon-like soldier entering  
Claimed it came from John. We read the tyrant's commands,  
Believing them to be orders from our own general. Through that letter,  
Acting as our leader, he repeatedly urged us to meet him out in the open plains  
And together overturn the scattered trenches of the Moors.  
The tribunes' spirits soared, though their minds remained uneasy.  
We gave the order to move the standards. Swifter than a silent breeze,  
The commander himself marched out with us, and through the shadow of the night  
The cavalry galloped, eager to join forces with their allies,  
The men fearing they were moving too late. But sprinting ahead of them all  
Went that wicked Sinon, laying his traps and preparing the tribes.  
As a somber Sun brought his fire-breathing horses up from the icy waves,  
The trap sprung wide. It was then, oh wretched us, that we saw the tyrant's banners  
Bearing down on our own, and the rabid Moors swarming across the entire plain.  
We tried to pull back our panicked steps—for who could possibly  
Stand against them? Raging through the meadows after our terrified troops  
Came Antalas and Stutzas. There was no path to safety,  
And a dense swarm of enemies circled, pressing hard on our panicked men.  
Death stared us in the face, and cruel fortune denied us any help.  
(Oh, if only we had fallen dead in the middle of that field!  
A shameful disgrace would then be far removed from our arms.)  
Snatched away from the thick of it by a happy death, John  
Fell; he did not have to endure the arrogance of a victorious enemy master  
By a similar fate, nor did he fear a master's swords as a captive.  
And so, fleeing across the wide fields, our brave horses were forced  
to gallop—their hooves repeatedly drumming against the hard ground—  
While the savage enemy pursued us relentlessly down from the mountains.  
  
Section 4.1.  
  
High on the open plains stands the fortress of Cebar.  
Hither our wretched band turned their horses' reins;  
Cavalrymen and their miserable tribunes occupied the stronghold,  
And the commander was there in their midst. To shut the gates  
Was not our concern. We protected the horses, prepared ourselves  
For a foot battle, and drove back the advancing enemies from the rooftops.  
The twin tyrants had rushed in with a gathering crowd,  
Along with the Laguatan cohort and the Naffur in dense armor.  
Then Stutzas, pretending to halt the brutal warfare,  
Flew like fire into the midst of the battle lines with drawn sword.  
With cunning he warned the fierce Moors to withdraw from the field,  
And checked their rising fury with unspeakable words.  
They withdrew from the spot. The wicked man stood on a high mound  
As if peaceful, swaying hesitant hearts with flattering words.  
Yet at the same time he promised and urged on war,  
Exhorting the men to battle with varied forms of speech.  
Now threatening, now flattering he stood. Overcome by terror,  
Our men threw down their weapons, and swiftly ran  
To the knees of the tyrant, greeting him with friendly voice.  
Among the leaders there was now no safety. Why recount everything?  
We begged for mercy; it was granted at once. We forced the enemy  
To swear by their own lives, and they did so. Under compulsion,  
We pretended to serve, as it were, the unspeakable tyrants.  
Justinianopolis was then handed over to the savage Moors  
Under an uncertain fate. Afterwards, I was able to sway  
The minds of our comrades through my words. Marturius,  
Mature in counsel, was with me. We both compelled  
Their wavering minds to return to their own standards.  
The men agreed, and realized they were in a hostile camp.  
Gradually we attempted escape. The friendly shadows of the night  
Snatched me away in my panic, accompanied by a crowd,  
And arriving home, I saw my own dwelling and my wife.  
Marturius fled and deserted the unspeakable enemy.  
Afterward the soldiers followed. Whoever wished to do so remained.  
Justinianopolis opened its gates to our nocturnal forces;  
A friendly citizen laid them open through a loyal betrayal.  
He could not bear the yoke of the brutal tyrant.  
Yet they kept the imperial standards safe within the closed walls,  
Nor could anyone, engaging in the open field,  
Assail the enemy's force, nor did the general  
Wish to trust his allies any longer while the malignant Stutzas lived.  
  
Section 4.2.  
  
While Africa was being crushed, exhausted by such great perils,  
The seas shone with the prows of Ariobindus' fleet.  
The enemy was stunned by the general's arrival: the terrified tribe  
Of the Laguatan withdrew from the land. If only he, in his weakness,  
Had never seen the homes of the Carthaginians! Then Africa  
Endured disasters with a heavier woe, and suffered savage plunders  
Between the two commanders. A power once delegated knows not how  
To tolerate two equals: no age has ever brooked  
A partner in friendship. Thus the examples of our ancestors  
Themselves teach us. The second foot follows the movement of the first  
In the stride, and its own limbs follow the pattern of the head,  
And the branches yield fruit of their own kind upon the tree:  
The world, while still raw and scarcely happy with its small ears of grain,  
Could not bear two rulers, nor could Rome, greatest of all things,  
Which consecrated her new walls with her own brother's blood.  
Rival ambitions split their distracted minds,  
And looking down on his equal, each remained at odds.  
The state was divided into two factions:  
Each group held to its own leader. And while that proud man  
Believed himself to be first, the other did not consider himself second,  
And so naked Africa wailed, stripped by barbarian plundering.  
By command of the generals, the men of the brave and deserving John  
Assailed the fierce enemy with unequal forces,  
But they were conquered and fled: bitter discord of mind  
Defeated the men. Once more the commander John himself,  
Moving his standards, advanced into the thick of the enemy to die,  
Trusting in his own valor, and drawing brave tribunes  
With him to the war, he did not fear to clash with such monsters.  
A content love for his country disdained the wounds of a cruel death.  
When he perceived that the enemy's fierce approach was hastening,  
No less eager did he willingly seek death. He positioned his standards on the plain  
And, relying on his comrades, spoke thus: “To despise life  
For one's country—this is truly life. Death, by the law of the Thunderer,  
Has been brought upon the human race and passes no one by:  
For it will come in one way or another. But for an honorable death,  
The highest honor, the highest praise, and a sweet longing endure.  
  
Section 4.3.  
  
The enemy is at the gates, comrades. How often will we flee from the glorious  
Work of Mars? How often must the enemy mock us as fugitives  
And think us cowards? Now is the time to face the challenge before us,  
Now is the time for courage. I have always known your courage,  
Men, and always your loyalty. Stand up together, citizens  
Of Rome, dutiful for the sake of glory, and break the proud  
Nations. For now, rid your leaders of such great shame.  
Suppose we did choose flight: the enemy tramples down fugitives,  
Slaughtering men like women. Turn your minds around,  
And let dishonor depart far from our shades.  
Oh, if only that fierce Stutias would cross paths with my weapons  
Right now, and fortune had given me the man himself!  
Either our breasts would have lain open at the same time to alternating steel  
And savage fate would sweep us both away, or a cruel arrow  
Would have pierced the tyrant's body with a lethal wound.  
Or, if my fates threaten me so, let the republic at least with my fall  
Be free from the guilt of a civil war.”  
While he was encouraging the men, behold, the fierce Massylian line  
Rushed forward with hostile standards and stood drawn up in a long formation  
By the banks of the nearby river through a cunning trick.  
Then for the first time swift arrows initiated the battle:  
Wicked bodies were pierced by winged iron.  
Yielding across the river, the enemy line turned their horses  
Backward. He followed, and, through a dense hail of missiles,  
He broke through the river with his characteristic agility. Fearing neither danger  
Nor death, he crossed that path of no return  
And attacked the men. Just like a harsh devastator,  
A lion tearing apart herds and fierce bulls,  
Chasing this one, and slaughtering that one, and the trembling flock disperses  
And runs everywhere along with the fleeing shepherd, while he rages with bloody  
Teeth, crushing and chewing them across the wide fields,  
Just so did the leader John throw the Massylian lines into chaos by his courage,  
Spreading death in the middle of the plains.  
Imitating him, his cohort pressed hard upon the fleeing columns  
With their swords, piercing them through. The fiery leader himself flew across the whole plain,  
Raging alongside his companions, and pursued the fleeing Moors  
Like a triumphing victor. Already, with much blood,  
Their weapons grew warm: already the fighting right hand of both soldier  
And leader carried stained iron. Oh, what a sad wickedness  
Of unfair fate! Oh, what a great disaster! Already the conquered enemy had yielded,  
And everywhere through the meadows the victorious Roman cavalry  
Was cutting down and killing the opposing troops,  
When suddenly the fierce Stutias rushed out from the middle of the valleys,  
Moving his hostile standards, and alongside him Hermogenes and Taurus—the shame  
Of the Latin name—charged forward.  
  
Section 4.4.  
  
Following them comes a Roman force—not ours, but rebels.  
Then, once more, they clashed with wretched weapons,  
And civil war was renewed. Kindred breasts  
Were targeted, guts were spilled by kindred right hands.  
High-minded John was the first to recognize the standards of Stutias,  
And facing him, he stood his ground more fiercely, bending his bow.  
But his allies took to flight, unable to endure  
Even the sight of the raging tyrant. He, however, placed an arrow  
Upon the string and, drawing it back with powerful strength, loosed it.  
The shaft passed right through the thigh of the fierce tyrant,  
Shattering the bone of the wild man as it struck, and reached  
The soft marrow, and its buried feathers grew red with blood.  
Blood spurted out from there, splattering his master's garments.  
Wounded Stutias, overcome by the lethal injury,  
Turned his horse and fled toward the rear. As he collapsed toward death,  
His allies caught him and laid him beneath a thick tree.  
Looking back, the victor saw his companions fleeing through the meadows,  
And an immense grief burned deep within his bones.  
Then, grim and lamenting their downfall, he cried out to them:  
“Whom are you fleeing, comrades? The victory is yours, citizens.  
Already Stutias, pierced by our arrow, has abandoned the war  
And lies prostrate on the ground. Turn your standards around!  
Where are you rushing? Where does this wretched fate lead you?  
Alas, Roman courage, you are perishing!” With such words he  
Rebuked the panic-stricken troops under the sway of their fate.  
But not a single man turned back. The Moorish force  
Pursued them, and so many thousands charged down upon the terrified men.  
There was a steep riverbed in the middle of the fields,  
Dividing both borders of that kindred land.  
Here the fleeing band fell in, and in their terror of death  
They tumbled down the banks and into the ditches. A wretched slaughter  
Unfolded. Alas, the crushing weight of civil strife and the fear of the enemy  
Pressed hard upon the miserable men. The very momentum of their fall drove many  
Onto weapons. Some impaled their own breasts on their own pikes,  
While a powerful horse, falling on top of its master in a rattling crash,  
Crushed him under its massive weight.  
Thus perished that pitiable force and the conquered tribunes.  
  
Section 4.5.  
  
At the same time, the leader Marturius, who was with them,  
Could scarcely manage to flee. Ready to die,  
He rushed into the enemy with a small band. Fortune provided safety  
To the daring man and rescued him from a harsh death.  
Meanwhile Stutias, with his life slipping away, was full of grief.  
The wild man repented having waged war. He drew deep sighs  
With groaning, and lamenting his lot, he rebuked himself thus:  
“What so dreadful a passion for war had possessed me?  
Why was I ungrateful to the master of the world, never loyal,  
And made unhappy by my own prowess? Only now do I repent these deeds,  
While you, cruel death, drag me down in my misery. I will pay the penalty, wicked as I am,  
Which I have deserved. Catiline is here, driven wild by the furies, a companion to my madness.  
I see Tartarus already splitting open,  
And spheres of flames and dreadful fires rolling.  
This is the reward for my treachery, which, alongside the guilt of a harsh death,  
These wars bring to me. Let the Latins grieve and beware  
Of these punishments, and let them keep faith with the state and their masters.”  
He spoke, and wicked death closed off his unhappy soul.  
When Stutias fell, although the people's grief over the leader's death  
Was bitter, courage began to grow again for the Romans,  
And our line was already taking to the fields.  
Look, here again is Guntarith, with his wicked mind—malignant,  
Treacherous, ill-fated, fierce, foolish, an adulterer,  
A robber, a murderer, rapacious, the worst instigator of wars.  
He seized the unsuspecting leader with cruel weapons,  
Overwhelmed him through trickery, captured him, and broke his oath.  
Reverence for so great an emperor did not move him at all,  
Nor did he fear to take up war and the title of tyrant.  
What slaughters the Tyrians suffered, what savage dangers they endured!  
They lay beneath a heavy sword, but only for a short time:  
The unspeakable and terrible days of his reign were cut short.  
For that good father Athanasius rescued the Africans from the midst of the villain's slaughter  
By his supreme counsels. He alone was able to restore Libya  
To the Roman annals and condemn the hostile tyrant to death.  
The Armenian Artabanes was then the agent of so great a plan.  
The elderly father, with his hoary gravity, calmly urged him  
To slay the ruthless man. In his old age, the father did not fear  
To risk danger for the sake of freedom.  
  
Section 4.6.  
  
Wretched Guntarith, desiring to hold a fierce kingdom,  
Felt Armenian swords in the midst of cruel feasting  
And defiled the ill-omened tables with his blood.  
Africa, perishing through such great plundering, remained unavenged,  
And so was overwhelmed. In her helplessness, she awaits you. Succor her in her groaning,  
For you have the power, and your courage is already known throughout the world,  
Your senses are vigilant, and your right hand is renowned for noble deeds.  
Recalling such things, the tribune related the stories of the wars,  
Clouding his eyes with tears, and with bitter grief he lamented the Libyan ruins  
And all the fates of the leaders.  
The kind leader groaned, his mind deeply shaken.  
The other leaders groaned together; they rose up for battle with indomitable  
Spirits. Grief and shame vexed their fierce hearts.  
Then a flush stained their cheeks—they grew pale and blushed at once—  
Nor was the fury hidden on their faces. Now they longed for the hesitant light  
To rise, and they complained that the dawn was slow and the night too long.  
Phoebus was furrowing the blue sky, bursting through with gleaming light,  
And brandishing his wandering rays through the clouds,  
He scattered light in the shimmering waves of his torch,  
And the day arose, most welcome to the wretched Africans.  
And already the leaders, stirring up the army with various shouts,  
Were urging on both the brave men and the renowned tribunes.  
Each one drove and encouraged his own men. They ordered them to move camp,  
To prepare weapons, and to look to the commands of their master.  
The men raised the standards and decorated them, rejoicing as they watched  
The favorable breezes playing against the unfurled sails.  
But father John, rising up with a deeply moved spirit  
And a pious heart, rested on his knees with bended knee,  
Suppliantly stretching forth both his hands along with his eyes,  
And chanting with his lips, he uttered these words: “To you, O Christ,  
Supreme Parent, glory is rightfully given by the tongues and pure hearts of men,  
And gladly I render praises and thanks.  
I wish to praise no other. You are the creator of the world,  
You tame nations and wars, you shatter impious  
Weapons, you who are accustomed to bring help to our affairs.  
Look upon the cities set ablaze by harsh nations,  
Almighty One, and behold the fields. No plowman now  
Cultivates the lands; no priest is able to offer tears throughout the temples  
On behalf of the people anymore. For all on the mountains  
Endure harsh chains, with their hands bound behind their backs.  
  
Section 4.7.  
  
Look down, holy Father, and let your thunderbolts no longer hold back.  
Trample the Moorish hosts beneath our feet,  
Rescue the captive Africans from these cruel nations,  
And looking with your customary mercy upon your Roman children,  
Be gracious, and favorably turn our grief into joy.”  
Speaking these words, he drenched the dry sands with his tears.  
Indeed, grief and piety moved his kindly mind,  
Shaking his soul and racking his limbs with thick sobs.  
When he fell silent, bringing all to a close with well-pleased words,  
The hero then rose up exultant. Wiping the flowing streams  
From his eyes, now with a calm countenance,  
He looked back and ordered the armed cohorts to quicken their pace.  
He himself took a hill, where, by standing higher and loftier,  
He might support all his officers with his counsel, according to his custom.  
The chosen leaders and brave tribunes gathered,  
Along with the soldiers arriving packed tightly in their columns.  
They surrounded the leader on all sides in a crowding throng.  
It was as how bees form a swarm with their feet linked  
To one another, while closely following their king,  
Til the leader has taken the tip of a tree, its highest peak,  
Or the middle of a wild ash with dense foliage,  
And he himself sits first, awesome with his buzzing, and chooses  
The spot, and around him the army gathers on thick wings,  
And they eagerly receive the commands of their king.  
Meanwhile, behold, a scout comes racing from the high mountains,  
And seeing the dense throngs standing there  
And the master shining in their midst on the high hill,  
He twisted the reins of his swift, wing-footed horse with his right hand,  
And heading for the leader, he quickened his flying pace through the grass;  
Dashing forward, he broke through the thick line,  
Leapt down from his horse, and then, according to custom, pressed  
Kisses upon the feet of the good master. And now the whole eager  
Crowd converged upon him, longing to know the turn of events  
And to hear him report the response of the fierce tyrant.  
  
Section 4.8.  
  
And they worried: would the enemy sue for peace? Then, ordered to speak,  
Amantius obeyed and, with a calm countenance, he told them:  
“Fulfilling all that my master commanded his servant,  
I ran into the midst of the nations, carrying your mandates,  
And arriving, I saw the wild tyrant beneath a cliff  
On the highest mountains. Then he called out to the rebels,  
Summoning them with his voice. At a rapid pace the Moorish throng  
Streamed together, and their black faces filled the tents.  
Just as they say Dis once held a council when about to wage war  
Against the gods, and a thousand monsters came through the wide paths—  
The Hydra and grim Megaera, and old Charon  
Running about with his deserted skiff,  
Mighty Tisiphone shaking a pine torch heavy with flames,  
Alecto raging with her twisted, cruel snakes,  
And all the hideous faces that are shown beneath vast Avernus.  
And when the dense column arrived and stood around their leader,  
He himself, seated, offered a place at the council to the worthy men of the host.  
Yet the prefect sat in their midst. Then, looking upon them all,  
Puffed up, he thundered thus from his rabid mouth:  
'Nobles, I want you to know that I have received the envoys  
And the harsh demands of John, and I want this man to lay out  
His spoken message before everyone. Listen to him openly with attentive  
Ears, and decide, if anything is fixed in your minds,  
Whether we should seek peace or war.'  
They permitted me to speak and imposed silence on their tongues  
With their fingers. I explained the commands of our ordering  
Emperor, and his custom. In a long discourse I went through how the great  
Mercy of the ruler encompasses all things.  
I recounted his indomitable courage blended with goodness.  
Often adding threats, I said that the Romans are merciful,  
But nevertheless have always broken proud nations  
Utterly, and have crushed powerful tyrants in war,  
Enumerating those whom mighty Rome and our own emperor  
Subdued when they attempted battles from the ends of the earth.  
I asserted war, and returning to the topic of peace,  
I laid it out, and falling silent at last, I demanded their answers.  
Raging among themselves with broken speech, they gave vent  
To various barkings, uttering horrible words with hissing tongues,  
And bound themselves to one another in a strange, renewed terror,  
Just like wolves in the middle of a mountain, when clouds  
Hang over the wide lands, and they strike the air with frequent howls,  
And echo with their barkings through the hollow shadows.  
  
Section 4.9.  
  
When their minds grew calm, then that proud man  
Responded to me with these bitter words:  
'That broken faith of the Roman empire has already been  
Thoroughly made known to me of late. Let no one think he can  
Deceive Antalas any further. It is quite enough that such a thing  
Was permitted to the Armenian just once. What? Do you pretend to be my friends  
With your cunning? Was I not yours? Did I not often hasten to your aid?  
Did I not deserve reward, carefully obeying orders? Did I not wage battles  
On behalf of your leaders, Roman? Surely the republic is  
Loyal enough—as your own blood confesses, my brother  
Guarizila, which was shed by the command of an unjust leader,  
And as the reward given to your Armenian has taught me,  
Because, relying on our courage, he was able to break  
The tyrant Guntarith here. Thus faithful to an amicable peace,  
Thus deserving, because I often favored your triumphs,  
Have I received these fitting rewards? Because I fought wars for you,  
Is this your loyalty? Are friends repaid in such a way?  
If only fortune had just now given Artabanes to me,  
By a new twist of fate, even as our brave Laguatan chief  
Rules the camp and the nations from the borders of the wild South!  
Yet look at those whom your leader John prepares to test in battle  
With a tiny force. He who thinks to seek our peace,  
Does so not with a humble voice, but clever in his art,  
He constrains with empty terrors those whom it was proper to entreat.  
After so many triumphs won by my subject warfare,  
Which my right hand bestowed, after their leaders were destroyed by my courage—  
After fierce Solomon, after the doom of the first John—  
Will it be permitted for anyone to challenge Antalas in war?  
Let the lamb pasturing in the valleys terrify the wolf then,  
And let the lion now fear the stag rising up with its horns;  
Let the vigorous hound dread the hare and the doe,  
And the fierce hawk fear the gentle dove beneath the cloud!  
Let the very armor-bearer of Jove fly terrified into the clear ether  
From the raucous crane or the singing swan,  
And let all nature perish with her laws reversed!  
Look, it pleases the Romans to clash yet again, though so often conquered.  
Let them return once more and try their luck in battle.'  
Scarcely had the envoy spoken these things when, in the midst of his words,  
A varied murmur ran through the leaders and through the fiery columns.  
It was just as when a fierce whirlwind strikes at the onset of a rising storm,  
And the buffeted seas groan, crashing against the broken shores;  
And the wave redoubles its sounds, and all the coasts roar  
With the swell, and the rough shores re-echo with the frequent surge.  
  
Section 4.10.  
  
The leader commanded silence with his raised and beckoning right hand.  
Swiftly they fell silent, and with rapt attention the men  
Looked up at their master. Every face and mind then  
Converged upon him, eager to hear the counsel of their commanding  
General. They inclined their senses and their ears to his words.  
The ruler then encouraged the astonished cohorts with a calm voice,  
And stirring up their minds, greedy for battle,  
He enkindled them with such words as these, hardening them against the enemy:  
"It was in this way, comrades, that glory grew for the Roman state,  
And the republic held dominion over the entire world,  
While taming nations and wars. The Roman people did not fear  
Armed populations, nor did the soldier turn his back in flight,  
Afraid of how great their battle lines might be;  
For he was loyal to his own, highly vigilant with great cunning,  
And powerful in courage to endure the hardships of Mars.  
But as often as loyalty was broken, and no respect for the Emperor  
Conquered minds already preparing themselves for war,  
And plundering and booty pleased them, and the soldier desired  
Military rewards with a mindset hostile to our standards,  
As often as that happened, the Roman force, terrified by its own right hands,  
Turned its back and made wild nations think they had won the day.  
Yet Fortune did not allow long hardships to follow upon these  
Acts of treachery, but brought joy and returned with better favor.  
She loves her own Rome by the gift of the fates.  
For her sake, utterly destroying many guilty men,  
She crushed them and granted her own joys to our affairs.  
What did it profit insane Guntarith to bear the title of tyrant,  
Who lay rolling in death from an Armenian blade?  
Did he not pay the well-deserved penalty for his broken treaty  
In the midst of feasting and the cups of a festive table?  
Why should I mention the fugitive Stutias, wandering  
Through so many parts of the world? While desiring much, while seeking vain goals,  
Wishing to hold the title of tyrant through an unjust war,  
What slaughters did he bring upon Libya? What madness upon nations?  
With how much blood did he stain the sword?  
Though somewhat late, he nevertheless fell, destroyed by a fitting death,  
And reaped his guilt and his punishment all at once.  
You see therefore, men, how hard Fortune labors  
To preserve loyalty to our emperors, and how much, with favorable  
Warfare, she acts to subject the entire world to the Romans.  
Come then, companions in war and loyal citizens,  
Deploy each your own strength into open dangers,  
Dutiful after the Roman fashion, and break the wicked  
Nations. Let conquered peoples and kings recognize  
What courage lives in our state, and what glory resides in our arms.  
  
Section 4.11.  
  
See under how great dangers our standards now waver,  
And be vigilant, men. Our tents are hemmed in  
By Moors on all sides. Safety must be sought through courage and the sword.  
Those whom you believe to be friends joined to our side,  
And whom you think are pacified, are watching to see how this crisis turns out.  
If the Romans are victorious, our foes will serve and adore us;  
Favorable fortune alone, and the fear it conceives, will make them loyal.  
Now, now rise up, citizens!  
This is the victory that will shatter both enemy factions.  
Let these perish by the sword; let our courage terrify those.  
Go, leaders, and station each your own numbers in your assigned positions.  
Let the standards advance with their own companies,  
And lead well-ordered battle lines into the fray.”  
He had spoken, and with a leap he swung himself up  
And settled upon the high back of his horse. The armor of the revered man  
Clanged, and his helmet, gleaming with a fresh splendor,  
Reflected the sun and flashed rays into their eyes,  
While the ruddy glow of his breastplate rippled through the camp.  
Just so does a cloud at the edge of the vaulted sky,  
Stirred by a fresh rumbling as it touches the middle of heaven,  
Thunder and suddenly brandish flames through the mists.  
Then all the leaders and all the tribunes leapt into their saddles.  
Following his example, the cohort leaned upon their stout spears  
And vaulted onto their mounts. Some held back the high  
Necks of their lofty steeds. The swift chargers fought against the reins  
Across the whole plain, bounding right and left through the grass;  
And when they felt their masters' hands upon their necks,  
They rejoiced and strained to run into the wide fields.  
They drew up the battle lines. On the right wing, Gentius  
Commanded the columns, surrounding the standards with chosen companies.  
The master himself rode exultant, his ruddy helmet flashing.  
He was carried by his horse, handsome with his crests and glittering with gold,  
Flying through the midst of the squadrons to kindle the fight,  
A most brave warrior organizing the lines with worthy skill.  
Next to him, the great and high-minded youth Putzintulus  
Moved his arms, drawing dense cohorts behind his standards.  
  
Section 4.12.  
  
The warlike man himself, elevated above the rest by his crested helmet,  
Glittering in his breastplate and more beautiful with his long spear,  
Rode tall upon his horse and according to custom encouraged his companions  
With his voice, since a supreme wisdom was always coupled with a brave  
Heart within him. Happy man, if only the fates had granted him  
Long days of life, so that he might have matured  
Into a hoary old age. How great a man he would have been with greater maturity!  
Third from there, raging with his grasped spear  
And light shield, Gregory shone with Iberian missiles.  
Near him, Geisirith was moving his units and standards,  
Longing to break through the enemy trenches even before the trumpet sounded,  
Fully confident in his own worth. Begirt with flashing arms,  
He carried lofty weapons. He himself shone with his entire body cased in iron:  
Gold and markings adorned his scale armor;  
A golden helmet was there, blazing alongside the tempered iron,  
And from it a horsehair plume formed his crest and peak.  
He fastened his gleaming belt with gem-bearing studs,  
And a sword adorned his side in an ivory scabbard.  
Then on his calves he wore greaves, which Parthian leather  
Had bound with much gold, and he overlaid them with red purple,  
Decorating them with gems and much clever artistry.  
All his standards were impressive, but his excellence and supreme courage were greater still.  
Next, arranging his columns across the wide-stretching plains,  
Rode brave Marturius, superior in his counsels,  
To guide his companions with a tribune's valor.  
Mighty Marcianus was carried into the thick of the enemy,  
Destined to mingle a river of Barcaean blood with his own,  
And also the excellent Senator, born of an illustrious lineage.  
Carried by his horse, he was very gladly beaming in his elegant armor.  
Following these came faithful Cusina, with a dense crowding throng,  
Leading columns under Massylian standards.  
He was a Roman in spirit, nor far from it in blood,  
Adorned with gentle manners and Latin gravity.  
Neither Adonis, beloved of Venus, nor brave Achilles  
Could equal him in throwing javelins or in physical strength.  
But on the other side, on the flashing left wing,  
Rushed that other leader, John, a leader by name as well,  
Who was already an elder and advanced in years. Yet his old age was vigorous  
And his courage was youthful, through which, working with no small sweat,  
The great man was able to shatter the enemy.  
  
Section 4.13.  
  
A piebald steed—black in parts, mottled with white spots,  
Proud with gems and gold, and chafing at the bit—carried him,  
As it galloped swiftly across all the fields.  
Clad in iron himself, he arranged his squadrons in order,  
Flying back and forth, reassuring his comrades as they advanced.  
Nearby, active Fronimuth moved his columns with upright standards,  
Bringing them into line to join forces on the open fields.  
Although he stood out in their midst, gleaming in his crests and helmet,  
And glittering with the fresh splendor of iron  
As he rode tall, catching and deflecting the rays of the opposing sun,  
Yet dense battle lines surrounded him on all sides,  
Walled in by flashing shields and helmets.  
Next from there, Marcentius urged and summoned his units,  
And his columns were deployed at his command across the wide fields.  
A golden helmet, stiff with bronze and heavy with crests, pressed upon his blonde  
Hair, and a breastplate, protecting his formidable shoulders,  
Encircled him with gleaming scales,  
While his steed moved its legs with proud strides.  
He carried weapons at his waist, girt with a quiver and a sounding bow,  
Bringing bronze-tipped arrows into the battle,  
And he fortified his standards, surrounded by terrifying tribunes.  
On this side was Liberatus, outstanding with his long spear;  
On that side handsome Ulitan, adorned in varied armor,  
Excellent with the javelin, and no less swift with the curved bow.  
Well-taught to guide his horn-hoofed mount with gentle reins  
Rode Ifisdaias, the leader and instigator of his people,  
Whom a fierce nation followed, accompanied by his son Bitipten.  
This nation was numerous in men, and the courage of the people was lively;  
The leader himself, strong in intellect, was loyal,  
Pleasing to the military, the generals, and the great master.  
His exceptional son was even bolder now that he had taken up arms.  
He was no less a man than his father, able to surpass the supple arrows of the Persians  
With javelins loosed from the bowstring.  
In the van, the leader Tarasis arranged the intermingled infantry lines  
In their varied armor, raising the lofty standards.  
He was carried on horseback through the slanted squadrons, directing the battle  
And ordering his men to lock their shields together.  
Bristling with interlinked shield-bosses, the densely packed line of Mars  
Stretched far out across the wide fields.  
  
Section 4.14.  
  
Like walls, their bodies were hidden behind dense  
Shields. Only their battle-axes appeared behind the bucklers  
And the tops of their helmets, gleaming with crests and peaks.  
Above them, the field bristled like a forest of upright iron spears,  
And the air flashed with fresh terrors.  
In this manner father John himself, powerful in arms, arranged the wings of his battle,  
Disposing the standards in the center, guiding everything in order,  
And balancing the squadrons across the columns.  
He rode well in their midst on his horse, so that he might fight with the full weight of his arms  
And be a brave director to his columns, moving the weapons of his people.  
Just as the watchful eyes of a fighting bull rove cautiously  
Between its straining horns, as it prepares where to strike its warring enemy,  
Pressing forward on one side, wheeling right and threatening left,  
Redoubling blows with varied lunges of its horn,  
So that where its eyes command, its horns drive home the wound,  
Even so did the leader, having weighed his parts equally,  
Order his lines to enter the battle. And just so are the working pipes  
And strings of a lyre, skillfully ordered for the striking fingers of a master.  
The pipe sounds with the touch of the wind he activates;  
Neither strings nor bronzes cry out, unless the song sounds forth at the ruler's will,  
Pressed out from the struck reeds.  
Not otherwise was the entire line moved at the command of its leader,  
Rushing together with its standards separated in perfect order.  
Joined to his side was Ricinarius, gleaming in illustrious armor  
And organizing the columns with equal devotion,  
He himself possessing a mature mind, modest in his gravity,  
High-minded, gentle, wise, most brave, innocent,  
Powerful in arms, a master of war and a servant of peace,  
Humble of heart—which Christ loves—and kindly in his piety,  
A man who was truly worthy to advance alongside such a master.  
For though not related by birth, a pure love, piety, goodness,  
Wisdom, and virtue linked the two men as a single soul in two bodies,  
As a sign of supreme favor.  
Therefore, illustrious in his armor amid the central lines,  
He rode his horse and encouraged his companions with calm speech.  
The hostile army marveled at the brave John ruling his successful  
Standards by this very formation.  
But Ierna, the chief planner of the wars, during the nighttime  
Had anxiously built walls of camels throughout his camp,  
Enclosing the field in an eightfold line.  
  
Section 4.15.  
  
Next he joined oxen together, binding horn to horn with skill  
In a sixfold circle, and being clever with deep cunning,  
He prepared deceptive traps through winding paths,  
So that the Moor might safely dash out from behind them during the battle  
And crush the enemy force as it advanced into the camp,  
Unaware of the traps hidden within the shapes of these ambushes.  
Not even the work of Minos in the Labyrinths of Crete  
Had wound its uncertain paths with such great twists,  
Distorting the ways in a maze—ways which the son of Aegeus found,  
Clever in his art. For he retraced his steps using a stretched thread,  
Having been forewarned; he then struck the double-formed breast  
Of the mixed hybrid with his sword, and the monster spewed blood  
And collapsed, breaking its horns upon the dark ground.  
Forming a third line of defense for the tightly bound rampart,  
He encircled the camp, scattering and chaining up smaller livestock  
To create an obstacle. He secured the central spaces tightly with closely packed  
Donkeys, and using skill, he stretched out treacherous ropes,  
And placed caltrops and two-pronged forks throughout the camp,  
Sharpening stakes and placing large rocks as barriers.  
Antalas likewise laid out his battle plan with skill  
And marched out, flanked by his own wings. Relying on his horse,  
He burned to enter the center, and for the infantry battle  
He drew up his phalanxes with their solid shields locked together.  
Yet he kept his columns closer to the fortified camp,  
Not wishing his foot soldiers to engage in grim combat  
Too far away, having learned so many times what terror in arms  
And Roman courage can accomplish. Nevertheless, in a reliable formation,  
The Moorish army advanced under two standards.  
Galloping ahead, the cavalry overran all the fields,  
And the nimble Laguatan chief advanced. His people filled the plains, the nearby hills,  
The hollow valleys, the woods, and the rivers.  
He was accompanied by fierce Ierna and proud Bruten.  
Bruten was second in command. Camalus, following them next,  
Burned to charge the opposing enemy with many thousands,  
Along with fierce Hisdreasen, bold Ialdas, and raging Sinzera—  
Names that no one could hold in mind or try to recount,  
Even if wishing to name so many savage nations.  
In their midst, the fuel and director of the war, was sharp  
Sidifan, who commanded the right-wing cavalry and standards.  
Meanwhile, linking his columns on the left wing, Carcasan  
Poured his densely packed lines over all the fields,  
Moving the Ifuraces into battle. Likewise, Melangus  
Ruled a standard, along with Gantal, Guentan, Alacanza, dreadful Iutungun,  
Swift Autiliten, and brave Catubar,  
And the thousand other leaders whom the Syrtes sent into battle.

## Book Five.  

Section 5.0.  
  
And now the opposing armies were closing in on one another  
And drawing up their standards. From their quivers they plucked  
Fleet arrows and bows, and brandished their missile iron.  
The opposing forces had closed the gap of empty plain between them,  
And the enemy line blocked the men from advancing any further;  
Standing firm in their positions, they forced the oncoming enemy  
To halt, and the columns ground to a standstill with reined-in horses.  
Looking out across the lines, Gunfarius spotted the enemy leader  
John in the midst of his standards, and recognized him  
From afar. Charging through the weapons of his own men, he spurred  
His towering steed forward and dashed right before the eyes  
Of the great general. Yet that bravest of leaders advanced to meet the foe  
And clashed with him in open combat.  
Antalas turned in flight, wheeling his broken steed around by the reins  
To flee backward. The general pursued him, shouting:  
"Where are you running, Antalas? Are these your boasting words?  
Look, John comes to call you out with only a small band of soldiers!  
Where are you turning your swift course? Let Him judge us now,  
Who moves heaven and earth, and hurls the thunderbolts!"  
As Gunfarius shouted these taunts and called him to battle,  
Antalas blushed, and in his shame hid himself among his own troops.  
Then, a bull was released through magic arts  
From the midst of the Moors—a bull that the priest Ierna,  
Who was also the greatest counselor to the chieftains of his people,  
Had fashioned to embody the divine power of Ammonian Gurzil,  
As a primary omen for his men. Wildly tossing its high horns,  
It raged between both armies, unsure which enemy line to break.  
While the columns trembled, it burst through the paths of the Syrtis,  
Charging forward, and then fiercely sought its own camp once more.  
A Roman knight pursued it, and hurling his trembling javelin  
Right into its flank, laid it low in the middle of the sands.  
The horns sounded the call to battle with a raucous blare;  
A fresh cry then rose to the stars in shattered voices,  
And the Fury raged with deafening shrieks.  
The whole forest rang, and every echo harmonized with the tribes,  
Mimicking and throwing back their various languages.  
On this side, the Moorish lines shouted, calling upon Sinifere  
And fierce Mastiman; and "Mastiman!" the echo resounded.  
On the other, they cried "Gurzil!"; and "Gurzil!" the hollow rocks echoed back.  
  
Section 5.1.  
  
On this side, the Roman host thundered, disturbing the heavens with their voices,  
And the mountains groaned back as quivers clashed.  
A revered voice sang out; they shouted: "May Christ in His divinity,  
O Justinian, fight most mightily alongside your arms!  
Preserve the empire of our prince, O best of Fathers!"  
At that name the heavens trembled, and groaning, they shook;  
As the earth shuddered, the forests on the mountain ridges and peaks,  
Obeying the shock, seemed to quiver; the mountains and lakes  
Uttered a raucous groan; the world trembled, loosed from its framework,  
And the elements confessed their Creator with their tongues.  
From this, their fury grew, and their spirits warmed for war.  
The men urged on their horses, showering dense clouds of weapons  
From their arms, and the sunlight turned pale beneath the thick spears.  
A dark night rushed in under the javelins, casting shadows over the plains,  
And the iron of both armies cut off the light of day above the weapons.  
Oncoming weapons thickened with twirling arrows  
Flying from either side. Then the whole sky turned more grim  
And dark beneath so many spears;  
For as many as they hurled, just as many wounds did both sides  
Receive. Every oncoming weapon threatened death,  
But a different fortune ruled them. For a hurled missile,  
Oftentimes became damaged by an opposing spear as it sped along,  
And they fell to the field locked together by their twin weights;  
And so Mars lost two potential wounds snatched away at once.  
Yet in other cases, death itself blushed red. Then the earth grew wet,  
Soaked with mingled blood. The air hissed with the flying spears.  
Mars raged, and in the midst of the slaughter, he rolled together  
Mangled men and lifeless horses. Ruthless valor clashed  
As they rushed from opposite sides, and men, blinded by the tumult  
And fury, threw their chests against the oncoming weapons.  
Those who were preparing to bring death to others received the iron  
Into their own vitals, and poured out their sweet lives through their wounds.  
Fierce Ricinarius, bursting through the resisting enemy,  
Rushed forward, and after slaying Eilimaras, put the opposing lines and standards to flight.  
He, relying on his horse and trusting in his men, was the first to mingle in battle,  
Daring to attack and seek out the Roman lines. But the greatest hero  
Cut him off: piercing the chest of the charging man with his leveled weapon,  
He shattered his ribs and ruptured his vital organs,  
And the spear ran through his back with great force.  
  
Section 5.2.  
  
Fleeing in icy terror, the Mazax wheeled his horse around.  
The Roman host pursued him, throwing his men into confusion by pressing hard;  
Rushing across the wide plains, they overtook the Moorish rear guard,  
And as the enemy fled, they wore them down with easy slaughter.  
When the broken horseman saw his infantry allies standing ready at the front,  
Valor returned to his breast, and turning his steed,  
He charged, filling the plain with fierce shouts,  
And sought a path into the midst of the opposing soldiers' swords.  
But with all his might, the leader stood in the way,  
And encouraging his men, he exhorted them with friendly words:  
"Roman nobles, the support of our state and of ourselves,  
You who have so often been tested in enduring the hardships of war,  
You desired battle: at last, the opportunity for battle is given.  
The whole cause now wavers in the peril of a single moment.  
In this brief span, drive back the enemy with your utmost strength.  
This will be the only labor by which clear victory  
Is granted to our side. We bring the highest honors  
To the soldiers who tame wars, crush proud nations,  
And renew the joys of Roman fortunes."  
He spoke, and rapidly bursting into the midst of the column,  
He spurred his horse and went raging through the enemy with a loud voice.  
The bravest battle line followed close behind with dense showers of weapons.  
Everything was hidden in dust: but an iron rain gathered  
Upon the plains, and flying arrows were sped from bowstrings.  
The leader first sent Mantisynas to the shades of the dead,  
Taking off his head with his sword. The man's massive neck  
Did not even feel the bitter wound, nor was the swift sword slowed by the bone  
Before it was stained with blood. His head lay upon the grass  
With wide-open eyes. Carrying the trunk across the plain,  
His horse ran lightly, and blood spurted from where the neck had been severed.  
Next, he struck Laumasas through the temples with his strong blade,  
And through the shattered bones, the sword passed straight through the soft brain,  
Cutting through both helmet and cloak,  
And slicing his eyes and long hair along with his brow.  
And hurling his spear at close range, he brought down the swift horse  
Of Guarsutias. The ash wood quivered where it pierced beneath the left shoulder,  
And as the hot iron ran through the vitals,  
It hung there, pinned right through the master's right foot.  
The steed collapsed from the wound, and by its own fall  
Pinned its master beneath it, crushing him with its lethal weight.  
  
Section 5.3.  
  
Split right down the middle by the rigid blade, the terrifying Manzerases  
Fell apart, his body collapsing to either side.  
Lashing out at Iartas, John severed his throat  
And his armed hand; the hand fell away, still gripping the iron,  
While he collapsed in death's cold embrace.  
A tremor of the earth shook him as he struck,  
Twitching his dying arms through their warm sinews.  
Catching sight of John raging through the slaughter, Mazanas  
Rushed to meet him, drawing back his arm to try his spear;  
He hurled his missile as he advanced upon the general,  
Then wheeled his broken steed around. But the brave leader,  
Fearing no wounds, and deflecting the straining spear with his shield,  
Took the blow, and undeterred, charged the enemy himself,  
Striking him without fear. The blood spurted out, staining the green grass,  
And his massive corpse lay stretched across the countryside.  
Gardius had seen his great brother struck down by the blow,  
And flew forward to avenge his brother's destruction.  
Behold him flying, protected by his shield and proud in his weapons,  
Pursuing the leader. He was driven by conscious valor  
And grief for his brother. But then, bending his knee in a dodging movement,  
He leapt up, and a quivering spear caught him in his ribs,  
Its point passing straight through his armor, and he fell pinned to the sand.

"Was this what you sought from us with your courage, unhappy man,  
In your ignorance? Or were you preparing instead to go as a companion to your brother?  
Behold, your wish has been granted. You are linked to your brother's fate,  
You stubborn fool; seeking both plunder and death at once,

And they repent of their wickedness. They have realized that war  
Is to be avoided at all costs. They curse both their fates and their gods.  
Nor was there just one color for all the captives.  
One sits there, rough and of the same dark hue as her black children,  
Just as you might see the black fledglings of crows growing dark  
While the mother sits over them, offering them their accustomed food  
With open beak, and protecting and embracing them with outspread wings.  
While it pleases the fathers and mothers to show their terrifying faces  
To their little children, the great-hearted commander, entering the threshold of the temple  
With his standards accompanying him,  
Prayed to the Lord of heaven, earth, and sea,  
And offered a gift, which the high priest, according to custom,  
Placed upon the altar for the return of the commander and the vanquished foes,  
And the father consecrated the offerings to Christ.  
Meanwhile, the chieftain of the Syrtis, Carcasan,  
Gathered his columns from all the borders where vast terror had scattered them,  
And spoke to the grim ranks with tears welling up:  
"I did not hope to see the Moors so cast down,  
O unconquered nations. The never-defeated Laguatans  
Are present here stripped of everything, and return vanquished. We have lost  
Mothers, daughters-in-law, and children all at once. What finally remains  
For men but death alone? What pleases you now? To be at peace?  
Or does it please you better to challenge with wars and weapons the arms  
Of the harsh soldier? It is a shameful and wretched crime  
To have given up once we were broken. Not all the aid of the gods  
Departed and was lost on those fields.  
  
Section 6.3.  
  
Ammon does not wish it so, nor does he who now grieves that the divinity  
Of Gurzil has been so violated; nor does fortune threaten us so,  
Since she wished to save our men. We have lost only  
Our livestock, for our strength remains. Discern how few  
Of our men have fallen. It is as if a jar should draw waves  
From the waters of the Ocean: could Thetis ever decrease  
Or feel the loss? Even though so many stars fall when their fire is spent,  
Yet the full sky always holds all its constellations with their signs.  
Just so did misfortune touch our nation,  
Yet it did not entirely feel the loss of its bravest men.  
Take counsel, and swiftly bring aid to your affairs."  
When Bruten perceived the grievances uttered from his mouth,  
Fierce in mind, he seized upon war. "Excellent father," he said,  
"By redoubling the war through our labor, you can win back  
Our wives and children, for it pleases us to pour out our lives  
And end them in battle. What reputation will our nation have  
Among all peoples, if the outrage of our slaughter  
Should be reported throughout the wide world, remaining unavenged?  
It would be better if death should snatch our peoples away, swallowed up  
By a sudden opening of the earth, or if Tartarus should open wide its jaws,  
Along with the paleness of the black house, and if helpless Proserpina  
Should hold the kingdom of her father through the wars of her dark husband.  
You have battle lines, you have weapons. Rise, seize upon war.  
Trusting in your leadership, I will lay down my life without reproach.  
This is our certain salvation. You are the glory of our nation,  
You the honor of our valor, you the most faithful hope for the Moors."  
Hardly had Bruten said these words when all followed with a shout,  
And roared for Carcasan, declaring with one tongue and mind  
That Carcasan alone was the leader for all nations.  
When he saw that the fury conceived by his people had grown,  
And that the madness of insane war was mounting,  
He sought from there the borders of the Marmaridae, where horned Ammon dwells,  
And demanded the responses of harsh Jupiter.  
Always this deceiver, Jupiter, loves to trick wretched minds—  
He whom you ask in vain. Dreadful is he, rejoicing in blood,  
And seeking to destroy all nations.  
When a fierce bull, struck on its opposing forehead by a double-axe,  
Fell dying, the most grim priestess snatched the hoarse drums  
In her hands and, driven mad, whirled in leaps around the altars  
With a clamor. Her neck rose high,  
Her eyes flashed with fire, the hair rose upon her forehead,  
And her face, bearing witness to the god, grew red with fervor.  
Now she stained her cheeks with paleness, now she rolled her eyes,  
Now, roaring from deep within, she tossed her head as she gathered her savage fires.  
  
Section 6.4.  
  
When she truly and entirely received the divinity into her breast,  
She looked up at the high moon in the night-time,  
Glowing with bloody light, and scrutinized the fates as she reviewed them.  
She burned, panted, gasped, paled, reddened, seethed, and froze,  
While she sought the prophetic path. At last, an unholy voice  
From her wild mouth brought forth the secrets of the fates into the air:  
"The victor of the Laguatans, driving them in bitter war,  
Will throw the Latins into confusion. For all time the Mazax,  
With great valor, shall hold the plains of Byzacium.  
Then there will be nurturing rest. Entering the high citadels of Carthage  
Through open gates, the leader Carcasan,  
Exalted and peaceful, shall be carried with a accompanying people  
Through the middle of the city. The African will look in wonder at his terrifying  
Face. They will run bearing laurels and palms  
At his arrival. Happy Carthage will be spoken of  
Among all peoples. Carcasan, a terror, will subject  
Bitter nations, and they will love the treaties of peace."  
While the priestess sang these things, the disturbing spirit blocked  
Her mouth and deceived their wretched minds with delusion.  
With these frauds the deceitful Ammon tricked the Massylian  
Nations. While singing true things, he clothed them in darkness  
And prepared a trap. For the Moors indeed held the fields of Byzacium  
For all time, and will always hold them  
With their own bones, which now lie broken by the valor of great John.  
The leader Carcasan did indeed go through the midst of the high citadels  
Of Carthage, exalted, the people accompanying him.  
For at that time, Africa saw his head, severed at the neck,  
Fixed upon a rigid pike.  
Devoid of reason, trusting too much in the hostile responses,  
He prepared dreadful wars. Rumor ran racing through all  
Peoples, singing that Ammon had granted the kingdom to the tribes.  
Swift squadrons charged out from the hot Syrtes,  
Inviting wild nations under the illusion of a kingdom.  
The barbarian crowd gathered: it grew in numbers and in arms.  
Then horsemen and infantry rushed forward, and those who sit,  
After the custom of the Moors, upon tall camels. Nor did the Laguastan act alone.  
Not alone did the nations who had waged the earlier wars  
Gather together, but if any fierce Nasamon plows the Syrtic lands,  
Or if anyone near the fields of Garamantis cultivates the earth,  
Or if anyone drinks the stagnant waters at the margin of the rich Nile,  
They came. Who could name the nations  
Or number them? You might as well number the waves of the sea,  
Or the drops of rainstorms, or how many grains of sand the shore  
Washes, or how many fish the sea contains, or how many birds the whole earth  
Holds, or how many ears of grain the varied plain brings forth  
In early spring, or how many stars adorn the entire sky.  
  
Section 6.5.  
  
Thinking of his own strength, the leader of the wars,  
Believing himself safe and strong, renewed his idols  
And standards, and set his battle lines in motion from the furthest shores.  
Roaring with more than his accustomed valor, he advanced against the enemy.  
Just as Antaeus, when often conquered by the arms of Hercules,  
Repaired his weary body by touching the sand,  
Falling on purpose, until the victorious Tirynthian discovered the trick  
And, embracing the enemy with his unmatched strength,  
Held up his leaning body with its massive weight  
While pressing his cruel throat—for he could not touch Mother Earth—  
Til a victorious death closed the wretched man's eyes,  
Even so the vanquished Carcasan renews all his strength  
From his own Syrtes, and so, doomed to die in his ignorance,  
He prepares weapons against the foe. Then the terror of war  
And the cruel dangers of the dark night left their breasts.  
Look, a swift messenger flies by, at the command of great Rufinus,  
And terrifies with his rumor the Libyan cities  
That were now at peace, saying that the conquered bands were fighting again,  
That squadrons of horsemen were already racing through the western borders  
And devastating the homes of Tripoli, and showing that the wild nations  
Were marching toward the walls of high Carthage with Carcasan as leader,  
And that he was promising himself the name of a kingdom.  
The soldier who was sent out had barely reached the Phoenician palace  
To bring the messages to the commander. A bitter fury  
Shook his heart as the man told the tale. But better wisdom,  
Overcoming the indomitable courage of his noble mind,  
Sought counsel. Cares rolled through his breast,  
And his gaze did not waver. The sharpness of his mind raced along,  
Foreseeing everything: he weighed the causes in this crisis  
And saw unspeakable danger on every side.  
Then, calling the nobles together according to custom, he asked for their advice,  
And with these words disclosed his thoughts and anxieties:  
"The conquered Laguatans, doomed to be conquered again, move for war once more,  
O comrades, daring to clash with standards that have tested them.  
  
Section 6.6.  
  
Here, the rebel already presses hard upon the lands of Tripoli and plunders  
Spoils, promising to advance into our borders.  
I am prepared to advance our standards, to go out to meet so many nations,  
Desiring to join battle in external lands  
And to overthrow the enemy far away from our countryside,  
Lest Africa, shaken again by savage ruins,  
Should perish further. The expenses, the terrain,  
And the difficult roads trouble my mind: for the year is seen to be barren.  
The province itself has lost its strength through wars.  
Alas, it lied down all too exhausted. A massive army  
Will not be able to endure hunger. If we allow the enemy  
To touch even the furthest borders of Byzacium,  
They will soon wish to hasten forward. They will destroy everything with plunder,  
And battles will once more throw the weary land into turmoil.  
Take counsel, and strengthen my doubtful mind by your instruction."  
Scarcely could the commander finish this speech. It pleased all the people to go  
Further. They declared that they could bear the very heats of western  
Libya, and the entire crowd promised their hands  
And fierce minds. For they wished to brave great hardships  
For the fatherland, and they despised the rebel nations  
With their grim spirits, and faced the battle lines with fierce valor.  
When the commander saw the battle lines aroused with this fierce valor,  
And that the men promiswd themselves without fear to the wars,  
He ordered the standards to be advanced. Then the trumpet groaned  
Its hoarse songs from the brass. The savage voice summoned the armed bands.  
All the squadrons gathered from their own stations,  
Along with the infantry as ordered, and the Latin companions and leaders,  
And the chieftain Cusina, drawing Massylian ranks into the wars,  
Who was always most faithful to Roman arms.  
The bravest commander advanced toward the southern pole,  
Where the burning day holds parched places under the constellation of Cancer,  
And where men always labor, ever scorched by the sun.  
The fields glow with thirst and western winds. For there the South wind  
Dries up all the countryside with flame-vomiting blasts.  
There also the panting, destitute Africans wander over the hot sands,  
Thirsting, ever searching for Stygian waters.  
Rumor, scattering proclamations through innumerable tongues,  
Flies ahead, reporting that brave John was hastening forward  
With all his leaders. The wicked news made its way  
To the ears of the Laguatan nation. Already the unjust plundering  
Horseman was devastating the borders of Byzacium. But the rumor,  
Striking their breasts with the great valor of John's name,  
Terrified them, and turned their innumerable battle lines backward.  
  
Section 6.7.  
  
Driven by terror, they now believe the commander is upon them  
And tremble, having felt his power before; they recall the man's fierce look  
And all his standards. They do not hesitate to cross the parched Gadaias  
And those grim places, where there is no way to travel  
Or to survive. In those territories no bird passes,  
Suspended through the burning air.  
Even the armor-bearer of Jupiter himself, who carries the fires of the thunderbolt,  
Can scarcely endure unhurt the blasts at the margin of the burning sky,  
Which terror has forced them to approach.  
When the commander realized that the enemy bands had retreated  
Through the deserts out of fear, he pursued the fugitives with his customary valor,  
Advancing more fiercely over the hot sands of the thirsting earth.  
Yet he ordered all his men to carry water  
And grain for themselves. The swift soldiers fulfilled  
The instructions of the commander. But how much could suffice in those  
Places to satisfy so many peoples, and for how many days  
Could he feed such vast columns? The waterskins were emptied of their spring water,  
And soon the grain was nowhere to be found. Then they burned with dry throats  
And collapsed from hunger. Alas, the soldiers raged, panting,  
And seething, red from the heat of the sun, burning  
With immense fires. But finding no river in any of the sands,  
They searched for water and labored in vain.  
It was like how, once, the glorious columns of the Greeks, while they were seeking  
The Theban plains, dreaded the lakes and springs dried up by the power of Bacchus,  
And their thirsting leader Adrastus sought rivers in the wide fields.  
Then, breaking into wretched complaints, a bitter soldier  
Said with a bursting voice: "If grim fates threaten  
To destroy the Roman nation all at once at this moment,  
We have swords, we have wars, the tribes are wild with wrath.  
Let the lance pierce us, or let all weapons come together  
Like a thunderbolt. Let the hurled iron pass through  
Our entrails and pour out our lives through bare wounds.  
Why do dreadful hunger, binding heat, and thirst constrain us  
To sluggish fates? Alas, they exhaust us with so lingering a death!  
Let our hands be dedicated to swords. Turn back  
The standards. This host, starving with hunger, begs you, O highest commander,  
All together. Mercifully have pity on us and on yourself.  
  
Section 6.8.  
  
Look upon your men, great leader. Emaciation now shrinks our limbs  
And our bare bones stiffen, wasted away, their marrow dried up.  
Our muscles are tightened, our skin is parched, and with sunken eyes  
Our cheeks are stained with paleness. Already the image of death  
Holds our bodies, and our thirsting spirit burns."  
Scarcely could the helpless crowd speak these things. The father himself restrained the grieving  
Peoples, and, good as he was, refreshed the exhausted men with his speech,  
Admonishing them often with these words: "O Roman hope and glory  
And salvation of the fatherland, do not despise these immense labors.  
Conquer thirst and dreadful hunger. Remember now the hardships  
Endured by our fathers. Nations have felt the strength of our ancestors.  
Thus did our fathers subdue the world by their own virtues,  
While enduring bitter things. Patience is the highest virtue.  
The tribes fear it. It terrifies our enemies,  
While it destroys them. The hostile line suffers from twin  
Destructions: on one side parching thirst, heat, and hunger  
Torment them; on the other, Roman terror drives them and presses hard.  
Fiercely essay the path of the sun and the forbidden regions.  
The glowing zone will report your witnessed labors,  
With the heavens confirming them, and posterity will read that I, second  
To great Cato, essayed this path, and that men have written of it.  
Let love of the fatherland conquer your mind, and let this relentless thirst cease,  
Driven away by holy divine power, and your burning ardor shall be satisfied."  
Thus the excellent commander soothed the Latin cohorts with his calm voice,  
As if watering all their hearts with a sweet river of words, or filling their bellies with a feast.  
Then a sad fortune befell the Latin affairs,  
Which shattered their great strength, and shook their vigor.  
The horses, seeking forage across all the plains,  
Wandered. For a dreadful hunger and burning heat possessed  
Every kind of beast. There was no green grass, no tree  
With any leaves. Then suddenly the plain grew pale with thick stalks,  
And the opposing fields reddened with flowers.  
Discerning from afar the longed-for herbs, the desperate herd  
Driven by dreadful hunger, ran scattered and cropped  
Every place far and wide. Soon they licked the bare sands.  
The horses, unable to satisfy their hunger, now pastured on poisonous grasses,  
And the dense herd fell upon those very fields  
By a strange death, with the grass still pressed between their cold teeth.  
By the death of their steeds were the Roman youths broken.  
  
Section 6.9.  
  
Their fierce rage collapsed. Every soldier went on sad  
And anxious, and massive cares threw their hearts into turmoil.  
By this single disaster, the great valor of the men collapsed,  
And they waited in utter terror. This was the first time harmful fortune  
Disturbed the camp with a sudden tumult. It crushed them with this weight.  
When the commander saw that so great a ruin had occurred  
And had diminished the strength of his men, he moved the camp  
And advanced toward the shores of the coast, desiring to relieve his grief  
And restore his men. And soon the soldiers felt a gentler air  
And, drawing near, found life-giving herbs,  
Yet no rivers. Then, with dry throats, they pressed  
The flowers themselves and stained their burning mouths with their fresh  
Juices. They sated their hunger with a feast of green,  
Yet they could not endure it. Seizing the opportunity of the night,  
Innumerable men retreated. Others scattered through the fields  
While they sought forage. Others wandered in search of springs.  
Savage hunger forced others to turn their backs,  
And they abandoned all the standards of their disregarded commander.  
The leader pitched camp by the waves of a notable river.  
The thirsting Roman youths flung themselves upon the banks  
And found comfort in the clear waters. From all sides the soldiers  
Rushed to the streams and drank the sweet waters.  
Destitute of bread, they cropped the flowers and green herbs.  
With these they desired to satisfy their hunger. The leader ordered commanders  
To go throughout the coastal cities to bring down ships  
Carrying provisions for his men. Oh, bitter fates!  
They had opposing South winds. Harsh fortune denied  
The ships permission to cross the sea's waves with their sails.  
The nearby Astrician band joined itself to the Latins,  
But was then unfaithful, thus adding to the Roman fate.  
For a long time now in those lands, the Astricians  
Held their rugged homes. They were a nation harsh in wars,  
Numerous in men, and unharmed through many years.  
When this nation perceived that the standards of oncoming John  
Were within their borders, they were terrified by his first approach,  
And humbly ran to send legates for peace.  
The calm commander received them in the middle of his tents.  
  
Section 6.10.  
  
Asking for pardon, peace, and safety for their nation,  
They poured forth prayers submissively: "O greatest ruler, your powerful fame  
And your vigor of mind, valor, and fidelity  
Have flown ahead to terrify all nations equally  
And draw them to your laws voluntarily. With bowed neck,  
The famous nation of the Astricians submits itself, bravest leader, to your commands.  
The elders of our nation love and likewise desire your treaties. Wishing to serve,  
They prepare their necks for the yoke. Spare those who ask, glorious one.  
We pray for peace and calm rest after wars."  
Amidst these voices, the murmurings of the ignorant common soldiers  
Sounded in the camp of the commander: "How much longer will unavenged hunger  
Kill our columns? There is no hope of life or safety.  
We, a wretched band, are being wasted away." When that sound  
Reached the ears of the commander, deeply moved, he spoke thus into the ear  
Of Ricinarius: "Quickly restrain the unfortunate complaints  
Of the cowardly soldiers. What madness of the heart  
Hurries the wretched bands forward with a confused mind?  
They see the legates. The nations are begging for my treaties.  
They stand humble, begging us with prayers. Yet these men of ours reveal our  
Inmost thoughts and secret misfortune. Oh, most wretched crowd, serving the belly  
In the manner of cattle or the custom of wild beasts!"  
Immediately going out, he swiftly suppressed all the murmurings  
With calm words, delivering the instructions of the commander.  
After quiet was made, the commander himself, more resolute,  
Said to the legates: "You have heard what rages our army  
Has just poured out. The soldier desires bitter battles.  
He prepares to march through your nation. But our authority  
Always strives to spare the submissive. Those who hold weapons,  
We crush. The humble, we adopt as friends.  
Go, men. If certain fidelity asks for my treaties,  
Deliver the pledges of your children to my camp  
And keep my peace. The whole Astrician race shall remain  
Secure and flourishing under our emperor."  
Having spoken these things, he loaded them with gifts. They declares their service  
To the Roman empire, having promised to offer their children  
As a pledge of peace. They approved of the Latins for their valor  
And piety. They praised both the strength and fidelity  
Of the emperor and the commander. With a peaceful arrangement made, they departed.  
  
Section 6.11.  
  
But far off in the barren plains, the exhausted Laguatans  
Wandered thirsting, nor could they any longer bear such great  
Labors and dreadful hunger. No way of safety  
And no path appeared to them. Behind their backs was John,  
Before their faces the excessive heat of the sun. On all sides, the nation's  
Death was before their eyes. They could neither advance straight ahead,  
Nor retrace their steps. Confounded by dangers,  
The anxious crowd groaned and prayed for death. Harmful fortune  
Persuaded them to turn backward, and diverted the unpromising  
Journey of the unholy nation. The relentless Syrtic band returned  
To the lot of war, not demanding battles with weapons,  
But seeking to attempt a doubtful flight. Everywhere the Roman scout  
Was racing, mounted on his horse, and, mixed with the Latins,  
The faithful Mazax was there, likewise inquiring where the tracks  
Of the nation might be. Neither rumor nor any  
Enemy was nearby, when suddenly in the foul night  
They saw burning campfires, and in doubt they weighed  
Which one has come back: the Astricians or the Laguatans.  
From the waves of the Ocean, a sadder Aurora, raising her dark steeds,  
Stretched her rays toward the pole, dragging a mournful Titan,  
And the horses of the Sun bore unholy fates. Clouds hid their courses,  
And Phoebus obscured the day with a dismal light.  
Behold, a rapid messenger reported that beneath the silent night  
He had seen far off, wide from the trenches of his own men,  
Innumerable campfires gleaming. Perhaps the exhausted Laguatans,  
Having turned around, were returning behind their backs, or perhaps the nearby  
Nation of the Astricians were pitching camp in those places. The voice,  
In making this report, was not sure. While the skillful commander,  
Pressed for time, inquired in his heart into the cause of this,  
And was perplexed with many doubts, behold, suddenly arriving from above,  
Faithful Cusina was present with a large surrounding band,  
And joyfully spoke to the leader thusly: "The fugitive Laguatans  
Have prepared to cross over here stealthily. They are weakened and unarmed,  
And their camp moves in exhaustion. The time has come—seize the standards,  
Greatest of commanders. Destroy this weary nation now.  
For the labor of warfare is easy. There is a shaded river  
Which washes its shaded banks in a green oasis,  
Enclosed by various trees and gray reeds.  
The nations reach for this spot. Let us be the first to come to the waves  
And let us hold all the waters." The words of the speaking  
Man pleased the people. Yet the commander forbide them to go,  
Restraining for a long time now the murmurs of the exhausted common soldiers  
In his anxious heart. But who could overcome the certain commands  
Of the bidding God, or stand against them?  
Advancing the camp, the commander ordered the arrayed cohorts to advance  
By their companies and squadrons. The dust went up into the height,  
Gathered into clouds, and the sand threw the sky into turmoil.  
  
Section 6.12.  
  
The men began an unspeakable journey, and the grim Gallic region  
Showed them spiteful hills and unpromising fields.  
The titan, ascending, inflamed high Olympus  
With his fire-bearing chariots, when opposing battle lines  
Occupied the middle channel of the river. But the Syrtic enemy  
Restrained their step out of terror and, turning backward,  
Deserted the banks and left behind the longed-for streams.  
Here the commander ordered the trenches and camp to be placed  
And to defer the war with a delay. Preparing to move the battle  
On the following day, he ordered them only to defend the flowing rivers  
And the streams with weapons in hand.  
A useful counsel, if only the Roman host had now fulfilled  
The instructions of their bidding leader. But a wretched fate  
Granted the men to be over-bold, and everywhere the youths  
Rushed scattered through the fields, and the first men challenged  
The first columns of the enemy. They clashed in no order,  
The Latins not arrayed in a battle line, but shouting their accustomed cry.  
No trumpet sounded the martial tumults with its blare  
At the command of the leader, nor did they set the steep  
Banners of battle in their proper places. Scattered through the enemy,  
Alas, the host, blindly confident, ran into a hostile fate.  
Terrified at the first clash, the columns of the Marmaridae  
Retreated backward. The Roman youths followed,  
Intermingling bitter battles with thick weapons.  
The Latin horsemen joined the struggle against the bodies of the fugitives  
With spears across the plains, and transfixed and pressed them hard,  
And fear forced the vanquished to go amidst their camels.  
But far off, the powerful-in-arms John was fortifying the standards,  
Arranging everything in its proper order, and, commanding the trenches  
And camp to be placed, for the sake of preserving only the river's wave,  
He ordered the leaders of the young men to drive off the enemy.  
They composed the lines. The man who held the right flank  
Was Cusina, surrounded by Massylian and Latin weapons.  
Fronimuth was next to him, brave in Roman arms.  
Also there was that powerful leader, adorned with the happy name  
Of the commander, John—but with a less happy fortune.  
  
Section 6.13.  
  
The left flank was held by the great Putzintulus,  
The bowman Geisirith, and Sinduit, exalted in his arms.  
The supreme leader of the commanders himself stood  
In the middle position, and warned his allies with speech in vain,  
As a heavy fate was already pressing upon them. Before him Tarasis,  
Shielding the dense phalanxes of the infantry with his buckler,  
Was flying on a swift horse and readying the squadrons.  
Meanwhile, behold, a swift messenger came to the commander,  
Reporting that the confused enemy, already vanquished across the fields,  
Were turning their backs in flight. But by these words the young man  
Was unable to drive him from his proper counsel.  
His mind remained unmoved. But the commands of the terrible God  
Did not wish it so now. As he delayed to enter the wars,  
His armor-bearers urged the leader—Ariarith, once the bravest,  
And the noble Ziper, two greatest lightning bolts  
Of war, whom the Massylian phalanxes then trembled at,  
Both equal in valor and comparable in their fates.  
Then Ziper, beginning to speak, said thus: "Succor the Latins,  
Greatest of commanders. Our allies are joining fierce battle on the plains,  
But, being few, they are thrown into confusion by the dense enemy.  
Numbers press hard upon them. Let us follow our allies into the war.  
Take up arms and succor your men." At this, brave Ariarith  
Burned with love of the fight, and compelled the delaying  
Leader to move the standards. By the voice of his faithful servants  
The thoughts of the commander were driven. The horrifying trumpet groaned  
With its death-bearing song and compelled the squadrons into battle.  
The well-arrayed host advanced in vain, because the fates were crushing them.  
Thus it pleased You, O Highest Father, while You wished to afflict  
The sinning peoples of Libya. Their transgressions were  
The cause of so great an evil; it was not the fault of the ruler.  
Carcasan discerned from afar a gathering dust storm,  
And the energetic Nasamon summonned his own column.  
Then, with these words, he strengthened their trembling hearts:  
"O unconquered nations, whose recognized valor  
Persuaded me to challenge the Roman battle lines with weapons,  
This is that day on which horned Ammon  
Promised to you the fated land. Now advance through the enemy,  
Fearless with your weapons. Display now your ancestral glories.  
Fight fiercely, each with your own hand, and trust in the fates.  
  
Section 6.14.  
  
The great divinities are our helpers. Victory will be given to you,  
Trust in my certainty, men. Cast off shameful fear  
And bring your accustomed strength and courage into battle."  
Scarcely could Carcasan finish this speech when a terrifying shout ran through  
The Syrtic camp with a savage, rattling tumult.  
The battle lines of the Marmaridae roared. Harsh fortune incited  
Their furies, and savage Bellona goaded the cruel bands,  
Compelling the barbarian lines with her bloody whip.  
Then their wild hearts swelled with rage. Now the innumerable horses  
Began to leave the camp to occupy the plains.  
There was a river in the middle, well-suited for the deceits of war  
And Massylian ambushes. A dense grove hindered their weapons  
With its tangled foliage. Barren tamarisks and the wild olive  
With its bitter leaves occupied the hated valleys.  
Here the lines of the Marmaridae, and there the Latin lines, on the opposing  
Side, were standing and rousing grim war.  
The grove entangled the weapons of the soldiers, and the branches blocked the flying iron  
With their opposing boughs. The slender reed could not pass through,  
Though hurled by a most powerful hand, nor could the unhampered tamer  
Turn his horse against the enemy, nor was any soldier, entangled  
On all sides by tight branches, able to wield his long javelin.  
That terrain held back the anxious leaders and the cautious commander  
From battle, and forced the lines to stand still.  
They checked their steps. No one who dared to commit himself  
To the steep slopes kept his footing on the precipitous bank.  
The commander approached the place with valor,  
Preparing to test it with his armor-bearers, hoping to discern  
By what way he might be able to cross it.  
The thickets were dense around him,  
And the Nasamon tribesmen were hidden inside,  
Preserved within the only safe path.  
The faithful band then pressed them into flight.  
At the hastening approach of the fierce foe, the Nasamon tribesmen did not even hurl a missile.  
Seeing their foes opposite them, they fled at once, swifter  
Than the South wind, and no slower than a phantom seen in a dream.  
Behold, a confident rumor the  flew to the great commander,  
Reporting that the pacified Moors were now leaving the battle,  
Driven by a heavy terror. Then, at the command of John,  
Wise Paul and Amantius together hastened  
With aid to relieve the men. But the tracks of the Moors  
Were suddenly nowhere to be seen. At this, the Mazax fled in fear, and did not look back  
At the battle, nor did he turn his face toward the foe.  
  
Section 6.15.  
  
Then the other leaders turned and fled, and the terrified tribunes  
All departed, leaving their commander behind to the wars.  
Then the victorious Laguatans pursued the scattered lines.  
A shout went up, reaching the heights. Then the hostile faction unleashed  
Their columns from all the valleys across the wide plains.  
You would believe that the men had risen up from a sudden  
Opening of the earth. Rushing forward, they surrounded the standards  
From this side and that, thick around the leaders. A host of thousands charged upon the scattered men,  
And the day was shut out by the hurled spears,  
And the shadows of night pressed wide upon the plains.  
The wretched band uttered a groan, and by bloody weapons  
Horses were poured out across the fields. The energetic enemy raged,  
Harsh and merciless. That single day could have shattered  
The entire Latin race alike in one ruin,  
Had not the Omnipotent Father, taking pity from high heaven,  
Preserved the Roman lines among so many thousands,  
And, by the voice of the great John, saved them even as they fled.  
For, seeing the allied squadrons abandon the fields,  
He thundered with his mouth, and aroused their rages with these words:  
"If we die, comrades, if the supreme fortune draws the Latins  
By their lot and prepares to overthrow them in savage wars,  
Why should I fall by a womanly death? If life remains,  
Why should I flee in fear? Turn your bridles now, citizens.  
Fix the standards, men! Despise the furies of the nation  
And fiercely try the fight. Either we conquer the enemy,  
If God Himself wills it, or, if our sins withstand us,  
We shall not lack our own glory when my side faces the myriads  
While we die. Halt your flight, and draw the steel.  
Let each do what he sees me do." Having spoken thus with a fierce  
Countenance, he gnashed his teeth and snatched the flashing hilt,  
And raging, drew his sword for wounds.  
At his voice, a certain part returned. Then bitter battles rose  
And the lances raced in a constant rainstorm.  
Cuirasses and helmets thundered, the bronze shields groaned under blows,  
And with veins ruptured deep within their breasts,  
Men poured out their crimson lifeblood. Proud in his valor,  
Fierce Ziper, raging, went through the weapons, through the enemy,  
Overthrowing the Syrtic columns with his death-bearing weapons,  
And Solumuth likewise, though each was unequal in his fate.  
  
Section 6.16.  
  
They pierced the oncoming breasts of many with long  
Pikes. Their spears drove into the trembling livers  
And the hearts of men. They split the hollow temples with swift iron.  
One tore off a head, another cut off a massive thigh.  
Not otherwise would you have watched two lions savagely raging  
With open jaws in the midst of the flocks. Now, fierce with claws,  
One tears the prey. Now, with bloody teeth, the other  
Crushes the tender beast and rejoices in the warm blood.  
But in another part, Bulmitzis and the great Ariarith,  
Fierce Dorotis, and John's own armor-bearer  
Were dealing deaths to the oncoming foes across the wide plains  
By various kinds of wounds. Proud with his blade,  
One raged; another, superior with the javelin, prevailed on high.  
One twisted stiff arrows from a whistling string,  
Another roared, powerful in his art, and fought with both kinds of weapons.  
The commander himself flashed in their midst with a blade  
Like lightning, keeping back the hostile phalanxes with terror.  
Not otherwise do they report in song that armed Jupiter  
Made the Giants tremble throughout the wars, when the stroke of his thunderbolt  
Destroyed the horrible brothers with a blazing wound.  
That host would have conquered, had not an angry fortune now denied  
Her successes. Soon the countless band of the enemy increased:  
The infantry columns of the Marmaridae arrived. Thick iron flew all around from every side,  
Along with the oak-wood of heavy clubs, and menacing stones  
Fell like thunderbolts. The plain was hidden by black dust:  
Hardly could the hard-pressed soldier now discern his own weapons.  
Struggling against everything, the commander resisted the weapons,  
Forbidding his men to turn their backs. As the massive tumult pressed hard,  
Two armor-bearers fell: the great Ariarith collapsed,  
And the magnanimous Ziper sank under more than one missile and a hundred wounds.  
A harsh blow from a foe coming hand-to-hand  
Transfixed the horse of the commander. Snatching that missile with his right hand  
From the body of his fierce horse, the brave commander  
Broke it and, furious, hurled it into the face of the enemy.  
Here, discerning his routed allies and now his own wounds,  
The commander groaned. Then, lifted up, he set himself  
Upon the steep back of a horse, and, terrifying with a menacing look,  
He swiftly burst through the dense column.  
  
Section 6.17.  
  
The commander made a path with his sword through the hostile columns  
With his companions. The battle lines, restored by terror of the leader,  
Returned behind his back. The hero won space for himself  
And for his men, then directed his course into the midst of the enemy,  
Arranging the men by their standards, and keeping back with arrows  
The Massylian lines. No men were able any longer to challenge him  
In his fury. Whoever pursued the leader  
Received wounds coming at him from a backturned bow.  
If anyone by chance attacked from the front, he was pierced from afar through his panting breast  
By an upright spear, and was poured out on the ground.  
Those who closed in on his flank gave up their fierce lives  
To flying javelins. The reed clove through either side.  
Then the Nasamons, fearing to join battle with the leader's standards,  
Held back, and spread their horses across the wide plains in a boiling ring.  
The commander slaughtered those fugitives who had abandoned the men  
And the standards, and who, at the first clash of war, had dreaded  
Its bitter threats and had turned and given their backs.  
He was an excellent leader, shining with the title of commander,  
Nor inferior in valor, renowned in Roman arms.  
Once, from afar, he espied a man fleeing the battle  
Across the wide plains, and he pursued him with a voice like this:  
"Is this your fidelity?” he said, “Did we move into battles for this,  
That the Roman host should perish? Do you now abandon your weapons?  
Where are you rushing, unfortunate man? Through you our wretched youths  
And our fame are perishing." At the words of the commander, that man  
Blushed, and at the same time a sharp grief burned in his very bones.  
Then a tragic sense of shame compelled him to face the pursuing nations.  
Desiring to break through the hostile squadrons,  
A savage death pressed hard upon the man. He struggled to defend  
His overwhelmed comrades. Now the victorious enemy was laid low,  
The vanquished was rescued, and the fates of those falling were changed.  
Foremost fortune helped him in his daring. Now proud with slaughter,  
He raged thoroughly and laid bodies low by various fates.  
Just as a breathless tigress, robbed of her cubs, rages through the Hyrcanian fields,  
Whose stolen young a horseman has by chance snatched from their Caucasian  
Den, bringing a spectacle for Persian kings, and the terrified rider tires  
His steed with an iron spur, and the tigress, like her mate,  
Fierce and lighter than the West wind, grieves for her tender offspring  
And flies: even so did the leader strive to clash with the peoples.  
From one he snatches the head with his sword. Winding the missile of a dying man,  
He lays low another from afar. He strikes another through the breast  
With a strong pike. Threatening another with a quivering spear, he transfixes  
The shield, the hand, and the side all at once.  
That man, dying, looks down at his thigh torn away by a wound,  
Part of himself falling off, and, while still alive, he grieves near his own limbs.  
Another lies wounded, crushed by the fall of his horse,  
And the steed is transfixed on top of him. The frothy blood spurts high  
And, running, mingles with the warm sands.  
  
Section 6.18.  
  
In a thousand ways the boiling victor scattered wounds,  
With Fortune offering him paths. Now, weary and panting,  
His horse smokes and refuses to run. The Maurusian battle line increased  
As it arrived: Camalus and Ceraus follow close behind,  
And the fierce Stontaus. A massive army surrounded the enemy,  
And ahead, a dense flight of lances threw the fields into confusion,  
Flying thick. The shield, standing against the oncoming javelins,  
Groaned under the bronze and thundered hoarsely. They pressed hard with shouts  
And threw the man into turmoil. His fastened buckler was covered over  
By thick missiles. Now he sweats beneath the armor itself,  
He is crushed by the weight of the weapons, and a heavy forest  
Of spears exhausts his weary frame. He labored as the crowd overwhelmed him.  
Gradually withdrawing, by his skill he sought the nearby shores  
Of the coast, and secured his right flank with the waves  
Of the sea. As he stepped, he protected his left flank and his back  
With his buckler, then defended his face and his path with weapons.  
Just as a lion, when a trembling ring of hunters hems him in,  
Roars fearless in his valor and savagely rolls forth murmurs  
From his terrifying jaws, while he arouses his rages, furious with erect courage,  
Nor do the men have the spirit or valor to run against him,  
But challenge their enemy only with voices and with spears hurled from afar,  
Even so did that raging leader depart, hemmed in by the crowds,  
Holding the curved shores and avoiding the spear-shafts with his shield.  
There is a place far off, steep in the midst of the sands,  
Which a wave of the sea, in the manner of a river, washes at its margin  
And, overflowing, encloses the fields with bitter waters.  
In these places the seaweed and back-sliding mud  
And deep mire rot beneath the trembling whirlpool.  
When he arrived here, his horse shuddered at the black seaweed  
And, terrified, recoiled backward. Then, blowing from its nostrils,  
It pricked up its twin ears, a sign of dread,  
And turned its flank, and raged smoking, and rolled back its eyes  
As it looked ahead. Nor did it dare to risk the dreadful danger.  
The great-hearted leader, alas, struggling to the end,  
Had finished the span of both his life and his journey. The enemy followed  
In a thick mass, driving him with shouts and throwing the man into turmoil. Then with frequent spur  
He struck the horse, redoubling his blows, and urged its great shoulders.  
Driven forward, the steed leapt out and tried to approach the path  
That was denied to its course, and, swallowed up by the abyss, it sank  
As it fell, and the unholy earth swallowed up the wild master above it  
In its chasm. Then Fortune, receiving the man, snatched him away,  
Rescued from his enemy, lest he should stand unarmed  
Or humbled and begging them with prayers. Thus she granted him a tomb,  
Lest his naked body should have lain upon the Libyan sands.  
  
End of Book Six.  

## Book Seven.  

Section 7.0.  
  
Meanwhile, the leader, with his troops marching in secure formation,  
Was now pursuing a safe route, and having brought back his allies,  
He entered, exhausted, within the walls of a small city.  
Here the hunger of the people was satisfied. Here, after the wars,  
It was granted to the men to behold safety. Here the horses’ burning thirst  
Was banished by water, and grim hunger departed from the pastures.  
The soldiers, once parched, tended to their horses in the rivers,  
Rejoicing in the long-desired waters, and recalling their past hardships,  
They plunged their bodies into the cool streams.  
After a time, they recovered their meager strength through feasting  
And drank life-giving wine after the grim wars.  
Now dark night rushed over the lands and put off the labors  
Of the world, mingling everything in the shadowy sky.  
Then followed sleep, soaked with solace for their cares,  
Bringing sweet relief at last, and embracing them with silent wings.  
The friendly rest of night relaxed their drooping eyes,  
Pouring over them from its overflowing horn. Then the hearts of the men lay forgotten  
Of evils and of themselves, and from their excessive labor  
The rest of their bodies breathed heavily through their exhausted limbs.  
The leader, however, dragged out a sleepless night in this troubled time,  
Turning countless anxieties over in his heart.  
Shaken by devotion, the father, weeping for so many deaths,  
Groaned and shed tears. Ricinarius was there,  
The general's solace and the companion of his labors.  
He always bore bad times mixed equally with the good.  
As a dignified friend, he didn't take his rest like the humble populace.  
As a man powerful in virtue, he never feared to lay down his life  
For his homeland. The fierce Persian knew all the deeds  
Of the man, his character, strength, wisdom, and labors,  
His illustrious work in war, and his peace and kindly loyalty.  
Hostile Africa also knew what he did against the Moors,  
And the father himself knew that the man had often endured  
Hardships alongside him. Thus love grew, thus grateful goodwill  
Remained between both. The latter regarded John as a father,  
While the former regarded him as a child born of his own blood.  
Therefore, wide awake, each was recounting his sorrows to the other,  
Recalling the dutiful deaths on that ill-fated field.  
The leader spoke first: "The vigilance of men is surely in vain  
If God is not our sentry. No one can win wars  
By his own strength alone. He alone crushes enemies—  
The Almighty, who moves, turns, and overthrows all things.  
  
Section 7.1.  
  
Yet the Roman race is not entirely hated by the Lord on high,  
Since He willed to save my people, hard-pressed among so many thousands.  
It is my wish to hasten to repair our ruin, and to try a battle suddenly,  
While the victor remains careless and thinks the defeated  
Are in flight. How often have the conquered overcome proud men  
In war! A greater victory is granted even to the few.  
Counsel me, dear friend, on what is more fitting for our arms."  
Then Ricinarius, calm in his characteristic dignity, opened his mouth,  
Offering friendly counsel to the general at his command:  
"It is right, O supreme commander, to look for divine help.  
Why do you wish to try battles again so soon?  
Does your well-known valor and the power of your mighty right hand move you?  
There is no need to expose your life to uncertain dangers so soon.  
Cruel fortune has made the enemy proud by favoring the bold.  
The hearts of men fleeing from battle are always frozen with fear.  
Great valor, broken by the terror of death, is afraid. It belongs to few  
To endure the tumult of war, especially after battles which, with recent slaughter,  
Have shaken the men. The largest part of our force is scattered,  
But it remains safe. Gather this part, brave leader,  
And command them to repair their strength. Seek out the tribes,  
Summoning them from every quarter—those who always keep faithful  
Alliances with us. Order them to march with moving reeds  
And under their own standards. The entire army, coming together,  
Will thus be secure, finding indeed whatever provisions it wants for sale.  
The tribes bring many herds of cattle with them. Ships will come to the shores  
And will bring grain and wine. The sea now has open weather.  
The troops will repair their shattered strength,  
And the squadrons, forgetting their fear, will renew the battles."  
The advice was pleasing: and the commander, holding fast to the excellent words  
Of his faithful friend, ordered swift messengers to carry instructions to all.  
The galloping horse ran everywhere, bearing the leader's commands,  
And they drove both the tribes and their own men forward with all speed.  
No general repulsed in war was ever able to renew fierce battles so quickly  
As the extraordinary John was able to do with his watchful heart.  
  
Section 7.2.  
  
Past fortune, when warfare was favorable, had not puffed him up  
Nor exalted his spirits, nor did adversity break him then.  
He was unmoved in his own virtue. Now, rising from the ocean,  
Dewy Aurora had removed the cold shadows.  
When the leader saw the whitening light arise,  
He washed his hands and his face with water. His face bore  
Hair matted with dust from yesterday's battle,  
And he prayed, lifting up his palms, with these words:  
"Almighty father, virtue and glory of the universe,  
Certain salvation and creator of the world, who arranging all things  
By a fixed covenant, turn and rule them by your moving power,  
You change the seasons, yet you are not changed in them.  
You roll the unfolded year through four successive seasons,  
And close the day with twice six equal hours.  
In due order you renew all things, yet you are not renewed by any,  
Remaining the same author, master, and builder of the world.  
I believe, supreme father, that yours is the great power,  
And I confess it. Let their own wicked deities deceive the wretched peoples  
Who believe in them. You often permit your own people to be tested,  
Yet by your devotion you likewise swiftly raise them up.  
Draw near and grant solace to your exhausted people.  
Raise up the Roman battle lines. Overthrow the proud  
Moorish forces who desire war, and to our ruin  
Swiftly bring help, I pray, and guide us as our ruler."  
Praying thus, he filled his face with overflowing tears.  
The father, shaken by devotion, and anxiously weighing the danger  
To Libya, groaned again and again.  
Here the almighty father, receiving the tears and words of the grieving man,  
Willed the Latin strength to grow strong again.  
The allies arrived, whom the dread of war  
And fear of the enemy had scattered. They reported to the commander  
That many were safe then, and says to look for the next force at Lunci.  
The news of the allies' safety created a little joy  
And comfort for their misfortune. The leader then assembled everyone,  
Summoning them with a bronze trumpet. Fearful, they surrounded him in a thin circle.  
They stood around sorrowful and poured forth tears in their hearts.  
The good commander addressed them with a gentle voice,  
Exhorting and comforting the men, and began with these words:  
"It is not with tears, young men, that we need to break brave spirits  
In war. Never is the Roman youth  
Broken by adversity. Why do you weep so much, friends?  
Behold, the allies are all coming from the midst of the enemy  
Unharmed, telling us that others survive at Lunci.  
  
Section 7.3.  
  
Since our allies are alive, our men should consider nothing  
As lost to the enemy. The booty which you perhaps grieve  
That those men have snatched from you now will remain preserved  
And will be increased even more. The enemies whom you see swollen with pride  
Over a slight slaughter will learn painfully what battles and Roman vigor  
Can achieve. Then it will please you to carry off your own spoils  
And those of the Moors alike by your accustomed valor.  
Release your sorrowing minds, drive out your anxieties,  
And keep unworthy fears far from your hearts, Romans.  
Victory will come to our cause."  
The leader brought forth those words to cheer the others.  
Though grieving himself, he feigned a cheerful countenance for his friends.  
He gave hope by his look, for he was suppressing anxieties in his heart.  
Then, heading toward Lunci, he assembled the Roman army,  
And he arranged for the leaders, the units, and the brave tribunes  
To return to their proper stations, to restore their weary horses  
By tending to them, and to look forward to the coming battle.  
He himself hastened on his way, marching along the coastal shores,  
Where he could supply his allies with daily provisions.  
From there, leaving the riverbanks behind, he sought the high walls.  
The city of Laribus rises secure in the midst of woods,  
Fortified with new walls, which the supreme Justinian himself built—  
The ruler of the Eastern world and sovereign of the West, the glory of the Roman empire.  
The leader ordered his swift allies, the commanders, and the tribes  
To assemble here before him—those whom he himself knew to be faithful  
To his standards from the very first struggle.  
An unhappy messenger had shaken the city of Tyre,  
Bringing news of the dire battle and the deaths on that savage field.  
They were indeed stunned, but their one most reliable hope in this plight  
Was that, according to rumor, the commander was safe.  
Meanwhile, this same winged news reached the ears of the general's  
Wretched wife. Warmth left her stunned heart,  
And trembling suddenly, she turned pale in her face.  
Presently, pitiable, she collapsed. Grief snatched away heaven and earth  
From her sight, along with the light. Then the image of death  
Held her eyes closed for a long time, as her limbs lost their strength.  
  
Section 7.4.  
  
The handmaidens rushed forward and quickly strove to lift  
Their fallen mistress, and they revived her chest with their palms.  
A faint breath was scarcely able to grow warm again  
In her cold limbs. Lifted by their hands, she sat up,  
Nor did she move her gaze, frantic and wounded with grief,  
And unmindful even of herself. Soon, her weary mind returned,  
As if wavering, and she thus began her lament in the first pang of sorrow:  
"My heart aches, and my eyes pour forth no tears.  
Why does the mouth of a wretched woman break out in no complaints?  
In such great mourning, has burning sorrow departed from my mind?  
Or has sorrow itself rather denied me tears and words?  
Did this wretched fate, driving me as a stranger,  
Lead me to Libya, after I followed over sea and land  
The arms of my husband? Why did I not run into the battlefields?  
Now, perhaps, a cruel fate would hold us two wretched ones together,  
Enclosed in the tomb by a sudden opening of the broken earth.  
I would have pressed his sweet chest with joined hands  
And would have mingled my body at the same time, embracing my husband.  
Death is sweet for the wretched. Would that it had been permitted  
To an eager lover by the fates to go as a companion again through the shadows!  
Ah, pitiable man, you lie forever enclosed in the sand,  
You whom fierce nations trembled at. The cause of virtue  
Drove you to die. Why indeed, when the people were fleeing, did you wish  
To return alone and to drive back countless squadrons,  
Alas, too trusting in yourself! Whither shall I turn and run back,  
Or whither shall I rush? Whose aid shall I ask as a captive?  
You were the rest for a wretched woman. With you I did not fear  
To cross the deep sea, careless of myself, when the burning South Wind  
Had tossed our trembling ships on the surging wave.  
Without you, how can I return through the dire storms,  
An unhappy wife, widowed of so great a husband?  
Oh, if only a prosperous fate had broken through the midst of my griefs  
While daylight dragged on! I wish cruel death had never permitted me  
To bear such great labors for so long, but by a recent lot  
I might have enjoyed, joined in death, the face of my John."  
With such great mourning she filled the Sidonian city,  
Groaning pitiably with tears. The high roofs multiplied the sounds  
Of her wailing. The eyes of all poured forth  
Salty fountains, and groans burst from faithful breasts.  
  
Section 7.5.  
  
But Father Athanasius, shifting his anxieties in due order,  
Anxious for affairs, for his homeland, and for his own safety,  
Urged all the troops to march into the broad plains  
And to hasten to the brave leader. His venerable old age  
Guided the peoples watchfully. Reverence and love  
For so great a man impelled them. His dignity, advanced years, industry,  
And persuasive words helped to soothe their sorrowing minds.  
Therefore, the father then ordered so many supplies to be sent  
And kept urging the allies onward, while the grateful elder  
Sent friendly counsel to the brave commander.  
Likewise, the excellent Peter, managing all things  
As if he himself were that old man, ordered swift servants to carry commands  
To the great father and to bring back word again.  
How much, revered youth, your devotion moved your soul to protect  
Libya alongside your father! Whatever I may think this to be,  
It is a wondrous sign that you, a boy,  
Should discern these anxieties in your tender heart.  
Already the tribes trembled and went pale at the rumor of you,  
And they fainted with fear, signaling their terror with hands and eyes  
At the mere sound of little Peter's name.  
Meanwhile, the officials likewise labored with exceptional care in all things  
On behalf of affairs and for their great master,  
They urged the hesitating squadrons to go out into battle,  
And they exhorted the men, swaying and moving them by their teaching.  
Here one entreated the allies with prayer, there another sharply rebuked them.  
In a time of crisis, the citizen hates to love delays.  
Indeed, grief, devotion, and love for great John  
Impelled the minds of the men. Now in his heart he visualized  
His gentle features, and in his mind he anxiously heard and saw him,  
Though absent, and he prayed to the Lord on behalf of the name  
And the safety of so great a leader. He trembled and pondered to himself,  
And, thinking of such harsh trials in his mind, he wore out his loyal heart,  
Wondering how that father was faring, and how Ricinarius was now doing alongside him.  
Doubtless each man's heart was saddened by the unspeakable disaster.  
Each burned to set out on the road, to see the beloved squadrons,  
Wishing even to pour out kisses upon the commander's feet,  
And he arranged everything well, cautious in every care.  
Wagons groaned, laden on the roads, and the route was bustling  
With tall camels. Iron struck against clashing bronze.  
They transported entire granaries across the broad plains,  
With many arms to be distributed to the men in the Roman fashion.  
  
Section 7.6.  
  
And now the leaders rushed from here and there with the brave tribunes,  
And they carried the victorious eagles and closed up the ranks.  
And the young John, son of Stephanus, a man of discerning mind,  
Was sent out, being skilled at reconciling two opposing parties.  
For indeed, between themselves, the savage Ifisdaias and faithful Cusina  
Then held the seeds of a harsh war with joined hatreds.  
Already a future war had begun to kindle their barbarian hearts  
With alternating passion, as envy redoubled their wrath. Therefore, the commander  
Ordered him to arrange a tranquil peace between both,  
To soothe the men, and to summon those very tribes  
To the support of their cause. Nor could any other man  
Bend their stubborn minds or calm their fierce spirits.  
He was able to tame wild tigers with his words,  
And, soothing fierce lions with his gentle speech,  
To keep away the evil poison of serpents by his utterances.  
Nevertheless, he delayed those who were raging on either side,  
Checked their wickedness, and strengthened the treaty for the nations  
By a new art. What great fears, what great labors he endured  
Between both of them until he could bend such great wrath!  
For his homeland, it was his pleasure to bear so many savage dangers.  
He reconciled the tribes and likewise moved them to battle.  
Therefore, raging and adorned in varied armor, the faithful leader Cusina  
Drove countless battle lines of Moors into the war.  
All the fields groaned as the reeds were set in motion.  
Their footsteps packed the broad plains.  
He, more valiant, armed thirty of his own chieftains,  
And although thousands followed each one in number,  
Cusina nevertheless rejoiced that he, as commander, was in the midst  
Of the Roman soldiers, whom the greatest prince of the world  
Himself gave as allies of peace and servants of battle.  
He relied on them, and he subdued both nations and wars.  
Ifisdaias came burning with a hundred thousand men,  
And filled the broad plains with his grim forces destined to burn.  
But their number was lesser than their valor. Nonetheless, he stood adorned in armor,  
Terrifying the enemy lines with his sight, while he joined a most bitter battle  
With his enemy via a thick shower of weapons.  
And the submissive Laudas gave assistance to their affairs,  
Arming twelve thousand companions along with his son.  
At the same time, the chieftain Bezina lead the entire camp,  
Assembling them, and filling the fields with his own herds.  
  
Section 7.7.  
  
When the leader had strengthened his forces with countless squadrons,  
He moved his standards and spread out the fierce tribes like rivers,  
Seeking harsh battles. On the fields of Mamma, the Austurians  
Were cutting down the rustic inhabitants with deadly weapons,  
Snatching a portion of Byzacium and favorable booty.  
Antalas then joined his own army once again  
To the opposing side and was bearing himself into the wars.  
A news messenger, crossing the winds on winged feathers,  
Sought the camp of the Syrtis, reporting that the commander was coming.  
It flew through every mouth. Now it terrified the swollen enemies,  
Singing harsh words, and telling that such great nations had come to the war  
To support the general. Now it aroused their anger,  
While the fierce Antalas, with his accustomed skill, declared  
That the crowds of Moors who had joined the Roman standards were cowardly.  
Then Carcasan, burning with rage, wished to go out and face them.  
The hostile Guenfeius recalled him, teaching him with counsel  
And words. He added these things moreover:  
"If you seek to defeat the Romans, greatest ruler,  
Receive willingly my sure words, which must be carried out.  
There is no need to join battle in these lands.  
The soldier is still brave, unshaken by any slaughters,  
And rages fiercely through the wars. You will not be able to bear them raging in warfare,  
Nor can any peoples stand against the Romans before hunger takes them.  
Instead, move the camp and feign to flee through a backward route.  
For a huge army will surely follow our flight.  
Whatever fields were green, we have already ground down.  
But they will find nothing. Either the enemy will scatter  
Or he will faint with hunger. If you then try fierce battles,  
You will be the victor, and hunger and the sword will kill the defeated."  
The unhappy advice was pleasing, and he raised the camp from the trenches.  
The brave commander pursued the fleeing camp,  
Doubling his pace on every march. Now, very close to the enemy,  
He was trying to join battle with joined standards.  
But because God had not yet granted the hour to the leader,  
He put off the clash of arms, reserving them for better triumphs.  
For indeed, the battle lines were marching through the extended plains,  
And the dust of the opposing enemy appeared more clearly to the men  
To be not far off. Valor was growing in their fierce minds,  
And they were preparing to try battle at any moment,  
When suddenly the day grew hot. For Phoebus was riding higher  
With his panting horses, balanced in the middle of the sky,  
At which time a shorter shadow surrounds a man's two feet.  
  
Section 7.8.  
  
The fire-vomiting South-West wind began to parch the lands  
With its blasts, and dissolved the strength and fury of the people.  
The bodies of all stiffened under the blast of the ignited wind.  
The men's tongues became parched, their faces flushed red, their deep lung panted,  
Stirring their breasts, and fire flared forth from their nostrils.  
Their rough mouths burned, stripped of saliva,  
And a parched heat raged in their dry throats.  
All moisture flowed anxiously from their inner organs  
And bathed their skin, which the hostile heat of the air  
Itself soon dried up, drawing the warm sweat from their bodies.  
When the leader John saw this, putting off the battle,  
He pitched his thirsting camp above sweet springs.  
The burning army rushed together toward the cool waters,  
And they yielded to their thirst. Just as in the channels of a garden,  
Countless bees crowd together when, under the high sun,  
They return from pasture, and the honey-making swarm  
Drinks water from a flowing stream, even so, scorched by the heat,  
Every race rushed along the banks. No bits restrained  
The tamed horn-footed horses, nor did the loose ropes that hold  
The camels by the face restrain them. Every mixed race was drinking  
The wave-wandering waters. Even as they drank, they burned and thirsted.  
One drank on bended knees, another with hollowed hands,  
Another, prone, lapped the waters from the river with his tongue.  
This one drank from a jar, another held a bowl and an urn.  
Already they were diminishing the rivers. Upon the very sands  
The warrior pressed his mouth, which the four-legged beasts  
Often defiled above by stirring them, and the crowd, burning with thirst,  
Did not even reject water mixed with manure. So the blast drove the wretched men.  
Nevertheless, a savage flame was burning the camp of the Syrtis,  
Troubled by excessive terror under alternating perils.  
For, worse than the wind, fear itself, wearying them  
And redoubling their thirst, was driving the bitter tribes  
Far away. But the South-West wind held back the conquered  
Peoples, impeding them with its fire. A horrible death  
Crushed the captive Africans. The plunderer pressed the wretched men, the flame delayed them,  
The lance struck the backs of the miserable, and the South-West wind,  
Burning with fire, agitated their breasts. Prostrate on the plains,  
The groaning crowd fell. They died along the trackless ways by varied fates.  
  
Section 7.9.  
  
Here one fell by the flames of the wind, there another fell by the wound of cruel iron,  
Here another fell by both. For with their swords they poured forth the bodies of those  
In whose mouth a faint breath still remained.  
Now a heavier blast, coming upon them, agitated the Moorish  
Bodies, pressing them down with death. Intermingled throughout the meadows fell  
The weary four-legged beasts. A cruel lot denied them life.  
But nevertheless, a driving terror ordered the hostile crowds to go  
Through the deserts. Nor did panic now permit them to pitch  
Their camp, but it drove the Moorish tribes to distant shores. For twice five days  
The immense South-West wind increased, parching all things with fire-roaming storms.  
For so many days the fleeing enemy line, out of terror,  
Placed itself far away from the exhausted Romans.  
By order of the leader, a tribune, the son of Caecilius, went out  
To scout the enemy, accompanied likewise by a fierce number  
Of riders, who pleased him by their valor.  
The character of the man had already been well known to the great commander,  
Falling no shorter than Hercules in prostrating enemies with his strength.  
He was swift in his agility, terrifying in his exceeding valor,  
And mature in counsel. Although the fierce Moorish nation  
Had closed off all the roads with a blockade,  
He, nonetheless superior, had often struck down the wild tribes  
With his careful weapons. The prostrate Laguatans trembled at the man's  
Warfare. The Frexes and the panting Naffur trembled too.  
That famous tyrant of the Vandal nation had feared him,  
And the father and commander himself loved this brave man exceedingly.  
He himself had often witnessed the man laboring in his own armor.  
He received the rebels captured in war from his hands.  
The Roman youths knew the brave deeds of the man, rejoicing in such great trophies.  
Therefore, having been sent, he departed. The hostile plunderer of Lunci,  
Holding the fields near the sea, secure and wandering about,  
Was burning all the estates with submissive flames.  
He took up his hiding places through the shadows of the night,  
And swiftly, through the midst of the enemy, he entered the walls  
Of an anxious city. So great was his confidence in the sword.  
I sing of things not unknown. He did not fear to overcome danger  
On behalf of his homeland. Thus he was able to carry out the commands  
Of the master and to endure such great labors.  
  
Section 7.10.  
  
He entered the gates, sought out the secrets of the enemy.  
Then, having departed, he went around the hostile trenches in order,  
Learning the terrain as he surveyed it in the helpful silence.  
From here he returned to his companions, and from there he sought the great commander.  
And soon he escaped undaunted through the countless crowds  
Of plunderers. Afterwards, he saw from afar that the tents  
Of the Marmaridae and the dangers of that savage tribe  
No longer stood above his own men, and he discovered that the enemy  
Was devastating the places far and wide. Then his spirit rose in anger,  
And he thus began to speak to his companions: "If we carry back only words  
To the leader, it is not full evidence. For we all confess that we know  
Only the places which their trenches occupy.  
To convey the counsel of this tribe to the supreme commander—  
This will be a perfect advantage for Roman affairs.  
The time is here, companions. Let us snatch men from the enemy,  
While there is opportunity, to reveal clearly to the leader all  
The secrets of Carcasan." He had spoken no more  
When behold, Varinnus came to meet them—that warrior  
Once so terrible—with a grim crowd swelling around him.  
This man was feather-crested, not the one whom the sword-mighty  
Solumuth had mangled before. Yet each was fierce in his stock,  
And being closer in blood, he was bearing himself through the wars.  
When Liberatus, looking out, saw him coming against them  
And attempting battle with upraised right hands,  
He himself rushed forward first and attacked the cavalry squadrons.  
Then, with a strong javelin, he drove clean through the breast of fierce Veuman,  
And with his heavy sword he delivered Marzin to death, along with black Lamalda  
And Zeias. H also struck brave Tifilan,  
Burcanta, Nathun, Sarzun, Tilis,  
And the sword-bearing Nicas. To the shadows he sent their companions,  
The terrible Masas and Dextrus, who was brandishing a missile.  
Peter transfixed the bold Tafaran with his spear,  
Whom Iammada attacked as he struggled against the hostile weapons.  
Peter, however, caught him and slew him with his blade,  
Whirling his weapon high at the crown of the head and cutting through the veins of the temples.  
Stephanus struck Altisan, Tarah struck Jugurtha with the sword,  
Priscus struck Murifer, Carosus struck Jelidassen,  
Siluutis struck Zembrus, and Georgius stained Auspur,  
Piercing his groin with the wound of a hurled spear-point.  
The defeated Moorish phalanx turned its back in flight.  
The victorious youths and the swift tribune pursued them.  
  
Section 7.11.  
  
A dense cloud rose to the ethereal plains,  
Made of the dust of flight. The hoof sounded with its hard horn,  
And the plain lay hidden, covered by scattered sands.  
Digging into the flanks of their horn-footed steeds with crowded spurs,  
Each enemy fled. Then the dust followed the lines  
And indicated their paths. Not otherwise does the wind, when bursting from its prison,  
Roll together the soft sands with its blast,  
When swollen Boreas, now free from his Scythian court,  
Rages wildly into the plains. A horrible whirlwind flew before them,  
Twisted by the power of the South Wind and forced into a gyre.  
It swept the sandy lands, throwing the waters into confusion.  
And now each soldier, raging in war, followed the fleeing  
Lines through the fields, and scattered the warring crowds,  
Pouring them forth over the grass with warm iron.  
The tribune himself, however, disdained to stop so low as  
To prostrate the men in slaughter, but striving to capture them alive,  
He kept flying on his swift horse, and turning back his javelin,  
He knocked down their huge bodies, striking their limbs.  
Snatching four chosen Moors from the line,  
He bound them and pinned their arms with interwoven knots,  
And preserved the men, so that they might report the secrets to the great  
Commander and reveal the hidden plans with their own tongues.  
Seizing Varinnus by the hair, he snatched him by his own strength  
And suspended him from his horse. The unhappy Nasamonian trembled  
And hung from his right hand. Straightaway, letting him down,  
The tribune threw him to the ground and swiftly pinned down his fierce breast.  
Then, dismounting with his characteristic agility, and with rippling muscles,  
He bound his rough hands tight behind his back with bonds.  
From here the fierce Varinnus was led vanquished, with his hands bound behind his back,  
Along with his companions, and with a downcast countenance  
He stood before the feet of the leader. Then the entire Roman crowd  
Came running together in their eagerness to watch,  
As well as the Moorish chieftains, desirous to learn the causes  
And what the clear truth might be. And the victorious son of Caecilius,  
Having been ordered to speak, said: "Keeping your sovereign commands,  
Best of leaders, with Christ fighting for us, I ran  
Through the midst of the enemy and saw the deadly camp,  
Which the men pitched on the wretched fields of Lunci.  
  
Section 7.12.  
  
Having entered the city, which was trembling with the wars surrounding it,  
I grieved. Yet there I saw great miracles:  
For the walls lay there surrounded by no fortifications,  
Protected only by the defense of God. High peaks of a plumed roof  
Did not defend it with towers.  
The priest tamed the tribes by the power of the word.  
Thus heavenly grace prepared their untamed minds.  
He is able to soothe raging lions with his warnings  
And to calm wild beasts. The hearts of wolves grow gentle,  
So that they do not harm the tender lambs with greedy bites.  
Now at this time, he urged that you command us to hasten in pursuit,  
Trusting that battles will be prosperous for Roman affairs  
If you should come. He would not cease from praying with tears  
On behalf of the peoples, your arms, and the Roman camp,  
Continually pleading that the Almighty may crush our enemies  
And humble the proud by His power.  
Having departed, I detained these rebels with exceeding sweat,  
So that they may reveal to you all the hidden secrets  
Of their wicked tribe and teach their mind in due order."  
The tribune had finished speaking of the deeds he had performed.  
But the leader, looking down upon the bound men for a long time now with a grim eye,  
Angrily said: "What daring possessed your minds?  
Speak, you gallow-birds. What dire fate compelled you  
To return from here once more through the Libyan fields in war,  
To attempt forbidden paths, and with your accustomed plundering  
To despoil the homes of the Carthaginians and the Roman nations?  
Does Carcasan think he has conquered? Him, at an appointed time,  
God will subject through war, and the Roman populace  
Will see him bound and made a captive under their own arms.  
Before the trumpet sounds, recall what terror drove your mind  
To treachery, and tell your deceits and the frauds of your people."  
Then the feather-crested Nasamonian said: "A harsh command presses me  
To confess all things. Although these very words may threaten me  
With a deserved death, yet in my telling I will speak everything.  
Carcasan is the most valiant leader for our arms.  
In his auspices our greatest hope of rule remains  
And stands manifest. Thus prophetic Ammon sang to our tribes,  
Granting the plains of Byzacium to the Moors through battles,  
Prophesying that proud Carcasan would go through the Libyan peoples  
And restore peace to the world. Compelled by these words of Ammon,  
Bellona drove countless tribes to return once more through your fields.  
Indeed, the leader himself already wished to join battle with you.  
The hostile Guenfeius, turning his mind,  
Put off the man's clash of arms and bent his mind away from war,  
Only by an exceedingly grave counsel. For as for the peoples whom you now  
Rather think to be fleeing, it is not a bitter terror that agitates them,  
Nor do they tremble as conquered men. So that hunger may exhaust your camp,  
They feigned flight, and they prepare hardships for you by artifice.  
Do not believe our tribes would flee,  
Even if the Emperor should come and exhaust the whole world,  
Moving it with him to war. Not even Maximian was able to bring open battle  
Against these people when he held the scepter as prince of the Roman populace  
And passed as victor through all the tribes of the Carthaginians in bitter wars.  
But now, when surer Ammon grants prosperous wars to his own people,  
Do you think the Laguatans give way or have fled?  
You would wish this, bitter leader. But your fates will not have it so."  
Then, disdaining that the words of the raging man should storm for long,  
The commander thus opened his mouth:  
"Most surely, you shall stay in these fields of ours even so."  
Thus he spoke, and ordered them to be suspended by the neck  
From five upright posts in order, upon a two-horned stake.  
At his swift command, the hurried servants did so.  
  
End of Book Seven.  

## Book Eight.  

Section 8.0.  
  
After the leader learned of the treacherous plot,  
And all the deceits of that wicked race lay revealed,  
He perceived their cruel villainy. Then, weighing everything,  
The sharp edge of his mind scanned with silent gaze  
Through various options, seeking what actions must be taken.  
For wise judgment can defeat fierce nations far better  
Than armed valor. With balanced justice,  
He quickly reviewed his various cares in orderly succession,  
And his mind took flight through it all—just as a swift swallow  
Flying in circles searches for tender food for her chicks.  
Now closer to the ground she spies the green grass,  
Darting back and forth, now exploring the branches of a tall tree,  
Cutting through the air with silent wings.  
Finally, a plan settled in his calmed mind,  
And the leader spoke thus to his allies: "Carcasan does not dare  
To clash with us relying on strength of arms,  
But acts fiercely through cunning, preparing to harass the Roman cohorts  
And then flee. Though the wicked man flees with guile,  
He shall not escape. He will be overcome by greater cunning.  
I must pitch camp in the middle of the fields of Lunca,  
Which the plunderer now holds. If by chance he dares  
To join battle with me, he will be routed on the open plains,  
For our soldiers will fight better in open fields,  
Free on horseback, striking with javelin or the blow of an arrow.  
But if that godless, fleeing race abandons those places,  
I command that we guard the shores and order that no supplies  
Leave the land. The enemy will perish nonetheless,  
Unable to endure for long. Striking down that wicked race,  
A hideous famine will kill them without warfare.  
As for us, the sea sustains us, bringing all things for sale  
At the proper time, supplying both bread and wine.  
Move the camp, men, and march with orderly standards."  
He had scarcely spoken these words when the high cavalry  
And steady infantry covered all the plains. Dense columns  
Of men broke across the fields, and the din of horses grew.  
Warned by his scouts, the cautious Carcasan and fierce Antalas  
Realized this. At the sudden, rattling uproar,  
They moved their camp. Afraid to stand on the open plains,  
They abandoned their positions, built trenches in the high mountains out of fear,  
And surrounded themselves with a high wall of camels.  
  
Section 8.1.  
  
The Roman army occupied the shores with dense armor  
And tents filled the broad plains,  
And the Massylian leaders placed the Romans in their midst.  
On this side and that, they pitched their reed huts throughout all the fields  
And held all the roads. Then, from every shore,  
The father and commander John himself gathered the ships and ordered them  
To stay in the port of Lariscus, and he relieved his allies and peoples  
By distributing provisions throughout the camp.  
While that bravest man bided his time with proper skill  
And prepared to torment the enemy nations, a sudden mutiny  
Arose, and madness urged the Roman peoples through the camp  
With insane goads. Now, with frequent murmuring,  
They sharpened their wavering minds against their own hearts.  
Oh, the grief! They turned their armed right hands back into their own bowels,  
And each man readied the sword against his own neck and his own people.  
What madness was that, by which every Roman soldier  
Was so inflamed to utterly destroy his own battle lines?  
A cruel fate was dragging their wretched minds away.  
Do you not fear your leader, Roman? Beware of so many wars, so many enemies,  
Whom it is certain have taken positions around the trenches.  
Behold, through wars you are preparing to overturn Libya along with its tribes,  
And with hostile weapons, wicked man, you attack the wretched land.  
Alas, piety! Where is sacred loyalty? Cruel fortune strives  
To snatch from the peoples' hands the triumphs already won.  
The soldiers had begun looking at their leader with sidelong glances,  
Now speaking harsh words, disturbing the quiet minds of their comrades,  
And filling their ears with whispering.  
No longer did they set a limit to their hatred, but with wicked voice  
They uttered impious words. Just as a fire burns a forest  
When applied to it, and the first flames spark the conflagration—  
When a thin spark begins to kindle the light leaves,  
Or fragile stalks crackle with the tinder,  
You often see black smoke and a little ash go up at first,  
But soon Vulcan raises himself into the open air  
And destructive blasts overspread the thickly wooded hills—  
So did they, blind in mind, gradually sharpen their fury,  
So did they burst forth with threats: "Whither, pitiable youth,  
Do you follow this leader through wars? Whither are our weary columns  
Being dragged? Where is a cruel death being prepared once more for the wretched?  
  
Section 8.2.  
  
Alas, he pours out our worthless lives in endless peril,  
And no reward is given. He has afflicted us with deaths  
In bloody warfare. Roman blood drenches the sands,  
And the seas grow black with mingled gore.  
Thirst, and hunger, and the African wind, with its burning heat, have scorched us,  
And for our services there is no honor. Rally together, citizens!  
Stones, torches, swords—whatever fury and anger supply—  
Seize them! Let our side be free from this unspeakable labor  
By the well-deserved death of our leader." Thus shaken by dire tumult,  
The camp roared and the impious murmurs grew.  
The maddened band gathered, and assembly having formed,  
They sharpened their wavering wrath in their fervor  
And decreed their crime. A horrific clamor threw the tents  
Into confusion, the air rang with savage din,  
Just as if the Nasamonian enemy were waging battle at the hard-fought trenches,  
Throwing all into chaos. As soon as that sound reached  
The ears of the leader, "What madness shakes my camp  
With this rattling uproar? Go search," he said,  
"And quicken your steps." General Tarasis stepped out ahead,  
Anxious to learn the causes of these events.  
When he perceived the faltering noise and the murmuring of the men,  
He approached to calm the peoples with gentle words.  
But neither the general's face nor his pleading words could sway them,  
Nor any regard for Roman honor. Reverence had vanished from their minds.  
They even dared to hurl threatening stones.  
Cruel fate was driving the men, and destiny, pressing hard upon them  
With impending death, was drawing out the final days of the wretched.  
From there, a swift messenger ran back on foot to the anxious commander,  
Reporting that the savage wrath of the insane soldiery  
Had flared up, and that a civil war was suddenly  
Erupting. The revered leader groaned aloud and seized his weapon.  
Then he furiously left the trenches and the camp behind,  
Stepping forth in all his valor. His companions followed him,  
Along with the generals and his loyal band. Then, standing upon a high rampart,  
He rebuked the raging peoples with a weighty voice,  
Presenting an awe-inspiring sight: "So you thought me cruel,  
Soldier, in your bitter madness? If right and lawful power have been given  
To civil arms, behold, here I am. Make haste, if you think wars can be stopped  
By my death, if John alone is the cause of your warfare.  
  
Section 8.3.  
  
Is this the loyalty of citizens? If Roman devotion has thus departed  
From your hearts, then I shall certainly wage wars using these tribes instead.  
The brave and ever-faithful Cusina will be side by side with me  
In these matters—his tribes, his allies, and his chieftains.  
Depart from our camp, you cowardly band!  
Go! Let Cusina come now with his stirred-up reed huts.  
Let our Ifisdaias be present with his own tribes,  
And Bezina, and the swift battle line of the serving Lauda."  
He had scarcely spoken these words when the Moorish columns,  
With dense standards, broke through the broad plains from every side,  
Coming to the assistance of the leader. Then the Roman band,  
Fiercer still, snatched up their arms, and through all the trenches  
Iron-clad men gleamed. No sluggishness struck  
Their bitter minds, nor did the dense enemy terrify them.  
Yet the sight and the dread of their own commander,  
And wise Ricinarius, counseling them with calm speech,  
Had begun to restrain their furies in their astonished minds.  
They laid aside their threats. Grim Erinys departed.  
The men repented of their madness. They now wished to bow their necks,  
Speaking with humble voice—not driven by the native tribes,  
But mindful of their duty. Both devotion and loyalty to their leader  
Moved them, as well as fear, the dignity and valor of their commander,  
And Ricinarius, recalling the columns with calm words.  
He ordered the opposing battle lines to stand fast for a little while  
Under a tentative peace, and delivered these words to the Romans:  
"See how many nations follow our loyal commands,  
And be ashamed to have desired such wickedness. But if by chance you are prepared  
To pursue bitter warfare and fulfill your malignant purpose,  
Speak out. Let me learn what final desire remains  
To you. Shall I save our allies, or destroy you rebels?"  
No less than Caesar, when a great tumult was impending,  
Was able to terrify the Romans with his proud words.  
The astounded phalanx blushed, and in a dutiful voice  
Thus entreated their commander: "An impious madness drove a few  
To attempt this kind of crime, and just vengeance  
Rightly awaits them as the guilty. Let their punishment follow the culpable.  
We, as humble servants, will follow the commands of our master and leader."  
Amidst these voices, the innocent multitude dragged forward the authors  
Of the crime and handed them over in chains to the leader for so great an offense,  
Atoning for their fault with devoted hearts.  
  
Section 8.4.  
  
Peace was made for the peoples. Vengeance for the wicked crime  
Settled their minds. Henceforth, the leader instilled even greater greater  
Over all the nations. Happy that the soldiers were quieted,  
He entered the trenches. With devoted hearts,  
The now joyful allies confessed that they would obey the words of his command.  
Straightaway he ordered the bronze horns, screeching with hoarse song,  
To summon the battle lines. Moving the standards, he deserted  
The shores and pitched his camp on the plains of Cato.  
But the Syrtic plunderer was holding his camp, secure  
In fortified places. Yet a dire hunger had already begun to disturb  
The entire race of the Marmaridae. Only their livestock  
Served for banquets, for there was no grain. When the great leader  
Of the Roman people learned of this, preparing to enclose the nations  
In a blockade, he then held back his standards for certain days  
And shut out the battle lines from the unfavorable woods.  
And now a day and another day passed, delayed from arms  
By the skill of the leader. That very stillness caused  
The hesitant tribes to trust themselves to the plains. The luckless Nasamonian  
Thought it was true panic. He ordered them to set up  
An unfortified camp on the flat lands. With death already too close,  
He had assumed an overly bold spirit, and savage wrath was in him.  
Leading his columns out from the middle of the trenches,  
The leader called a council. He himself stood higher upon a rampart.  
Then the chosen generals and brave tribunes assembled,  
And the soldiery, arriving packed together in their respective columns,  
Rushed forward by companies and troops. Then, faithful to the cause,  
The Massylian band gathered, thus mingled with the Romans,  
Just as fierce as if they were Roman. Love for their commander itself,  
And a terrible fear, ordered them all to meet on the plains  
As if they had one mind. Only their attire distinguished the two peoples.  
The tunics of the former were of iron. The latter, with arms bare by custom,  
Wore embroidered purple mixed with varied color.  
A shield covered the former, a small leather buckler the latter. The soldier  
Stood high with a helmet, and the Moor with the wrapping of an interwoven cloak.  
But another, brushing his long hair back from his forehead,  
Leaned upon twin missiles with bent knee  
Or drove his hard spear-point into the solid earth.  
The leader, standing in their midst, addressed them with friendly words:  
"Roman comrades, sole hope of a weary land,  
The certain salvation of Libya now hangs upon your arms.  
  
Section 8.5.  
  
Now it is time to end the war and our harsh labors.  
We must fight." The joyful battle lines and Roman peoples  
Moved their standards. A single voice roared, and the nations, the leaders,  
And the Massylian bands sang out with a great crash.  
They pledged their courage, and a sound ran through all the columns  
With a joyful murmur. Not so loudly do the shaken seas leap up,  
Signaling from afar the coming winds.  
When the excellent John perceived the wishes of his peoples,  
He guided them with a better counsel, which was the primary cause  
Of safety, and gave victory to their highest endeavors.  
When he suppressed the din and commanded silence with his right hand,  
The men fell silent in anticipation and looked up at the face  
Of him speaking. Their minds and ears were readied for his commands.  
The master thus addressed his allies with a clear voice:  
"This day, allies, is past. Nor is tomorrow given as a day  
Suited for battle, since it is consecrated to the Lord throughout the world.  
Therefore come, O chieftains, let us serve Christ with rejoicing.  
Let us humbly pray for protection from Him with tears,  
And it will come quickly, I trust. God will crush those malignant nations  
By His power, look upon our labors, and give new joy to our cause.  
But when the revered priest has rightly offered celestial gifts  
To the Lord through completed holy rites,  
And the Roman soldier has paid his vows in due order,  
We will set out the tables. Do not pasture your horses far  
In the plains, since, after feasts have been received according to custom,  
It is my pleasure to move the camp—not to join grim battle,  
But only to hasten from these places, so that on the following day,  
As soon as rising Phoebus touches the world from its first edge  
With his flame-breathing horses, it may be permitted to join battle  
In fervent slaughter near the fields of Latara.  
Nor will the journey weary us long, and both horse and foot  
Will be strong, and will kill the fierce nations by their valor."  
The men rose up with a shout, applauding, approving,  
And exulting in their hearts. The cohorts returned to camp.  
No less did the rebellious Nasamonian, in a different quarter,  
Holding a council for the time of that unspeakable war,  
Gather the impious columns of his kindred multitude.  
Other tribes also, whom the love of plunder had compelled  
To tear Libya apart in wars, were now, by a wicked fate, brought to their final day  
And impending death. Guenfeius, stirring up bitter warfare  
Alongside him, turned counsels over in his mind,  
Impatient to delay any longer. Yet first in rank,  
Carcasan thus began to speak: "John's nearby camp  
Presses us hard, and a cruel hunger now grips us all.

Our tribes' sole safety lies in joining battle swiftly,

The men assented to the plan, and the wicked nations  
Groaned aloud in spirit, sharpening their minds for battle.  
The wave hid the day in the Ocean, and dark night followed.  
  
Section 8.7.  
  
Then Phoebus unyoked his horses, then Cynthia yoked hers,  
While yet she renewed our strength. The one rose from the waves,  
The other sank into the waters. Every kind of creature received  
Placid rest throughout their limbs, and overcome by sweet slumber,  
The other animals across the plains were held by inactive sleep  
In their weary bodies—the cattle, the various birds,  
The terrible wild beasts, and the cold fish upon the shore—

Night, and clever in his cautious heart,  
He turned over his immense cares, organizing them in due succession.  
Next to him, the wise Ricinarius weighed grim warfare  
In his calm breast, and running through them with sagacious intellect,  
Reviewed his various concerns in orderly sequence.  
Both relieved their minds with alternating words,  
And their mutual conversation warded off sleep from their eyes.  
Oh, how often they approached the powerful Lord together,  
And for the safety of affairs, of the people, of Libya, and their own,  
Begged for a long time with tears! Nor did either of them in their sadness  
Pour out empty prayers. The Father, who terrifies the world with lightning,  
Saw them praying from His high seat,

Roared aloud, and they set up altars and called upon vain

And poured out the wretched blood in streams across the meadows.  
Some sacrificed to Gurzil, others to you, horned Ammon.  
Some worshiped Sinifere, whom the Mazax race accepts  
As the deity of Mars and thinks to be the powerful god of war,  
While others worshiped Mastiman. By this name, the Moorish tribes  
Called the Taenarian Jupiter, to which destructive plague a victim  
Of human stock was sacrificed with much bloodshed.  
Oh, ill-omened crime! A wretched groaning, shaking the breezes  
On every side, beat against the ether with its cries.  
Here a man drove iron into a throat and with invocations  
Driven by the deity itself, calling it to step forth through the shifting shadows,  
Ordered it to test the path of the sun. Then, after their profane custom,  
They tore apart the entrails of the cattle and sought the fates.  
God had suppressed these things, and every deity was deaf  
To their incantations. The priest brought back responses to no one.

Phoebus, shining bright from the farthest edge of heaven,  
Broke through the waters of the Ocean, and rising fairer with his light,  
Extended his gleaming rays across the world  
As an auspicious day was born. And now, in established order,  
The Christian peoples assembled—the Roman youths  
And magnanimous leaders together with their accompanying standards.

In the middle of the camp along with his chieftains,  
There the priest set up a great altar, veiling it,  
And surrounded it on all sides with sacred cloths in the customary manner,  
After the fashion of the fathers. Then the ministers formed choirs  
And sang sweet psalms, weeping with humble voice.  
But when the leader stepped across the threshold of the consecrated shrine,  
The grieving sighs of the people broke forth as he entered.  
They flooded their eyes with tears. Their voice on all sides beat against  
The heavens, and so many hearts, conscious of the hostile battles ahead,  
Struck their breasts. "Forgive the sins of our fathers, we pray,  
And our own, O Christ," they groaned, and with outspread palms  
They looked up to the sky, begging for the Lord's consolation.  
John himself, among the first, with knees and body bowed,  
Praying for his people and moved by devotion,  
Poured forth tears from his eyes like a river,  
And striking his breast with repeated blows,  
Entreated with these words: "Creator of the world, sole life  
And salvation of all things, O God, almighty author of earth,  
Sea, and sky, filling heaven and earth with your powers,  
And the wave-wandering deep, and whatever is brought forth in the world,  
The air, and the grim Avernus of the pale people—  
You alone hold dominion. To you belongs the supreme power,  
The praise, the kingdom, and the might of your great right hand.  
Look at last upon the Romans, look upon them, Most High,  
And as a loving Father, hasten to help. Crush the proud nations,  
I pray, by your power, so that the peoples may acknowledge you alone  
As the mighty Lord, while you destroy the enemy  
And save your own through warfare. Now every race condemns  
Graven images, and we confess you, O Great One, as the true God."  
Speaking thus, the father drenched the ground with the fountain of his eyes,  
Compelled by his piety, grieving in his mind for the peril of Libya  
And the heavy hardships of his cause and his people.  
No less did Ricinarius, wetting his face nearby,  
Pour forth water from his eyes, and as a suppliant, with sorrowful countenance,  
Prayed for aid for the Roman peoples.  
  
Section 8.9.  
  
And the magnanimous leaders sent their weeping up to heaven  
With wet breasts, as did the brave tribunes,  
And all the cohorts equally poured out their prayers  
Before God with tearful voice. And the high priest  
Offered gifts for the Roman peoples, loading the altar,  
And drenched the altars with a fountain of tears.  
Then with calm prayers he blessed the Father, honoring Him  
And offering Christ the customary praises in due order.  
The offering was acceptable to the Lord of highest heaven,  
Sanctifying and cleansing at the same time the whole Roman race.  
Adding to the leaders, he said: "Brave Putzintulus,  
Hasten with your troops and standards to where faithful Cusina  
Holds his banners. You likewise, mighty Geisirith,  
Join your allies to the man. It is fitting to relieve the faithful.  
But you, Sinduit, with the weapons of your young men now assembled,  
Join the Roman battle lines where brave Ifisdaias has placed his tribe and standards.

Veiling the high sky. They warded off the coming wounds with their shields.

With the strength of its vertebrae. While he fiercely withdrew the spear  
Where it stuck fast in the bones, struggling for a long time as he pulled it out, behold,  
Proud Mirmidonis came and aimed to hurl his quivering weapon.

The men attacked and threw into chaos the fleeing columns  
Of the Marmaridae across the fields, slaughtering, prostrating, and pressing them hard.

"Roman comrades and oh, our own faithful race,

Endure the threats of the Laguatans. Let the oncoming enemy not terrify  
You. See, John is already here as victor, moving all the Roman banners  
At the same time. Go through the enemy!  
When he arrives, let him praise those who are brave and faithful to the cause.  
  
Section 8.11.

Inflaming their hesitant minds, urged his allies into battle,

Burst onto the plains, along with the Moorish cavalry  
Mingled with the Romans, and the chieftain himself flew through the dense enemy,  
Raging in the midst of his soldiers, and the brave tribunes  
Bore Roman arms. In their customary manner, the Nasamones received

Obscuring the day along with the sun. The sky was veiled  
By thick missiles, and winged iron was scattered  
From bowstrings, and spears darted in dense showers.  
Some bodies fell, some took to the fields. They struck and felled  
The tribes on this side and that. With great valor,  
The Marmaridae pressed hard, but the loyal hope of their oncoming master  
Alone sustained the chieftain, his companions, and his peoples alike.

He himself inflamed his men against the enemy with these words:

It beholds to be Roman citizens.

Cusina, most faithful to our arms, is waging war  
Under a doubtful battle. If the enemy does not harm him,  
His name will remain a lasting glory for our fame through the ages.

Suddenly they looked back and saw the standards of oncoming John,  
And catching their breath, they wheeled around the supple necks  
Of their swift steeds. Awift in their valor, they demanded battle once more.
