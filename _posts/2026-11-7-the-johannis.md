The Johannis is a poem by Flavius Corippus, one of the most important poets of the Vandal Remaissance. It uses a style influenced by Vergil and Claudian to tell the story of the reconquest of Roman Africa by Emperor Justinian's General John Troglita.

Set around 535 ad, the poem details the battle preparations on both sides and dramatizes the twists and turns of war, with the Moorish enemies helped by demons and the Romans helped by God.

One thing I find notable about this epic is: it is the first "secular epic" I am aware of in which a role that is typically played by pagan deities is played by a Christian angel. Namely, in Section 1.6, an angel descends from heaven to assist John by banishing a demon that warned him not to enter Africa, and to tell him what to do next. In classical pagan epics, this would be done by some god or goddess, such as Juno or Mercury. Here, the gods and goddesses who perform cosmic interventions in pafan epics are replaced by Christian angels and demons.

A second thing I think is notable is: the classical gods and goddesses are not *entirely* absent. They are relegated to stylistic metaphors, or explicitly called out as demonic counterfeits of the true God. The Moorish god Ammon and the Roman god Jupiter are said to exist, but are demons, whereas gods like Mars and Phoebus appear several times, but only as poetic metaphors for warfare and sunlight, respectively.

A third thing I find notable is the ending. The poem ends very abruptly, in a way that reminds me of Vergil's Aeneid. The Aeneid ends in the middle of a climactic one-on-one battle, as Aeneas thrusts his sword through the breast of his opponent Turnus. The "denouement" in which he would have taken control of the city of Rome after (now that its greatest warrior is defeated) is left unversed and implied. Similarly, in the final section of this poem, John hurls a spear through the breast of the enemy leader Carcasan, but the poet chose to zoom out *slightly* compared to Vergil. He declares that the Romans are routing the Moors, and ends on a final image of the remaining Moors hiding in trees as the Romans stab them from below, expressed through the metaphor of a hunter killing a bird that he has trapped in a tree using birdlime. It is *slightly* less of an abrupt ending compares to Vergil's Aeneid, but it still leaves the peaceful "denouement" implied.

Then, I fed each section into Google Search's AI (based on Gemini), and asked it to translate each section idiomatically. Then I combined all the sections, read through the text, modified a few lines for readability, and decided it was good enough to post. Below is the result, and I hope you enjoy it.
