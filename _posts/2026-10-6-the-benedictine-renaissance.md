title: "The Benedictine Renaissance | 529-669 ad"

In Italy, during the 500s, four separate movements all combined to create a “perfect storm” of renewed interest in ancient knowledge, especially Greek knowledge. I already mentioned two of them in a previous section: Italy’s literary schools were largely staffed with expert Byzantine scholars thanks to Justinian’s reforms, and the Vivarium monastery was founded with an explicit goal of studying and preserving pagan and Christian literature.

A third movement was the Monophysite controversy, which drove a bunch of Syrian monastic refugees into Italy, who tended to speak Greek and bring libraries of Greek texts with them. The fourth was the “one man renaissance” of St. Boethius, who was captivated by Greek learning and in particular tried to personally translate all of the works of Aristotle and Plato into Latin. (He failed, but he managed to translate a bunch of them.) He also composed original prose and poetic works to synthesize pagan and Christian philosophy (not to mention science and theology).

Together, these elements produced a veritable torrent of translations from Greek to Latin. Major texts of Aristotelian science, math, and philosophy were translated by Boethius; three Byzantine history books were translated at the Vivarium, along with the historical books of Josephus', the ancient Jewish historian; newly arrived byzantine scholars translated many of the medical treatises of Galen, Oribasius, Alexander of Tralles, and the musical treatises of Gaudentius; to fight the Monophysite heresy, theological texts by Didymus the Blind, Epiphanius of Salamis, John Chrysostom, Gregory of Nyssa, Cyril of Alexandria, and Proclus of Constantinople were translated; and the monks translated ancient Greek poetry like the Ephrem Graecus collection (which became the Ephrem Latinus) as well as narratives like The Life of Saint Pachomius and the Relics of St. John the Baptist.

This firehose of ancient knowledge and mental clarity flooded across western Europe, carried by an ever expanding network of monastic institutions following the Rule of St. Benedict. Benedictine monasteries adopted the founding documents of the Vivarium (quoted in a previous section) as their intellectual marching orders, and thus that monastery’s goal of preserving, studying, and disseminating sacred and secular literature became an international characteristic of western europe.

This knowledge started spreading from Byzantium and Syria to Italy, and from Italy it went to France, England, and Spain. The “new learning” blossomed in all of these locations, but in modern scholarship, I have only heard it called a “renaissance” when it appeared in Spain.

In France, Bishop Agilbert of Paris made a “scholastic pilgrimage” to Italy to obtain sacred and secular books and bring them to the library of Jouarre Abbey, which he founded. Queen Balthild, who founded Corbie Abbey, funded a similar expedition to get more books. Saint Amand (who founded a French abbey now called Saint Amand Abbey) did the same thing multiple times.

In England, Saint Benedict Biscop traveled to Italy five times to purchase books with which he stocked the monastery of Monkwearmouth-Jarrow. In Spain, Tajon of Zaragoza made one expedition to purchase a multi-volume commentary on the Book of Job, and the manuscripts he obtained were copied by scholars in England and France. Leander of Seville made a similar trip, except he brought back books from Constantinople instead of Rome.

Many monasteries of this time had a “scriptorium,” where monks copied books by hand, and France served as a crossroads. Spain and England tended to send monks there to get books and bring them back, and France tended to send monks to Italy to do the same. Italy, meanwhile, was connected to Byzantium, and through this network, books and knowledge constantly passed back and forth, to and fro. Some of what was lost in one place to fires and wars was preserved elsewhere because of the constant desire to copy, study, and disseminate sacred and secular literature.

St. Theodore of Tarsus and Adrian the Grammarian traveled from Rome to England in this period, both of whom were fluent in Greek. They brought a treasure trove of classical and religious literature with them and set up a school to provide a classical education to the natives. Their presence in England, from 669 ad, marks a shift in Benedictine influence: the new dominant cultural force in England was the School of Canterbury which these foreigners founded. Consequently, I mark that date as the end of the Benedictine Renaissance, though the order itself didn’t change at this time, and continued to have massive influence.

Between the 500s and the 700s, four of the regions mentioned above – France, England, Italy, and Spain – learned from the classics and imitated them. France produced St. Avitus of Vienne and Venantius Fortunatus, Vergil-inspired poets who wrote epic poems narrating biblical history and the lives of the saints. England produced St. Aldhelm, an expert of the classical style who created an epic narrative poem about female martyrs called In Praise of the Virgins. It also produced the anonymous author of Beowulf, which on its surface appears to be a purely secular adventure story, but is filled with Christian themes and subtly infuses its heroes with Christian virtue.

Italy produced Arator, who wrote a Vergilian adaptation and commentary on the Acts of the Apostles. The Ephrem Latinus, also produced in this period, contained a long narrative called Joseph the Beautiful, which adapted the biblical story of the son of Jacob in a popular poetic style featuring no specific meter, but end rhymes, and lines of similar length – the technical term is “isocolon,” but the specific variant used in this case is sometimes called “breath length lines” and was inspired by biblical poetry.

Overall, the Benedictine Renaissance spread ancient knowledge throughout most of western europe. It also produced new, brilliant scholars who, despite the recent collapse of the western Roman empire, matched the intellectual output of their imperial predecessors, and in many ways surpassed them. The Germanic kingdoms which helped break up the empire would stay in place for centuries, but the fire of Christian intellectual fervor was growing up within them.
