title: "The Northumbrian Renaissance | 669-780 ad"
---

The School of Canterbury, mentioned in a previous section, operated under its original founders until 709 ad, and under their bright successor Albinus until 732 ad. The School became an overflowing well of intellectual fervor, alongside the Abbey of Monkwearmouth-Jarrow, mentioned in a previous section, and which was located in the English kingdom of Northumbria. Both institutions amassed impressive libraries of Latin language books. The School produced St. Aldhelm; the Abbey produced St. Bede the Venerable. The intellectual output of that man is staggering. Here is how he describes his compositions at the end of his magnum opus, the Ecclesiastical History of England:

“I have made it my business, for the use of me and mine, to compile out of the works of the venerable Fathers, and to interpret and explain according to their meaning these following pieces. On the Beginning of Genesis, to the Nativity of Isaac and the Reprobation of Ismaal, three books. Of the Tabernacle and its Vessels, and of the Priestly Vestments, three books. On the first Part of Samuel, to the Death of Saul, four books. Of the Building of the Temple, of Allegorical Exposition, like the rest, two books. Item, on the Book of Kings, thirty Questions. On Solomon's Proverbs, three books. On the Canticles, seven books. On Isaiah, Daniel, the twelve Prophets, and part of Jeremiah, Distinctions of Chapters, collected out of St. Jerome's Treatise. On Esdras and Nehemiah, three books. On the Song of Habacuc, one book. On the Book of the blessed Father Tobias, one Book of Allegorical Exposition concerning Christ and the Church. Also, Chapters of Readings on Moses's Pentateuch, Joshua, and Judges. On the Books of Kings and Chronicles. On the Book of the blessed Father Job. On the Parables, Ecclesiastes, and Canticles. On the Prophets Isaiah, Esdras, and Nehemiah. On the Gospel of Mark, four books. On the Gospel of Luke, six books. Of Homilies on the Gospel, two books. On the Apostle, I have carefully transcribed in order all that I have found in St. Augustine's Works. On the Acts of the Apostles, two books. On the seven Catholic Epistles, a book on each. On the Revelation of St. John, three books. Also, Chapters of Readings on all the New Testament, except the Gospel. Also a book of Epistles to different Persons, of which one is of the Six ages of the world; one of the Mansions of the Children of Israel; one on the Words of Isaiah... one of the Reason of the Bissextile, or Leap-Year, and of the Equinox, according to Anatolius. Also, of the Histories of Saints, I translated the Book of the Life and Passion of St. Felix, Confessor, from Paulinus's Work in metre, into prose. The Book of the Life and Passion of St. Anastasius, which was ill translated from the Greek, and worse amended by some unskillful person, I have corrected as to the sense. I have written the Life of the Holy Father Cuthbert, who was both monk and prelate, first in heroic verse, and then in prose. The History of the Abbots of this Monastery...two books. The Ecclesiastical History of our Island and Nation in five books. The Martyrology of the Birthdays of the Holy Martyrs... A Book of Hymns in several sorts of metre, or rhyme. A Book of Epigrams in heroic or elegiac verse. Of the Nature of Things, and of the Times, one book of each. Also, of the Times, one larger book. A book of Orthography digested in Alphabetical Order. Also a Book of the Art of Poetry, and to it I have added another little Book of Tropes and Figures; that is, of the Figures and Manners of Speaking in which the Holy Scriptures are written.” (Ecclesiastical History Book 5 Chapter 24)

The man was like a second Aristotle, and after his time, practically every intellectual book with an unknown author was attributed to him by default, because it seemed like he had already written everything. I want to particularly highlight a detail from that list: he not only had access to an existing Latin translation of the Life of St. Anastasius, but also the Greek original, which he used to correct the Latin version, because he learned Greek. Clearly, the book dissemination network that extended from Byzantium to England was still in operation and producing effective results.

His scientific works are also notable: in the book he called “of the Times,” he observed a relationship between the moon and the ocean’s tides, namely, that during certain months, the moon appears 48 minutes later each night than the previous night, and that during those same months, the tide comes in 48 minutes later each day. He theorized that this is because the moon causes the tides, which is correct.

His theoretical mechanism was also remarkably close to the truth: he surmised that the moon and the earth alternately “breathe” on the ocean, i.e. they apply a remote force on the waters. I'm not sure if by “breathing” he meant exhaling/blowing, which would constitute a pushing/propelling force, or inhaling/sucking, which would constitute a pulling/attracting force. I suspect he meant the former, and thought the moon pushed the waters one way, and then the earth pushed back the other way, to produce alternating rising and falling tides. Isaac Newton would later say the motion is caused by gravitational “attraction,” a pulling force rather than a pushing one. But whether Bede understood the moon’s “breathing” action as an attractive force or a propulsive one, his theories were about 1,000 years ahead of their time, and all based on scientific observation.

In his “Book of the Art of Poetry,” he explains (among other things) how to write a good epic poem like the Iliad or the Odyssey. To prove he could not only teach it, but also do it, he took a prose version of the Life of St. Cuthbert, a local hero, and composed a verse adaptation of the book in Latin in the style of the Aeneid. (Later, he rewrote the prose version too, which is why his catalog says he wrote the Life “first in heroic verse, and then in prose.”)

King Aldfrith of Northumbria was another leading figure of the Northumbrian renaissance. He was originally a prince who was second-in-line for the Northumbrian throne, and, not thinking he would be king, his family had him educated in a monastic school for an ecclesiastical career. But then his brother died, and Aldfrith ended up on the throne instead. As a pious, scholarly king with connections to the ecclesiastical intellectuals of his day, Aldfrith contributed generously to the edification of the English Church, and was partly responsible for the relatively peaceful environment within which Bede and the other scholars of the Northumbrian Renaissance worked. He is also associated with the Law of Innocents, which was a kind of charter of human rights developed by St. Adomnan of Iona. It encouraged a mutual agreement among Irish and English kingdoms about the rights and immunities of women, clerics, students, and children during wars, and though various kingdoms nominally said they would adhere to it, there is little evidence that it was heeded during actual warfare – a sad legacy of most efforts of that kind. Still, the law itself was a pretty forward-thinking idea for its time.

England frequently produced narrative poetry in this period, but usually in an early form of English rather than Latin. From the 700s we have two poems that narrate different parts of The Life of St. Guthlac, and we also have 2,300 lines from a verse adaptation of the Book of Genesis (the original was probably about 6,000 lines, making it comparable to the length of old pagan epics like Jason and the Argonauts), and 600 lines from a verse adaptation of the Book of Exodus (the original was probably about 800 lines). All of these works are in Old English.

Overall, the schools and libraries established in the 600s, like the School of Canterbury and the Abbey of Monkwearmouth-Jarrow, established England as part of the network of knowledge that spread from Byzantium to Ireland. But they didn’t stop there. They produced new scholars and poets who became the intellectual giants of their age, and some of whose genius could go toe-to-toe with the brightest minds that would appear nearly a thousand years later.
