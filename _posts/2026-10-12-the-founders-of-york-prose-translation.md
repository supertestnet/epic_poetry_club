The Founders of York is a poem by Blessed Alcuin of York composed in the 700s ad. In it, he gives an epic overview of the history of the English city of York. The principal divisions of the poem are:

1. Prechristian History of York  
2. Christian Kings of York  
3. Bishops of York  
4. Golden Age of York

This translation was created in several stages. First, I divided the poem up into about 43 sections of roughly equal line counts. Then, I fed each section into Google Search's AI (based on Gemini), and asked it to translate each section literally. Then I combined all the sections, read through the poem, and asked the AI to identify the "principal divisions" of the poem, which are outlined above. Then I inserted those divisions to break up the text, and below is the result. I hope you enjoy it.

# Section 1. Prechristian History of York.

Christ God, the power and wisdom of the Father Most High,  
Life, salvation, creator of humankind, renewer, lover,  
The unique Word of God, you, bountiful giver of gifts;  
Grant gifts to my mind, give words to a fragile poet;  
Dewing a dull breast with a lively stream,  
So that my tongue may be able to speak of you and your gifts.  
Without you, no tongue has the power to say anything worthy.  
You also, citizens of Olympus, I humbly call to witness,  
O Saints, a brave people, divine race of the Thunderer,  
Who carry victorious eagles into the citadel of heaven,  
Bringing royal gifts to the ethereal king,  
Who for your sake willingly poured out his sacred blood  
On the earth, so that he might save you from the shadows,  
And lead you with him into the court of God the Father;  
Bring your steps with me, compose this song with your prayers:  
Since my mind hastens to speak the praises of my homeland,  
And to bring forth for a little while its ancient cradle,  
In free verses for the glorious city of York.  
The Roman hand first founded this city high with walls and towers,  
Employing only the native British peoples as companions  
And partners in their labors.  
(For at that time, fertile Britain deservedly supported  
Roman kings, who ruled the scepters of the world)  
So that it might be a common emporium of land and sea;  
And become a secure power of the kingdom for its leaders,  
And the glory of the empire, and a terror to hostile arms;  
That it might be a welcoming place for ships coming from the far ocean  
To port, where the sailor, hurrying his prow with a long tow-rope,  
May halt, weary from the sea.  
The fish-filled Ouse flows between it with its waters,  
Stretching out flower-bearing plains on its banks everywhere.  
The land is beautiful on this side and that with hills and woods,  
A lovely, healthy dwelling-place in noble locations,  
Destined to have many settlers by its own fertility.  
Where chosen people from various nations and kingdoms everywhere  
Come in the hope of gain, seeking in a rich land  
Riches, a home for themselves, profit, and a hearth.  
When the Roman hand departed from here, with thrones thrown into confusion everywhere,  
Desiring to drive away savage enemies, and to protect the kingdom and seat of Italy;  
Then a lazy race of Britons held the scepter of the city.  
Oppressed by almost continuous battles with the Picts,  
And finally devastated, they underwent the weight of servitude;  
Nor did they have the strength to defend their homeland with their own shields,  
Or to recall their ancestral freedom with their swords.  
There is a nation, ancient, powerful, and outstanding in war and physique,  
Among the peoples of Germany and foreign realms,  
Called by the name of "Saxon" on account of their hardness.  
It pleased the leaders of the kingdom to hire this nation with gifts,  
So that it might be a help to the homeland and a terror to the enemies.  
Immediately the fickle common people favor this with a shouting voice,  
And praise the good counsels of their fathers,  
They break open the royal halls of treasures, and prepare to send  
Gifts to an unknown nation, so that it might all the more  
Agree to strike a treaty of pleasing peace with them.  
But they see the gifts as unworthy of such great prayers.  
They order ambassadors to go out, and already to furrow the salty shallow waters  
With swift ships; they repeat their prayers with tears and sweet fear:  
Since the love of freedom itself and the hope of reclaiming their homeland thus urged them.  
Why should I sing more now? Behold, a hurrying army,  
Carried across the waters of the wavy sea, had come,  
Bringing aid to their allies, and it drove out the abominable enemy,  
And conquered through many triumphs in war,  
Until the fierce Pict fled with his terrified army,  
Content to defend himself in his own lands;  
Meanwhile, the foreign soldier demands greater payments to be given to himself:  
This was the cause of war—to turn the sword against the allied nation,  
And to drive the lazy people from their ancestral kingdom.  
This seemed good by the piety of God, that a wicked race  
Should perish from the lands on account of the sins of their fathers,  
And a more fortunate people should enter their cities;  
One that would keep the commandments of the Lord.  
Which was fully done, by the gift of the Thunderer.  
Now, while new scepters flourished with frequent triumphs,  
The future people of God already began to have powerful kings from among themselves.  
At that time, the holy Bishop Gregory, venerable to the whole world,  
Ruled the seat of the Roman Church, and as the greatest  
Cultivator of Christ's fields, devoted everywhere,  
He scattered many seeds of everlasting life.  
Nor had he broken the Latin fields with the plowshare alone,  
But as a good and pious cultivator of a foreign plot,  
He furrowed beyond the swelling waves of the ocean with his plow,  
Drenching the pagan hearts with the wave of the divine word,  
Pouring honey-flowing streams upon the arid fields:  
From which the ocean-dwelling Britons drank the drafts  
Of everlasting salvation, already granted to them by Christ.  

# Section 2. Christian Kings of York.

Meanwhile Edwin, from the lineage of ancient kings,  
Born in York, destined to be lord over all things,  
Was driven into exile as a boy and fled envious kingdoms.  
Where he, as a pagan, saw a heavenly vision,  
In the dead of night, while at a certain time, alone  
And carrying an anxious heart with cares, he went to a fitting place,  
And sat in silence under the faint light of the moon:  
Suddenly a man, unknown in clothing and countenance, stood  
Before the eyes of the youth, and addressed him with friendly words:  
"What harsh care consumes you, bravest of young men?  
God the eternal King, who made the stars of heaven,  
Gives you certain comforts, which beautiful things you now see.  
Behold, he preserves your life from the enemy who seeks it;  
Moreover, he bounds a wide empire for you by the waves.  
Let that God be King to you forever and ever."  
And placing his right hand upon his head as a covenant,  
He said: "These shall be to you the signs of our pact."  
With these words, the messenger suddenly departed from him,  
And abundance of strength ran down into all his veins;  
Fleeing far away, driven out, despair also had left  
His innermost being. The outcome proved the words of the arriving  
Guest: immediately the king who was envious of his life  
And empire alike fell by an abominable sword.  
Then the amiable youth, returning, entered his now native  
Cities, received by the favor of the people and the nobles.  
Who, soon receiving the honor of the royal scepter,  
Sought good things for his own nation, generous toward all,  
Not fierce through his scepter, but kind out of piety,  
He became the love of the people, the father of his country, and the glory of the court.  
Overcoming hostile camps with continuous triumphs,  
He added all nations to his empire,  
In territories and regions, as far as the island extends wide.  
And now with bowed neck, they underwent the king's yoke:  
The people of the Saxons, the Pict, the Scot, and the Briton.  
Meanwhile, while the powerful-in-arms king ruled the tribunal of the kingdom  
In peaceful quiet, with wars put to rest everywhere,  
He reined in the peoples with the strong bridles of justice,  
Nor did madness seize arms, being pressed down under the weight of the laws,  
Because everyone feared the avenging axes of execution,  
Unless they observed with their whole heart the far-seeing decrees  
Which the ruler set down to be kept by the subdued peoples.  
He takes a faithful wife from the southern region,  
Outstanding in morals, and illustrious by the lineage of her fathers,  
And bountiful in all the virtues of the holy faith.  
To her is given a bishop, a keeper of an honorable life,  
Paulinus by name, a most illustrious citizen of the city  
Of Rome, relying on the great honor of his merits,  
Who was at once truthful in speech and prudent in heart,  
A cultivator of justice, a true lover of piety,  
A Catholic teacher, administering heavenly gifts  
To the ocean-dwelling nations, just as the morning star, running ahead  
Of the rising sun, scatters the foul shadows of darkness,  
And shows to the world that a serene day has come.  
Thus that pious father, by the divine light of the word,  
Drove out the foul shadows from human hearts.  
And on a certain day, approaching the king with a constant heart,  
He brought back the sign, which we said long ago  
The youth, expelled from his ancestral borders, had seen under the dark night,  
And he placed his right hand upon the crown of his head.  
Terrified, recognizing the signs of the foretold salvation,  
The king immediately descended as a suppliant from his high throne,  
And rushed before the feet of the venerable bishop, and  
"I will now do all things," he said, "whatever I promised,  
And believing, I will worship the God of heaven everywhere,  
Who granted to me my life, and the crown of the kingdom;  
For He alone shall be God to me through all time.  
But tell us now, how is He to be worshipped?"  
Rejoicing, the bishop answered with a ready mouth:  
"First, let the foul worship of gods flee far away,  
Nor let the blood of cattle smoke any more on false altars,  
Nor let the soothsayer seek an omen in warm entrails,  
Nor let the most vain augur watch the songs of birds;  
Let all the idols of the gods be thrown down to the ground."  
From this point, the pious bishop began to testify  
The mysteries of the faith openly and constantly to all in order,  
Until the pious king himself conceived the faith with his whole heart,  
And persuaded the people to believe in Christ.  
Behold, Coifi at that time was the chief of the priests  
And the head of errors, to whom the king said: "Come,  
Seize weapons previously unaccustomed to you, priest,  
And you first, profane the high temple with a javelin;  
You who had been a teacher of crimes, be now a teacher of salvation."  
The elder assented to these words, and answered in a few words:  
"Up to this point my life has hung by an uncertain thread,  
And a mist has overwhelmed my mind with doubtful darkness;  
From now on I will follow certain things, desiring to know the truth,  
And the eternal God; whether a future life awaits us,  
Or whether torments remain for the wicked, or rewards for the just."  
With these words, he seized a spear with a threatening right hand,  
And mounted a male horse, not according to his usual custom,  
Whose mane flies along its neck, and its steep crest swells;  
With raised chest, its swift hoof dug the earth,  
And impatient of delay, it shook the gold bridle with its biting,  
Just as terrible as a Parthian becomes with his curved bow,  
Or if he brandishes long spears into the light breezes—  
Such was he himself as he sought the roofs of the temple with his javelin;  
O exceedingly happy audacity of so great a deed!  
Before all others, he defiled the altars which he himself had consecrated.  
Full faith lay open, and not yet washed in the fountain,  
He fulfilled a work of virtue with faithful piety.  
Soon the whole crowd follows the health-bearing master,  
With unanimous strength they throw down and hack at the shrine:  
Then the erected structure of the profane temple collapses,  
And utterly destroyed, it falls apart even into ashes.  
Meanwhile, Easter time was present throughout the world,  
At which the king resolved to undergo baptism with his people,  
Even under the high walls of the city of York;  
In which he ordered small shelters to be placed quickly for God,  
So that he might receive under them the sacred wave of baptism.  
When the festive day of that bountiful time dawned,  
Together with his children and leaders, and with the following common people,  
In the eleventh year of his reign, he was consecrated to Christ  
In the health-bearing fountain, within the walls of the aforementioned city,  
Whose summit from that time on he raised up more sublimely,  
And established it to stand as the metropolis of his kingdom.  
Thus also Bishop Gregory had decreed long ago,  
When he sent the seeds of life from the Roman citadel  
To the nations of the Angles—he immediately commanded that this city  
Be held as the head for the churches and the summit of honor,  
And that consecrated bishops be clothed in the palliums within it.  
Thus the pious bishop Paulinus first held it,  
Who, meditating on the law of the Lord day and night,  
Assidulously scattered the precepts of salvation among the peoples.  
Wherefore he converted many thousands to Christ;  
And flashing with the flames of faith and the fire of virtue,  
He drove away the northern chills for twice three years,  
During which the same illustrious King Edwin had reigned,  
And arranged his laws with a just governance,  
Enticing them to keep the faith by both gifts and threats,  
And founded ample churches in his cities:  
Of which that noble one of York, supported by solid columns,  
Remains beautiful in its high glory,  
In which station he was washed in the sacred wave,  
And as long as he lived, he held the precepts of Christ.  
Wherefore clement God was preparing to deliver better kingdoms  
To him, joined to perennial light;  
For when the hour of his prescribed death had come to him,  
The warlike man suddenly fell by the arms of allies,  
O too blind a thing it is to trust in an earthly kingdom,  
Which headlong fortune rotates, and by malignant fates  
Is overturned, and is always shifted in changing hours.  
Behold, after he had reigned for seventeen years,  
Edwin fell, that most illustrious of kings.  
After him, glorious Britain did not have such a man.  
Yet the Almighty did not suffer this to happen unavenged,  
But granted Oswald, the nephew of the king, to reign.  
Who, coming suddenly as an exile from foreign shores,  
Firmly trusting in the arms of invincible faith,  
Draws small bands, hastening, and proceeds against the enemy  
Who was devastating the homeland, burning it with iron and flames,  
With countless thousands, and exceedingly proud with spoils.  
But pious Oswald, not terrified by any number,  
Addresses his own troop with a constant heart:  
"O you in whom there is always a vivid virtue of wars,  
Now, I pray, take up invincible strength in your minds,  
And seek the help of God, more outstanding than all arms,  
With a pious heart; with prayers, lower your  
Faces before the cross, which on this summit of the mountain  
I have raised, which shines bright with the trophy of Christ,  
Which also will now grant us a triumph over the enemy."  
Then the shout of the praying people is carried above the stars.  
And so before the cross, the powerful Lord and God,  
On bent knees, the whole army adores.  
When these things were done, he promptly advanced against the enemy,  
Bursting into the hostile camps with bloody slaughters.  
Just as a cruel lion with its cubs devastates the sheepfolds,  
And fierce, slaughters, devours, and drags away all the cattle;  
In no other way did King Oswald everywhere lay low the barbarian  
Phalanxes, marching as a victor through weapons, through enemies,  
He cuts down and tramples them, and wears down their fleeing wings.  
Oswald's army anticipated the enemies by laying them low,  
Leaving bloody rivers behind their backs upon the plains,  
Until the abominable Cadwallon himself fell, paying  
The penalties of his treachery, dying in the slaughter of his own men,  
And the bright victory yielded to the magnificent king.  
The enemies having been slain, the most holy Oswald enters  
The kingdom—a hero fully worthy of his ancient ancestors.  
A man powerful in virtue, a protector and lover of his country,  
Outstanding in morals, having followed the commandments of Christ,  
Generous to the poor, sparing to himself, rich toward all,  
Truthful in judgments, benign in the piety of his mind,  
Exalted in merits, yet submissive in that very mind,  
Terrible to all enemies, delightful to all friends,  
As untamed in war, so faithful in agreed peace.  
After he grew strong in the scepters and summit of the kingdom,  
He builds up churches and adorns them with rich gifts,  
Providing precious vessels for the sacred ministries;  
He clothed the altars with silver, gems, and gold,  
Stretching silken coverings along the sacred walls,  
Beautifully adorned with golden borders and crowns,  
And he hung various lamps throughout the holy roofs,  
So that there might be in the temples an image of the starry heaven,  
And devoutly he led Christ-worshipping flocks into them,  
So that there might be praises to the Lord from those singing without end.  
O piety, o lofty faith! For whatever treasures he had,  
Lavishly he scattered them for the honor of the Lord:  
Wherefore he shone with titles of virtues, and  
He became known everywhere by the celebrated fame of his signs ,  
Which are now read inscribed upon sheets throughout the world—  
A few of which it is pleasing now to touch with a lyrical plectrum,  
And to recount portions of the song with a running pen.  
For at a certain time, the most holy bishop  
Celebrated the Easter feasts with the king and the people  
(For the king served his counsels in every action);  
A very large crowd of the helpless was then lying along the streets,  
Asking for alms from the king with frequent shouting.  
Entering, both the king and the priest now recline ,  
And immediately he sent a silver dish—weighing a great many  
Pounds beforehand—together with the feasts to the needy;  
When the bishop saw this, he grasped his right hand, and said:  
"I pray that this hand may remain uncorrupted through all time."  
Which was indeed done; for when the holy king was destroyed,  
They hang the right hand, cut off from the body by a pagan sword,  
Upon a stake: the king arriving as his heir,  
Oswiu, his brother and the avenger of his brother's blood,  
Snatched the right hand, and carried it into the city of Bamburgh (Bebba),  
Storing it in a small casket of silver under the roof of the temple,  
Which he himself had previously established for God under the name of Peter.  
Up to this point, that the hand is whole is shown by the growing fingernail,  
The flexible tendon, the fresh flesh, and the beautiful form.  
How great his faith had been, or the power of his merits,  
Shone more and more everywhere after his death through signs;  
For where he fell, fighting for his country, slain by a pagan  
Nation, many miracles happen from the health-bearing dust.  
While a certain traveler by chance was making his way  
Near where the field of battle had been,  
His horse suddenly began to grow faint in all its limbs,  
And gnashing, to foam from its bloody mouth,  
And collapsing on the plain, it rolled about there on the verge of death;  
And turning, it came into the place where the pious King Oswald  
Had once fallen, and then immediately rising up with a healthy body,  
It greedily began to crop the pleasant green grass.  
The rider understood that there was something more outstanding  
About that place, and marking the spot, he rode away from there  
Coming to the lodging where he had been heading; behold, a girl,  
Wasted away by paralysis, was lying in cold weakness,  
Repeating the final sighs of her wretched life;  
And while the troubled household was mourning the granddaughter of the father,  
The guest suggests that she be led to where by chance his horse  
Had been healed; and the girl, having been placed in a cart,  
Is led, as he ordered, the traveler showing the holy places,  
And laid upon the ground, she slept there for a little while,  
And waking up, she thoroughly felt that her health was present.  
Then she asked for water, washed her face, and arranged  
Her very hair, and covered her head with a veil,  
And then, joyful, she followed her guides.  
Another man, making a journey through the places of the aforementioned battle,  
Behold, beheld a space of the place more pleasant than the whole plain,  
Unique, and more beautiful with its green grass.  
He reflects on these things with himself: "A holier man fell here,  
I think for that reason this earth is beneficial for health."  
And binding some of the dust in a cloth, he had carried it with him;  
And growing weary in the evening, he came to a certain village where  
The common people were feasting; he then came into one cottage of the village,  
Which the arriving guest, having been received, had entered,  
And he hung the cloth of dust on a high post.  
It happened that suddenly, with flying flames, a devouring fire  
Snatched the high roof, nor by any strength was it able  
To be extinguished, until the fires consumed the entire  
House; but an exceedingly wondrous thing was done:  
The fiery ardor feared to touch the post made sacred by the dust,  
But it remained completely untouched by the flames.  
Indeed, when this miracle was seen, a huge amazement  
Fell upon everyone immediately; then they recognized that that  
Dust was mixed with the sacred blood of King  
Oswald, which had been the cause of health for many.  
After that place became illustrious through these performed signs,  
And with pleasant peace restored to the churches of Christ,  
The faithful Queen Osthryth, wife of King Æthelred,  
Who was indeed the daughter of holy Oswald's brother,  
Took care to bring into sacred roofs the holy  
Relics of her uncle, and to store them with worthy honor.  
The bones having been brought, the inhabitant of Lindsey marveled widely at the miracles,  
Perceiving above those bones of the holy man a column  
Of ethereal light gleaming all through the night up to the summits of heaven.  
The larger building was sheltering the relics at that time;  
For, because of ancient hatreds, the fierce settlers, refusing at first  
To take up the bones and carry them into the monastery,  
Made them remain outside for the night;  
But when they saw the fire of divine light,  
They asked to retain with themselves what they had previously refused;  
And in the morning, washing them and storing them in a prepared sarcophagus,  
They carried them into the church under the roof with great honor,  
Taking care to store living riches in the earth.  
In which place, even until now, many gifts of health happen to the sick  
On account of the merit of so great a patron,  
If the virtue of holy faith accompanies them.  
Out of which, it is enough to touch upon this single one with a hurrying plectrum,  
So that you, devout reader, may believe the rest;  
A certain boy lay with a fever for a long time,  
Languishing in that monastery, soon about to die.  
Behold, on a certain day, the wretched boy is led to the holy places of the tomb,  
Lest the bitter fever should touch him, through the merits of Oswald—for faith commands all that it prays for:  
As he believed, the languishing boy ran back to his own roof,  
Hurrying with a healed body and a joyful heart,  
Nor did the morbid fever dare to touch him any more.  
Afterward, the fortunate King Offa adorned the sepulcur  
With silver, gems, gold, and much beauty,  
So that the glory and excellence of the tomb might remain through the ages,  
He being destined to receive great rewards for a modest labor.  
Moreover, the renowned earth shines with heavenly signs,  
Which, as I said, was washed by the washing of the holy body,  
And it took up the effect of conquering the weapons of the malignant  
Demon, and of restoring healthy senses to the vexed.  
Indeed, a certain abbess came to see the holy places,  
And to commit herself to the merits of the saint.  
And now, returning home, she had carried away some dust of the earth  
Which had absorbed the sacred wave washed from the bones;  
After some time had passed, a guest came to her,  
Who had been accustomed to be vexed by a demon, quite often in the nightly hours:  
Behold, suddenly, gnashing his teeth, he began to fill the wide places  
With horrendous shouts, and, raging, to lacerate himself with abominable bites.  
And when no one was able to bind him with chains,  
Or to restrain the savage movements of the wretched man with whips,  
Someone ran to tell these things to the abbess.  
She, seeing the wretched movements and the voices of the raging man,  
Ordered the casket with the sacred dust to be brought.  
And when the virgin came carrying it, and entered the halls of the house,  
Suddenly the furious man fell silent, and settled all his movements,  
Just as if he had given his limbs to sleep.  
Everyone standing by watched what the outcome of the matter would be.  
After the space of an hour, the vexed man even sat up by himself,  
Sighing heavily; then, restored to his healthy senses, he said,  
"The wandering enemy has fled into the empty breezes."  
All marveled with amazed talk on this side and that,  
Suddenly discerning him uninjured in his whole body,  
Vigorous in bones and tendons, and also in mind;  
They inquire after the cause of the sudden health, wondering,  
And he answered, rejoicing: "As the virgin came carrying the small box,  
And touched these halls with her feet, immediately they departed far away,  
The demons who were accustomed to torture me, just as darkness flees when light arrives."  
To him, a part of the bountiful dust was given to be carried away with him;  
And after that, such a vexation was never his.  
You also, O holy cross, powerful in virtues, renowned for many miracles,  
Venerable cross, I will sing of, even if I am unable to bring forth verses worthy  
Of your praise. You repaired things long ago lost,  
Now again through you, victory yields to King Oswald,  
As a few muses  once sang.  
From that time, your glory shone with wonderful signs;  
Famous Britain runs to you with all faith,  
To seek medicines for themselves for various diseases.  
And even when coming to you in hope, you know not how to deceive them;  
For, returning, they often carry back the riches of health.  
I, with a rustic plectrum, am not able to run through  
All the signs which are performed through you so many times,  
Both upon cattle and men, and also upon youths and old men;  
Finally, small pieces cut from you are carried away everywhere,  
Through which pious gifts of health are made.  
It is enough out of all of them to speak of this single sign:  
There was a certain brother broken by a bitter accident,  
And grieving, he groans deeply from the bones of his broken upper arm.  
And when the too great pain had increased, and a burning tumor  
Of the chest doubles the weeping sighs of the weary man,  
Someone brought to him some old moss from the cross, now in the evening,  
Which the sick man throws into his own lap:  
And when he went to bed, having forgotten to put down the moss,  
He unconsciously held it in his lap, overtaken by sleep;  
Waking up in the middle of the night, full of disease,  
Behold, he felt something cold to be present at his side,  
And his hand having been moved there, he found that by chance he was healthy,  
And he felt nothing from that fracture.  
The renowned fame of the man, not only by right illuminates  
The British peoples, but moreover, across the waters of the sea,  
It scattered rays by which Germany also shone:  
And of which populous Ireland also felt certain ones.  
But it pleases us to narrate this miracle, out of many, by a true report;  
While at the time of a dire plague,  
Which destroyed the ocean-dwelling peoples with a wide slaughter,  
A scholar, keen in art, of the race of the Scots ,  
Had been struck by this disease in that homeland,  
Learned in the study of books, but no care for the future  
Life had been in the wretched man; and when he saw for himself  
That the day of death was present, he began to tremble with great fear  
On account of the merits of his crimes, lest after dying he should be snatched  
To the dire tartarus of hell; with a groaning voice  
He addresses his companion: "Brother, the moment of bitter death  
Hastens for me, and I shall quickly be led to tartarus  
Of perpetual death, because for no small time already  
I was accustomed to serve entirely so greatly my offenses.  
Oh, what grief! On account of these things I know that a deadly tartarus,  
With flames, has been prepared for me. Hence it is with all my thoughts,  
If the Almighty should wish to grant life to a wretched man,  
That with exerted strengths I would change my malignant habits.  
And yet I know that it is not of my own merit to live so much,  
Unless the grace of merciful Christ, through the merits of the saints,  
Should now grant to me the gift of a beloved life.  
I heard once, recounted by celebrated rumor,  
The ample praises of King Oswald for his virtues.  
Because you are born a colleague of the race of the Saxons,  
Perhaps you have something now of his relics?"  
To whom his companion immediately speaks into his trusting ear:  
"A piece of the sacred trunk of wood has been found with me,  
Upon which the head of that slain man had been fixed;  
And if you hold firm the thoughts of faith, believing,  
The divine piety, through the merit of so great a patron,  
Will yet grant to you long times of this life;  
Moreover, it will perhaps provide the joys of the eternal one."  
There was no delay, but he said that he believed with his whole heart.  
Then the companion blessed water, and throwing in a part of the sacred  
Oak, he gave it to the sick man to drink.  
Who soon recovered, and was snatched from that death.  
From that time, he lived uninjured now for a long time,  
And had turned his entire life to the Lord,  
And was rendering magnificent praises to God everywhere,  
And was celebrating the servant of the Lord with great honor.  
After holy Oswald happily ruled the reins of empires for thrice three years,  
 those nations which famous Britain herself contains,  
Divided by tongues and by peoples according to the names of their fathers;  
And after he had lived for thirty and eight years,  
He dedicated the August Nones with his holy death;  
He ascended to the heavenly kingdoms by his gleaming merits,  
Leaving the earthly kingdoms to his brother Oswiu.  
Meanwhile, the new ruler Oswiu obtained the scepters with much labor  
In the beginning, as the leader of his own nation;  
For he had been attacked on this side and that in turn,  
Quite often by foreigners plundering the borders everywhere.  
Moreover, he endured wars from his own kinsmen,  
Who with cruel hand were tearing the vitals of the kingdom,  
Filling their wicked right hands with kindred blood;  
Nor, driven by fierce envy, do they fear to lead pagan camps  
Through native cities, attempting even thus  
To overthrow the king with external forces.  
To whom, before all others from his earliest years,  
King Penda was a hostile enemy, strong in valor and deceit,  
The slayer of his brother, and the bitter devastator of the kingdom;  
Who hired thirty thousand warriors for himself,  
And arranged for thirty leaders to lead just as many troops,  
Whose skill in arms was certain.  
And coming to devastate and overturn the kingdom with this force,  
He destroyed walls and destroyed men with the swordpoint.  
Just as a torrent overflowing with rains from high mountains  
Lays low the fields, sweeps away the crops, and cuts down the woods;  
Thus that fierce leader, devastating all things, weighed heavily,  
Giving over to destruction boys, young men, and old men alike.  
No sex, or no age had held him back  
To a work of piety; because he spared by no law.  
But the ruler, whose care was to defend the nation,  
And to protect himself and his own at the same time with the arms of Christ,  
Gathered brave men himself, and against such great bands,  
The king came forward prompt, with a heart by no means trembling,  
With but a sparse soldiery, but first he called upon the name of Olympus   
With tears and vows and a constant heart.  
When these things were done, as he also saw the hostile,  
Innumerable battle-lines running to and fro everywhere on the wide plains,  
Against these he opposed his own troop, small though undaunted,  
Summoning only three thousand men, but prompt for the duel;  
And there was no delay, but bold he cast himself into the midst of the enemies,  
Overthrowing their battle-lines, relying on the shield-wall of Christ.  
Soon fear scattered the hard-pressed wings of the peoples,  
Who, unmindful of the fight, with their arms and weapons abandoned,  
Sought safe places of flight, and tore up their own standards.  
The victorious king cuts down the wedges of the fleeing everywhere,  
Chiding the retreating phalanxes with a horrific voice.  
Weapons swim in gore, the springs are changed with blood;  
Leader Penda himself also fled at the same time, scarcely forced by so great a slaughter,  
Discerning the killings and downfalls of his own men:  
Yet fleeing, he did not prevail to evade death,  
But he too fell, slain by a victorious sword.  
There is a rush for booty everywhere, and worthy praise is rendered  
To the eternal Thunderer, who, clement, always delivers all  
Hoping in Him, and saves them everywhere.  
This was, this was indeed a war sufficiently useful to many:  
For the king rescued his own nation from sharp enemies,  
And subjected the Mercians to the bountiful scepters of the faith,  
Causing them to be washed in the sacred river of baptism;  
And through him, by God's gift, each  
People is enriched by the magnificent reward of a heavenly gift;  
The former made an heir to the homeland, the latter a citizen of Olympus .  
Both nations shone with a twin triumph,  
The one loosed from the yoke of the demon, the other from an earthly enemy.  
Oswiu subdued a great many kingdoms with continuous triumphs after this,  
And nobly ruled his ancestral reins,  
And subjected the ocean-dwelling nations to himself on this side and that,  
Pressing these with terror, restraining those with the swordpoint,  
He carried victorious eagles everywhere through the kingdoms.  
He was also most just with equal laws,  
Invincible in wars, and also faithful in peace.  
Generous with gifts to the wretched, pious, fair to all;  
Retaining the empire for indeed four times seven years,  
With affairs settled, he rested happy in peace,  
Handing over the diadems to his own son Ecgfrith,  
Dying, leaving the royal scepters to Ecgfrith.

For at that time, Bishop Wilfrid shone forth,  
Whom almighty God infused with heavenly light,  
So that he might drive the foul shadows of errors from the lands,  
Wherefore through many places he scattered the lights of doctrine  
Of everlasting salvation to nations and peoples.

For at that time, during three continuous years,  
Neither dew nor rain irrigates the thirsting fields.  
The arid earth wastes away under the flame-bearing stars,  
And equally denied sustenance to both man and beast.  
And a heap of destruction of the dying follows the famine,  
And many headlong cast themselves down from high cliffs.  
But others plunged themselves under the rapid waves,  
So that a swift death might snatch away long torment from them.  
For on the very day on which that nation had received,  
Imbued with sacred doctrines, the wave of baptism,  
Rain descended, and a serene air upon the lands,  
And its beauty returned to the earth with greening fields,  
The plains and mountains are adorned with a flower-bearing cloak:  
A fruitful year is granted to the rejoicing farmers,  
And the flesh and hearts of all exulted in the living God,  
(Just as David sang before)  
Desiring more certainly with their breast eternal gifts,  
In the place where they first received earthly ones through him.  
He too, having been compelled, was hastening to go to Rome,  
But first he was carried by the winds into the shores of the Frisians,  
And there soon converted many thousands of the people to Christ,  
Demonstrating a great many gifts of everlasting salvation.  
And wherever he moved his foot, he sowed pious seeds,  
Drenching dry breasts with heavenly dew,  
And filled renowned granaries with heavenly fruit,  
And is praised with celebrated fame in the joyful world.  
While the pious priest eagerly desires to complete that journey,  
Behold, suddenly he was struck by a sharp disease,  
And through many days, with the powerful pain increasing,  
He came to the very end with a worn-out body,

Without any sense of the body, and deprived of breath,  
Lacking a voice, and paralyzed in his limbs, and in his chest alone

To whom the almighty Judge wishes, He will be able to become gentle,  
And to turn the final life into a new beginning.

Were ascending and falling equally with the flames,

Inasmuch as I was ignorant of what I should do, where I could turn my step,

And also a cackling as if of a crowd over a captured enemy;

Dragging five howling souls to punishments,

With flaming eyes, and they had surrounded me,  
Breathing foul fire from their nostrils and from their ignited mouths,  
And they threaten to seize me with pincers;  
Yet they were not yet able to touch me so much, though they had power to terrify me greatly.

Looked around with my eyes, if by chance any help might come  
By which I might now be saved from the bloody enemy.  
Then behind my back it shone through the shadows just like a star,  
Which, growing larger and hastening, had put the enemies to flight;  
It was my guide arriving suddenly with light,  
At whose arrival the black demons fled.

And with the light there was a fragrance of wondrous odor,  
So that on account of it, the first one was thought by me to be very small.

My guide himself halted and turns back his step,  
And by the path through which we came, he led me back from there,  
And entering again the beautiful places of the first plain,  
He inquires if by chance I knew all that I saw:  
I said to him, "I know not," and immediately he added these things:  
"You saw the valley filled with flames and frost,  
In which souls are now purged by sharp punishments,  
And they return again, having been cleansed, to the rewards of life.  
But indeed, the pit which belches out foul fire  
Is the mouth of hell; for whoever by chance casts himself  
Into it shall never after this be saved from there.  
This flower-bearing place, which the white youth possess,  
Is the rest in which they await the heavenly kingdoms—  
Those who did good things, although in part less than  
Bountiful faith demands; for he who is perfect everywhere,  
As soon as he dies, shall penetrate into the court of heaven.  
To whose vicinity pertains that place, flashing  
With excessive light, and also full of wondrous odor,  
From which the most sweet voice of those singing was resonating.  
Because you must now take up your body again,  
And live a mortal life among men,  
Correct, I pray, your habits and words or deeds,  
So that your holy dwelling may be accounted among these crowds."  
When he was saying these things—though how, I was unable to know—  
I suddenly saw myself clothed in my own body."

Out of whom was he called by the surname of Holy,  
Bishop Ecgberht, who in the first years of his age  
Had left his homeland for the love of the heavenly homeland,  
And seeking foreign places, he was then giving the greatest examples of life  
To the Scots , gleaming with the lamp of doctrine,  
Instructing through deeds whomever he was teaching with words,  
Generous to the poor, but always destitute to himself.  
Thus as a good man, he led the guidance of an illustrious life,  
Gleaming with bright piety until the day of death.  
To whom Wibert was a companion, well-suited in merits and morals,  
And a comrade of his exile, sufficiently renowned in all  
Religion, but afterward separated from him,  
He alone was leading a rigorous, contemplative life.  
From there, he built an excellent sheepfold for the monks of his own nation,  
Adorning it with the merits and morals of life, eager for nourishing the sheep of Christ;  
He himself, devout, led them along a narrow path by a straight route  
To the pastures of the celestial kingdom.  
Therefore, he shone with miracles, and in the manner of a prophet,  
Seeing many future things, he remained renowned everywhere;  
Afterward, he penetrated the joys of the heavenly life.  
But others, carried by ships across the eastern sea,  
Sought pagan soil, where they were attempting to scatter the words of salvation  
By sowing them into rustic hearts.  
Just as that illustrious Bishop Willibrord was,  
Who had gained a great many thousands of the people of the Frisians for Christ  
By his heavenly admonitions, and  
Adorned the pontifical glory over many years,  
And constructed many churches for God in that same place,  
Appointing priests in them, and ministers of the word;  
Completing all things, he rested happy in peace.  
Behold, two priests had followed his examples,  
Kindled by an excessive fervor of faith;  
Each called by one and the same name, Hewald.  
An equal work of life belonged to both, but also one and the same end.  
This one was white, that one black—the only difference being in their hair;  
Yet the black one was more studious in books than the white one.  
Therefore, these men were entering the crossroads of the pagan common people of the Saxons,  
To see if they could acquire any for Christ;  
But when by chance the wretched pagans recognize the new customs and habits of the faith,  
They fear lest the entire worship of their ancient gods  
Should now crash down too quickly.  
Having suddenly snatched them, they killed them with a cruel death:  
They slaughter the White one immediately with a bloody swordpoint,  
But they seek out long torments for the hard-enduring Black one,  
And they cast the bodies of the slain beneath the waves of the River Rhine,  
Which, however, soon by a wondrous order  
Were carried against the exceedingly powerful currents of the river,  
And the wave-borne bodies swam for up to thousands  toward their companions.  
Moreover, in whatever place the bodies arrived at night,  
A massive ray of light flashed from there above the stars.  
And even those who had previously killed the saints behold this light  
Gleaming always, every night.  
Furthermore, one of them was seen at night in a vision by one of their companions,  
To whom he said: "You are able to find our bodies immediately  
In the place where you see the light radiating from the heavens."  
Then that vision did not deceive the companions,  
For the bodies were found in those very places,  
And they are buried with worthy honor for the pious martyrs.  
But other and yet other ministers of the Word from the aforementioned nation  
Were in those parts of the world.  
Out of whom the illustrious priests Suitbert and Wira  
Shone forth in their own times, who had been renowned at the summit  
Of virtues, all of whom it has not pleased us to touch upon in our song.  
For now it is right for the Muse to return to the city  
Of York, from which she has receded far,  its highest  
Bishops; and to leave behind the succession of kings  
Who after Aldfrith varied the times of the kingdom—  
He having discharged his empire for ten and nine  
Orbits of years alike, who afterward under a time of peace,  
Appeared to his fathers, resting by his ultimate lot.  
Meanwhile, as Bosa was seeking the happy kingdoms,  
That most illustrious man John takes up the governance of the Church—  
A man in piety, faith, merits, and mind,  
The peak of the pontificate, the model of the ancient Fathers.  
Pouring rivers of doctrine from a pure breast,  
From which he intently irrigated the living meadows;  
Whom the honor of virtue accompanies with manifest signs,  
A few of which it is pleasing to commemorate in our muses .  
While the pious Father himself was observing the holy fasts,  
Accompanied by honorable men, he seeks out a small enclosure for men,  
So that he might pluck heavenly flowers in his soul,  
And pay the tithes of his own life to the Lord,  
From which he might find rich treasures in heaven.  
Entering therefore a place suited for rigorous habits,  
He then ordered the helpless to be sought through the nearest crossroads,  
So that he through himself might bring sustenance to the destitute poor.  
Then a sick and mute youth is led to him,  
Who was able to bring forth words by no speech at all,  
Whose head had been covered with a horrible roughness ,  
And instead of hair, the skin remained clothed in blemishes.  
To this needy youth, the pious bishop built a small hut,  
In which that wretched man might receive his accustomed alms.  
And after seven days of the elapsed time had passed,  
He ordered him to be brought forward, and to show his mute tongue;  
Upon which, by marking it, he impressed the figure of the holy cross,  
And commanded him to bring forth his long-silent speech.  
He, swifter than speech, having followed the precepts of the Father,  
Broke his quiet silence, speaking with a ready mouth,  
And he who was mute spoke thoroughly with full speech.  
And through the whole day, equally with the following night,  
Rejoicing, he did not cease to bring forth various speech,  
And to lay open the secret thoughts of his mind;  
And together with his voice, his skin now returned healthy as well;  
And new hairs are restored with curled hair;  
And the youth was made beautiful, and prompt in speech.  
And thus, healed, he was going to his own home rejoicing.  
Nor does it weary  to remember another praiseworthy sign:  
While the shepherd was surveying the sheepfolds with watchful protection,  
He came to visit the cell of the holy handmaidens,  
From whose number a certain young virgin was lying down,  
Whose vein had recently been cut in the middle of her upper arm,  
And her hand grew stiff with too great a swelling thickening.  
Therefore, the girl was seeming to be about to die quite soon;  
But the holy bishop restored her to health by the power of the Lord—  
Who, asked by the mother, was entering the roofs  
Of the virgin, and according to custom salutes her as she lay,  
And pouring out prayers, he had blessed the sick hand.  
Immediately the girl grew strong as the pain fled,  
And in a wondrous manner, as the bishop was departing outside,  
The plague receded entirely from her whole body.  
Straightway snatched from great dangers, the virgin sings praises  
To the High-Throned One, destined to live through many years.  
Another sign succeeded this one in a similar order.  
Behold, a certain count calls upon the by right venerable John,  
So that he might dedicate the roofs of a church to the Lord.  
His wife had been infirm for many days,  
Lying down for forty nights, weighed down by pain,  
Chilled, nor did she have the strength to raise herself from the marriage-bed;  
A cold pallor had covered her lurid face,  
A thin breath was scarcely blowing through alternating nostrils.  
To her, the pious bishop had sent blessed water,  
With which he had previously consecrated that church to the Lord,  
So that she might drink it, and smear her grieving limbs.  
When she had done this, medicine follows through her limbs,  
And under the health-bearing draft, the diseases perished,  
And the granted power of desired health came,  
And the woman then immediately rises safe from her couch,  
And with renewed strengths, she carries cups  
To the holy priest, and active, ministers to all.  
And she returned thanks to God together with her own husband.  
But another count, at a different time, caused him to be summoned  
To dedicate a church of the Lord according to custom,  
Whose boy, by chance subdued by a deadly plague,  
Remained dead in every part of his limbs;  
His spirit , except that he was moving his weary chest,  
Scarcely doubling cold sighs with a thin breath,  
For whom the count himself was then preparing the funeral rites,  
And a coffin was standing nearby, in which the body  
Was soon to be buried, since no hope of life was remaining:  
Weeping for him, the leader himself was asking the bishop  
That he might deign to bless the boy immediately,  
And to pour out prayers to the Lord for his life.  
Nor did he by right deny what the man was asking with full faith—  
The pious and clement man—but immediately visits the sick youth,  
And blessed him, and returning, salutes him according to custom:  
Saying, "Recover soon, boy, and resume your strengths."  
After these things, when the bishop and the leader were sitting down to dine,  
The sick youth, thirsting, requests that cups be brought to him,  
To whom the leader, rejoicing that he was already able to drink,  
Soon sent a chalice of wine blessed by the bishop;  
As he drank this, immediately he arose healthy, and was walking,  
And entered the house where the leader and the bishop were eating.  
And when he declares to them that he wishes to drink and eat,  
He drinks and eats, sitting joyful while they were rejoicing,  
And after these things he lived safe for many years.  
The bishop also made a journey on horseback with his clergy  
Through a certain level plain of a field suited for racing;  
Then the young men burn to contend in a race of horses,  
But the pious bishop specifically forbade one  
Of his companions, lest he should mix himself in the empty game.  
He, however, contrary to the prohibition, petulantly loosened  
His reins, trusting in his horse, and bursts forth into the middle of the plain;  
Therefore, while that fiery horse leaps over a certain hollow,  
The youth, having slipped from the powerful effort,  
Fell upon a stone, which by chance level with the sand  
Lay hidden in the middle of the field, covered beneath the turf,  
Nor was any other stone found in that field;  
Against this he struck his head and hand by a bitter accident,  
And loosed the joints of his skull, and with his brain  
Shattered, he lay now devoid of all his senses:  
And he was moribund, without any movement of his body.  
Indeed, it was then around the seventh hour of the day,  
And half-dead he is carried away to a house by those companions.  
But the watchful priest was persisting in prayers through the night;  
And having returned, he came to the sick youth early in the morning,  
And with his hand placed upon his head, he blessed him.  
And addressing him by his own name, he calls out;  
Therefore, just as if he were rising from a heavy sleep,  
Opening his eyes, he answered his beloved Father,  
And he recovered quickly, with his strengths restored, and  
The next day he rode on horseback, proceeding rejoicing with his dear bishop.  
As they report, this John performed many other signs,  
Which by the law of brevity it has not pleased us to recount now.  
We have spoken here only of those things which Master Bede set down  
With indubitable faith, weaving the nations and deeds of the Angles  
From their first origin in his historical report.  
After that aforementioned bishop grew old,  
While still living, he yielded the seat of honor to another,  
And sought out a monastery with a devout heart,  
And completed there a life fully worthy of God.  
And then at last leaving behind the foreign rights of the earth,  
He was restored as a proper heir to his heavenly homeland.  
The illustrious priest Wilfrid succeeded John by right—  
A most worthy heir to the bountiful Father.  
Who previously had been the vice-lord and abbas of York:  
But afterward, relying on a great summit of merits,  
He took up the fully worthy honor of the highest pontiff,  
And adorned his rank with merits and bountiful morals.  
For he added a great many ornaments with beautiful titles  
To the holy church; he, who by right made silver vessels  
Of reddish beauty, fit for the sacred ministries,  
And had covered the altar with plates of silver, and crosses  
With gilded plates: and unwilling to hide his treasures,  
The prudent bishop expended them for divine honor.  
The pious bishop, while performing these things in the city of York,  
Adorned other churches with rich gifts,  
Nor did he manage with less care from a pious heart  
To multiply his flocks, having followed the commandments of the Lord,  
By the warnings of his doctrine and by his gleaming examples.  
These he was nourishing with feasts of the mind, but those with feasts of the flesh:  
These he cherishes with heavenly things, those he enriches with fleshly ones,  
With mouth and hand alike, generous with gifts to both,  
A ruler driving the work of piety with a double governance;  
Accepted by all, venerable, honest, and loved.  
But after that good pastor had completed his deeds  
In the churches, he sought out a special enclosure,  
Where he might now be free to serve God with his entire mind:  
Giving himself in all things to the contemplative life,  
He left behind the various empty cares of the world.  
And although he was residing on earth in his very body,  
Yet with his whole mind he was already remaining in Heaven (Olympus),  
Watchful, awaiting the rewards of the heavenly life,  
Which, when his present life was finished at a fixed time,  
He received, carried across into heaven in angelic arms.  
When he laid down the weights of this pastoral care,  
He handed over the rights of the venerable chair to Ecgberht,  
Whom he made to succeed him as the highest pontiff.  
This Ecgberht was begotten from a royal lineage;  
From a root of noble parents before the world,  
But before the Lord, more illustrious by his bountiful merits.  
Rich in the wealth of the earth, which he scatters to the destitute poor,  
So that he might become richer while he collects them in heaven.  
Who, devout, was always managing the cares of the helpless,  
Bestowing treasures upon the poor with a devout heart,  
Which, by losing them on earth, he was storing up for himself in Heaven (Olympus).  
He was a most illustrious ruler of the Church, and  
An excellent teacher, venerable to all the people,  
Chosen in his morals, affable to the just, and  
Fierce against the wicked, at once gentle and severe.  
He divided both the nights and the days into sacred turns,  
Active, praying assiduously through the spacious nights,  
Celebrating the holy solemnities of Masses by day,  
And he provided many ornaments in the houses of God.  
Those  he clothed in silver, gems, and gold,  
Hanging silken veils with foreign figures.  
And he himself consecrated honest ministers for the altars,  
Who might celebrate the feasts of the Thunderer in various orders,  
And he made others sing together with the reed  of David,  
Who might resonate hymns to the Lord in modulated turns.  
His very brother, nurtured in Tyrian purple,  
Eadberht, had taken up the royal scepters of the nation.  
He expanded the borders of his own kingdom,  
Quite often subduing hostile phalanxes with terror.  
The times of this nation were fortunate then,  
Which the king and the bishop were ruling with harmonious law;  
The latter  the laws of the Church, that king the business of the kingdom:  
The latter carries upon his shoulders the pallium sent by the apostolic ;  
The former lifts upon his head the diadems of his ancient fathers;  
The former strong, the latter pious; the former strenuous, the latter benign,  
Preserving the laws of brotherly peace in turn,  
Each brother being happily aided by the other.  
The latter ruled the Church for thirty and four years,  
The former held the scepters of his parents for thrice seven years.  
Both fortunate in merits, they were buried in peace.  
In the first times of this aforementioned bishop,  
The priest Bede, extraordinary in merits, by surname,  
Seeking the stars, closed the lights of his present life.  
Who soon from boyhood attached himself, intent upon books,  
And served sacred studies with his whole heart.  
Inasmuch as the care of his parents made him, at seven years old,  
Enter the narrow cloisters of the monastery of Jarrow (Girven),  
Over which the already illustrious Abbot Ceolfrith was in charge;  
Who, seeking foreign shores, led by the love of Christ,  
Died as an exile within the borders of the city of Langres (Linguana),  
And there, fortunate, was buried with fully worthy honor.  
Whose body, after a long time, was found  
Entirely incorrupt, and was brought back thence to his homeland.  
Therefore, Bede, nurtured in that monastery,  
Adorned his tender years with illustrious morals.  
For, wise to learn or always to write, the youth  
Fervently pressed on, laboring with no sluggish mind:  
And progressing thus, he became a master by right.  
Wherefore the illustrious teacher produced a great many short works,  
Explaining the obscure volumes of Holy Scripture,  
And he also composed an art of Metres ;  
Concerning Time also  a volume with wondrous reasoning,  
Which contains the courses, positions, times, and laws of the stars.  
He wrote historical books as well in elegant speech,  
And sang a great many songs with a versifying plectrum.  
In deed, mind, and faith, he followed the footsteps of the ancient Fathers  
By a straight path, as long as he lived.  
Indeed, what sort of life this master had before,  
Was revealed after his death by a bright sign of salvation.  
For while a certain sick man is girded with the relics  
Of the bountiful Father, he was entirely healed from that plague.  
Touching you also, Balthere, with a Pierian  plectrum,  
And marking out this place for you, O saint, in our verses,  
We beg: hold it with a peaceful mind,  
And guide now our bark through the blue waters of the sea  
Away from the monsters of the deep, and amidst the rocky waves,  
So that, carrying , it may be able to reach a safe harbor.  
There is a place surrounded on all sides by the wavy sea,  
And enclosed by horrendous cliffs and a steep border,  
In which Balthere, a warrior powerful in war within an earthly body,  
Quite often was conquering the airy troops ,  
Which were varying wars against him in manifold shapes.  
He, however, intrepid, dashed back the hostile camps,  
The warlike man always opposing the weapons of the malignant ones  
With the blessed arms of the cross, the helmet, and the shield of faith.  
Indeed, while that pious man at a certain time alone  
Bent over his prayers, meditating only upon heavenly things:  
Suddenly he heard a horrible noise and a crash at the same time,  
Just like that of a crowd bursting forth against enemies.  
Then the soul of a certain man fell before his feet  
From the clouds above, trembling with too great a fear;  
Which a threatening crowd soon followed with immense horror,  
Wishing to torture the wretched soul with various punishments:  
But that pious Father snatched it into his lap with gentle embraces,  
And immediately inquires from it,  
Who it was, why it was fleeing, or what evils it had done? To whom it then  
Answered: "I was a deacon , but with a malignant mind  
I merely embraced a woman's breast with my hands,  
And living in the flesh, I blushed to confess the fault.  
For this reason, now, fierce enemies follow with harsh incursions  
To torture me throughout thirty days.  
Neither have I been captured, but nor have I remained secure."  
Then one out of the enemies, shouting, was terrifying him:  
"You shall not escape today, not even if you are held in the arms  
Of Peter, but, most wicked one, you shall suffer your merited punishments."  
But the saint, growing angry on account of the insults to Peter,  
Says these things: "Behold, I am a hundred times minor in merits than that  
Apostolic prince; but trusting in the piety of the Thunderer,  
I say to you, grim and savage tyrant,  
You shall not carry it away into hell with you today."  
Then the pious intercessor is prostrated upon the ground, and  
With tears, he supplicates the Lord for that fault.  
Nor does he cease to pour out sacred prayers before  
He had seen with his own eyes the soul being carried  
Higher, through angelic arms, above the stars of heaven.  
The clemency of Christ also carried out a sign concerning this pious Father  
Equal and comparable to an ancient miracle.  
For just as Peter had trodden upon the ocean waves,  
So it happened to him; for walking at a certain time  
On the steep border of a lofty cliff, by chance  
It occurred that he fell: but supported by the marine waves,  
He walks upon the seas with dry soles.  
And just as if he were pressing upon the country fields of the soil, so he walks upon the waters:  
Save that now the wave had received the falling man more lightly  
Than if the hard fields had accepted the tumbling man.  
While he falls, the wave flows, lest the fall should injure him.  
The fields remain for his steps, lest the sea should submerge him.  
Therefore, he walks upon a solid deep, just as on a path of land,  
Until he himself had arrived at a wave-wandering bark:  
Which as a pedestrian, along a secure path, he soon mounts.  
No moisture had clung to his clothing, no wetness to his slippers.  
What nature denies, your right hand grants, O Christ;  
By your command, the wave of the sea becomes passable to the just:  
But the earth, on the contrary, becomes a punishing whirlpool to the wicked.  
The one  devours the proud.  
But now, holy Balthere, we devoutly ask you,  
That just as the wave was carrying your body from the sea,  
Conveying you back entirely healthy to the known shores:  
So by your prayers you may cause our souls to evade the worldly  
Waves, and to enter the harbor of salvation.  
In these days also, the venerable Echa shone forth,  
A holy anchorite, having followed the secrets of the desert,  
Chaste in body, now fleeing earthly honors,  
So that he might be able to have heavenly ones with God the King.  
By devoutly leading an angelic life on earth,  
He predicted many future things with a prophetic mind.  
Concerning whom the Muse, running back to the end of the song  
And to the deeds of her own master, forbids more to be narrated;  
Who, after Ecgberht, undertook the insignia of the venerable chair—  
The wise Æthelberht (Aelbertus), called by name.  
A man good and just, generous, pious, and benign;  
A promoter, teacher, and lover of the Catholic faith;  
A ruler, teacher, defender, and foster-son of the Church,  
A cultivator of justice, the trumpet of the law, the herald of salvation,  
The hope of the helpless, and a father to orphans, the comforter of the destitute.  
Fierce to the rigid, and gentle to the good, and harsh to the proud:  
Brave in adversity, he was humble also in prosperity.  
Wise in mind, not talkative in speech: but strenuous in action.  
By as much as the summit of accumulated honor grew for him,  
By so much did his mind subdue itself in humble piety.  
Concerning him, I pray, let it please you, youth of York,  
To proceed with me a little further with a versifying step.  
Because he often drenched your senses with nectar,  
Belching forth sweet juices from a honey-flowing breast—  
Whom, soon from his first cradle, a most beautiful reason  
Seized, and was carrying him into the highest citadel of things,  
Laying open to him the secrets of the doctrine of wisdom.  
Therefore, he was born of sufficiently illustrious parents,  
By whose care he is soon delivered to bountiful studies,  
And is placed into a monastery in his boyish years,  
So that his fragile age might grow up with sacred senses.  
Nor was such great hope of his parents concerning the boy in vain.  
By as much as the extraordinary boy was already growing in body,  
By so much was he progressing in intellect through books.  
Growing thus in merits, in years, and in a wise mind,  
He was already made a holy deacon  in fully worthy order.  
While the fortunate adolescent well managed this honor,  
As an honest youth he undertook the laws of the priesthood:  
So that he might grow in ranks, who had grown in bountiful merits.  
Then a pious and prudent teacher alike as well as a priest,  
He attached himself, joined as a companion to Bishop Ecgberht,  
To whom also he was already related by the right of blood.  
By whom he is decreed as defender to all the clergy,  
And at the same time is put forward as master in the city of York.  
There, where he was irrigating thirsting hearts with diverse streams  
Of doctrine and with the varied dew of studies:  
Giving to these diligently the arts of grammatical science,  
Pouring into those the flowing floods of rhetorical tongue:  
Those he took care to polish on the juridical whetstone,  
Those he taught to sing in harmony with Aonian song;  
Training others to play the Castalian pipe,  
And to run through the ridges of Parnassus with lyrical soles.  
But others the aforementioned master made to know  
The harmony of heaven, the labors of the sun and moon,  
The five zones of the sky, the seven wandering stars,  
The laws of the stars, their risings, and also their settings.  
The aerial movements, the sea, and the trembling of the earth,  
The natures of men, of cattle, of birds, and of wild beasts,  
The diverse species of number, and various geometric figures.  
And he gave certain calculation for the solemnity of the recurring Easter,  
Most of all laying open the mysteries of Holy Scripture.  
For he revealed the abyss of the new and the old law.  
Whatever youths of extraordinary talent he saw,  
These he joined to himself, taught, nourished, and loved;  
Wherefore, through the sacred volumes, the teacher had  
Many disciples, enriched with diverse arts.  
Not once did he travel through foreign lands on a traveler's path,  
Already journeying rejoicing, led by the love of wisdom;  
If by chance he might find anything new of books or of studies  
Which he might carry back with him from those lands.  
He also, devout, came to the Romulean city,  
Rich in the love of God, widely traversing the holy places.  
Returning home from there, by kings and magistrates  
The highest teacher was received honorably everywhere.  
Inasmuch as great kings wished to retain him,  
That he might irrigate their lands, flowing with divine dew.  
But the master, hastening to the deeds preordained for him,  
With God dispensing, returned to benefit his homeland.  
For after he had been brought down into his own borders,  
Soon compelled to take up pastoral care,  
He is made highest priest, at the request of the people,  
And had adorned the office with his bountiful merits,  
And was found everywhere a good pastor of his order.  
For, cautious, he protected the divine sheepfold,  
Lest from any side the wolf should injure the lambs of Christ,  
To whom he was providing the sustenance of the sacred word;  
Lest thirst and hunger should vex them with any bitterness.  
And those wandering from the flock through the pathless places of the vast desert  
He carried back to the folds of the Lord on his friendly shoulders:  
And those unwilling to follow him as he called with gentle speech  
He pursues with the terrors of the law and with whips.  
Nor did the just man spare an unjust king or leaders.  
But neither did his burning, ancient industry of reading the Scriptures  
Decrease on account of the weights of cares:  
Having become both a wise teacher and a pious priest;  
Enriching these in their understanding, and adorning those in their morals.  
Nor had the Father, arriving into so great a summit of honor,  
Changed his ancient custom in clothing and food:  
Fleeing excessive delicacies, nor yet had he pursued  
Exceedingly cheap things, rejoicing in a middle moderation.  
Nor less meanwhile did he add ornaments with varied beauty  
To the churches, filled with the fervor of faith.  
For where the warrior King Edwin took the wave of baptism,  
The bishop had constructed a grand altar,  
And covered it with silver, together with gems and gold on all sides,  
And dedicated it under the name of Saint Paul,  
The teacher of the world, whom the teacher loved exceedingly.  
Above this altar he had suspended a tall chandelier,  
Which contains three grand vessels in nine-fold rows:  
And he erected the sublime standard of the cross at the altar,  
And covered it entirely with exceedingly precious metals.  
All things were great enough, structured with beautiful effort,  
And they balance many weights of pure silver.  
Moreover, he made another altar, and clothed it also  
With pure silver and with precious little stones:  
And he had dedicated it to the martyrs and to the cross alike.  
He ordered that from pure gold of no small weight  
A larger flask should be made, from which the priest  
Might pour wine into the chalice, celebrating the sacred solemnities.  
Furthermore, a new structure of a wondrous basilica in the days  
Of this bishop was already begun, completed, and consecrated.  
This exceedingly high house, supported by solid columns,  
Which stand beneath curved arches, inside  
Gleams with extraordinary paneled ceilings and windows,  
And shines beautiful, surrounded by many porticoes,  
Retaining a great many upper rooms under diverse roofs,  
Which contains thirty altars with various ornaments.  
Two disciples, at the command of the teacher, built this temple:  
Eanbald and Alcuin, both harmonious,  
Attending to the work with a devout mind.  
Yet the Father himself, together with his associate bishop, consecrated this temple  
To bountiful wisdom, ten days before he closed  
The ultimate lights of his present life.  
Therefore, the most illustrious administrator in the sacred order,  
A perfect bishop, and full of days in merits,  
Joyfully handed over the pontifical glory to Eanbald, his beloved pupil;  
For himself, he sought out a secret enclosure,  
Where he might now be free to serve God alone.  
But he handed over his treasures, dear above all things,  
His books, to another son, who always attached himself to his father,  
Accustomed, thirsting, to drink the streams of doctrine:  
Whose proper name, if you care to know,  
The present poems will immediately reveal on their forehead.  
To these he divided his riches in diverse portions: to the former,  
The governance of the Church, the treasures, the lands, the talents;  
To the latter, the specimen of wisdom, the study, the seat, and the books,  
Which the illustrious master had previously collected from all sides,  
Storing extraordinary treasures under a single roof.  
There you will find the footsteps of the ancient Fathers,  
Whatever the Roman contains for himself in the Latin world,  
Or whatever glorious Greece transmitted to the Latins;  
Or what the Hebrew people drank from a heavenly shower,  
Or whatever Africa scattered with light-flowing illumination.  
What Father Jerome, what Hilary perceived, and  
Bishop Ambrose, likewise Augustine, and  
Holy Athanasius himself, what ancestral Orosius brings forth;  
Whatever Gregory the highest teaches, and Leo the pope;  
Whatever Basil and Fulgentius flash with.  
Likewise Cassiodorus, and John Chrysostom;  
And whatever Aldhelm taught, what Master Bede;  
What Victorinus wrote, and Boethius; and  
The ancient historians, Pompeius, Plinius, and  
Keen Aristotle himself, and huge Tullius  the rhetorician.  
What also Sedulius, or what Juvencus himself sings,  
Alcuinus and Clemens, Prosper, Paulinus, Arator,  
What Fortunatus, or what Lactantius bring forth.  
What Maro Virgil, Statius, Lucan, and the Author ;  
Or what the masters of the grammatical art wrote:  
What Probus and Phocas, Donatus, or Priscian,  
Servius, Eutychius, Pompeius, Cominianus.  
You will find very many other masters there, reader,  
Extraordinary in studies, in art, and in speech,  
Who wrote a great many volumes with clear meaning:  
But to write their names in the present poem  
Seemed too long for what the use of the plectrum demands.  
With these things thus arranged, the highest bishop, completing his times,  
Wholly mature in merits and in years,  
After two years and months  just as many years  
From when the holy bishop sought his secret enclosure;  
In the presence of his disciples as pastor, patriarch, and master,  
Passed joyful and happily into the heavenly court.  
Yet quickly leave, my mournful pipe, leave this, this part,  
Lest you perish, submerged in a whirlpool of tears,  
While you hasten to port with sails hitherto favorable—  
Why do you eagerly commemorate the most sorrowful fates to us?  
When suddenly before all our eyes, envious death  
Closed the venerable lights in ultimate sleep  
Of the highest pontiff, our father and master.  
O woe to us! O black day! O bright day, yet  to him!  
That day left us orphans without a father,  
Weighed down by weepings, by exile, and by hard labor.  
But it restored him to his homeland and to the Father High Above,  
Loosed from weepings, from exile, and from hard labor.  
Now to whom Christ is love, drink, food, Christ is all;  
Life, faith, understanding, hope, light, way, glory, virtue.  
Who slept in the fourteenth year of the highest rank  
He had received, under the eighth sun  of November,  
While that heavy day was shining at the sixth hour.  
To his funeral rites a great multitude had run together,  
The bishop with his clergy, the people, both young men and old men,  
Taking care honorably to bury the body of the father.  
O Father, O pastor, the greatest hope of our life,  
Without you we are carried through the troubled seas of the world,  
Deserted, with you as our guide gone, we are enveloped by varying waves,  
Uncertain what sort of harbor we deserve to touch.  
As long as the sun and night yield to one another, as long as the year  
Is divided into four seasons, as long as buds grow in the earth;  
As long as the stars shine, as long as the wind drives the clouds;  
Always your honor, your name, and your praises will remain.  
Here, although I wished to close you, Thalia, with a fixed end,  
A matter carried out in our own days occurs to me.  
Therefore, although weary, sing yet a few verses,  
And gird up the case of my boy for a little while,  
To which matter it also happened that I myself was a present witness.  
Therefore, there was a certain youth nurtured in the city  
Of York, simple in mind, but fervent in action.  
And he who ruled the times of my boyish life  
With counsel, alone on a certain night,  
Persists in his accustomed prayers in the church of the Mother of Christ.  
Then a bountiful light had suddenly filled that house,  
And together with the light at the same time came a man in white garments,  
Gleaming in aspect, sublime in his honorable stature,  
And with gentle words he lifts up the youth, who was falling from too great a terror,  
And shows him an open book.  
The youth read it through, to whom, with the codex closed,  
That white-clad man said: "Knowing, you shall see greater things again."  
With these words, the bright guest suddenly disappeared.  
Hence also after some months, in no long a time,  
The youth is struck through his limbs by a running plague,  
And sick for a long time, he lay under doubtful death,  
Drawing back his perishing breath through narrow nostrils;  
And reclining in my hands, then his spirit  
Was suddenly snatched away, and his body remained empty.  
Returning after a space of time, however, he was again moving his limbs,  
And he narrated to me that someone had led him  
To places exceedingly beautiful, where he saw many rejoicing,  
Unknown and known alike, but most of all he recognized  
The joyful pupils of that holy Church.  
Who soon receive him with gentle embraces,  
And wished to retain him with themselves entirely forever,  
But quickly the guide led him back along a reversed path  
To his own body, saying: "By the rising of the sun  
You shall now be about to feel better; but another of the brothers  
Shall be about to die today, whose prepared seat  
You saw." Nor did the speech deceive the youth.  
For he quickly recovered, while the sun was glowing at its rising;  
Before midday, however, the other  was dead.  
Yet after a short time, that same youth, within the same  
Year, is struck by the plague of a devastating sorrow,  
And immediately he predicted to me during that illness:  
"By this pestilence I shall now die, quickly shall I leave the cloisters of the flesh."  
Nor did it happen otherwise, since the great force of the pain  
Increased, and led the youth down into his final hour.  
Who, dying, when he began to pass his soul with a thin breath,  
One of the brothers had been keeping watch—  
An honest and truthful man—who saw that from a high summit  
A man had descended, gleaming in his countenance or in his clothing,  
And had placed his mouth soon to the mouth of the dying youth,  
And also quite gently embraced with his hands the limbs of the one lying down:  
And turning back, releasing the soul from the prison of the flesh,  
Flying, he carried it away with himself above the stars of the heavens.  
These wares, compiled in tender vessels, I, a rude sailor,  
Steering through the waves of the sea and through blind shallows,  
Have by right brought back to the port of York;  
Inasmuch as she nourished me as her own pupil,  
And imbued me, however reverently, from my earliest years.  
For this reason, I have written these rustic songs for her  
Concerning her own Fathers, and Kings, and Saints.  
These saints likewise, whom I have touched upon in these verses,  
I pray, that by their merits and prayers they may steer our bark  
From the whirlpool of the world to the harbor of life.
