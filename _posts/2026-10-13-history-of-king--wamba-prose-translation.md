The History of King Wamba is a unique text of the Visigothic Renaissance in which the author uses the style of the Roman historian Sallust. The text has several notable characteristics:

1. It is a narrative thriller. Unlike contemporary chronicles, which largely read like lists of kings and notable events, this text features dialog, military speeches, intrigue, and betrayal, all written in elegant, classical Latin with dramatic flair. The author wanted it to be interesting and gripping.
2. It contains a prose panegyric of the visigothic King Wamba. Imperial Rome, as well as the contemporary Byzantines, had long employed literary historians and poets to eulogize the king and spread propaganda about his greatness. The visigoths seem to have learned to do this too, so the author uses his literary skill to make the king look great.
3. It is educational. The opening addresses the young visigothic noblemen who presumably learned to read Latin in part through teachers who used this narrative as a kind of textbook. The visigoths were not interested enough in classical literature to not just compose their own works in classical styles, but to teach their youths to do so too.

This translation was created in several stages. First, I divided the poem up into about 10 sections of roughly equal length. Then, I fed each section into Google Search's AI (based on Gemini), and asked it to translate each section literally. Then I combined all the sections, read through the text, and decided it was good enough to post. Below is the result, and I hope you enjoy it.

# The Text

In the name of the Lord begins the book of the history of Gaul, which was published by Lord Julian, bishop of the see of Toledo, in the time of the prince of divine memory, Wamba.
In the name of the Holy Trinity begins the history of the most excellent King Wamba, concerning the expedition and victory by which he subdued the rebelling province of Gaul in a famous triumph.
1. It is customary for the recounted narrative of triumphs to serve as a safeguard for virtue, and for whatever glory from past deeds has been proclaimed to lift the minds of young men toward the standard of virtue. For the very urgency of human conduct possesses a certain sluggishness regarding internal virtue, and for this reason, human nature is found to be less swift toward virtue than it is inclined toward vice. Unless it remains steadfast, instructed by the constant prompting of useful examples, it remains cold and grows numb. For this reason, so that the narrative of past events may heal disdainful minds, we introduce the deeds performed in our own times, through which we may incite future generations to virtue.
2. For in our days there was the most illustrious prince Wamba, whom the Lord worthily willed to rule, whom priestly unction declared, whom the community of the entire nation and fatherland elected, and whom the affection of the people sought out—he who, before reaching the pinnacle of kingship, was most famously predicted to rule by the revelations of many. When this most illustrious man was performing the funeral rites and lamentations at the death of the departing prince Recceswinth, suddenly everyone turned together in harmony. Prompted equally by a single impulse—not only of mind but of voice—they cried out that they joyfully accepted him as prince. With united voices, they thundered that he and no other should rule among the Goths, and they threw themselves in crowds at his feet so that he would not refuse their requests. Fleeing from them in every way, choked by tearful sobs, the man could not be overcome by any prayers nor swayed by any vow of the people, at one moment crying out that he would not be sufficient for so many looming ruins, and at another declaring himself worn out by old age. As he fiercely resisted, one of the leaders from the court officials, as if acting on behalf of everyone, boldly stepped into the midst, looked threateningly at him, and said: "Unless you promise us right now that you will consent, know that you will be struck down this very moment by the edge of the sword. Nor will we depart from here until either our expedition receives you as king, or this bloody stroke of death swallows you today as an opponent."
3. Overcome not so much by their prayers as by their threats, he finally yielded. Accepting the kingdom, he received everyone into his peace; nevertheless, he delayed the time of his anointing until the nineteenth day, so that he would not be consecrated as prince outside the site of the ancient see. For these events were taking place in a small estate to which antiquity gave the name Gerticos, which is located in the territory of Salamanca, about one hundred and twenty miles from the royal city. There, on one and the same day—namely, on the very Kalends [first] of September—was both the end of the departing king's life and the acclaim of the people which we mentioned above for the election of the subsequent, already named man. For although they had already surrounded him with royal honors through divine favor, the eager vows of the people, and the obedience of the high officials, he still would not allow himself to be anointed by the hands of a priest until he entered the see of the royal city and sought the throne of ancestral antiquity. In that city, it was fitting for him both to receive the banners of holy unction and to patiently await the consensus of those living far away regarding his election—namely, lest moved by a swift ambition for the kingdom, he should be thought to have usurped or stolen, rather than received from the Lord, the sign of such great glory. Delaying this with prudent seriousness, he entered the city of Toledo on the nineteenth day after he had accepted the kingdom.
4. But when they arrived at the place where he was to receive the banners of holy unction, in the praetorian church—namely, of the saints Peter and Paul—standing before the divine altar already conspicuous in royal attire, he swore his oath of fidelity to the people according to custom. Then, on bended knees, the oil of blessing was poured upon his head by the hands of the holy bishop Quiricus, and an abundance of blessing was bestowed, where immediately this sign of salvation flashed forth. For straightway from the very crown of his head where the oil had been poured, a certain vapor like smoke raised itself up in the manner of a column over his head, and from that same place on his head a bee was seen to leap forth, which surely portended the appearance of a certain future happiness. And indeed, it will perhaps not be idle to have prefaced these things, so that posterity may know how courageously he ruled the kingdom—a man who, not only unwillingly but running through such great ranks in an orderly fashion, and compelled by the impulse of the entire nation, deserved to reach the pinnacle of kingship.
5. Therefore, in his glorious times, the land of Gaul, the nurse of perfidy, is branded with an infamous mark, since vexed by an incalculable fever of unfaithfulness, it devoured the members of the unfaithful born from itself. For what was not cruel or slippery in that land, where there was held a gathering of conspirators, a banner of perfidy, obscenity of deeds, fraud in business, justice for sale, and—what is worse than all these things—a brothel of blaspheming Jews against our Savior and Lord himself? For this land, by its own birth, so to speak, prepared destruction for itself and nourished the trap of its own overthrow from the viperous offspring of its womb. And so, while it had already been afflicted for a long time by a variety of these fevers, suddenly, with the fall of one wicked head, a whirlwind of unfaithfulness arose in it, and the consensus of perfidy passed through one man to the many.
6. For report names Hilderic as the head of this tyranny; he, while managing the care of the city of Nîmes under the command of a count, took upon himself not only the name, but the title and work of unfaithfulness. He joined to himself as partners in his wickedness Gumild, the detestable bishop of the see of Maguelone, and Abbot Ranimir. Therefore, while this head of the crime was lighting the fire of his unfaithfulness through various regions, he strove to drag Aregius, the holy bishop of the city of Nîmes, into the infamy of his perfidy. Seeing him resisting his counsels with a pure mouth and a steadfast heart, Hilderic deprived him of both his rank and his office, loaded him with the weight of chains, and handed him over into the borders of France to be mocked by the hands of the Franks. Then, in place of the removed bishop, he brought in his partner in perfidy, Abbot Ranimir, as bishop. In his election, no orderly process was observed, and no decision of prince or metropolitan was awaited; instead, with an elevated arrogance of a proud mind, and against the decrees of the ancestors, he was ordained by only two bishops of a foreign nation. After the outcome of such great rashness was accomplished, the venomous seeds of perfidy of these three men—namely, Hilderic, Gumild, and Ranimir—set the boundaries for their conspiracy. From the place called Mount Cameli all the way to Nîmes, they divided the land of Gaul and drew it into their conspiracy, whereby unfaithfulness was entirely separated from faithfulness. Then, having gathered a band of men, they plundered the citizens, drained the fruits of their labors, and ravaged the entire province of Gaul.
7. This report flew to the prince, and soon an army was dispatched into Gaul under the hand of Duke Paul to extinguish the name of the rebels. This Paul, marching at a lukewarm pace with his army, broke the spirit of the army by the interval of his delays. He himself also abstained from battle, failed to direct his first impulses against the enemy, and by such design turned the minds of the young men away from the fury of fighting with which they were burning. And so, Paul was transformed into the mind of a Saul; while he refused to make progress for the sake of the faith, he instead endeavored to work against the faith. Enticed by ambition for the kingdom, he was suddenly stripped of his loyalty. He defiled the promised charity of the religious prince, forgot his obligation to the fatherland, and, as someone says: he secretly invaded and publicly armed a rapidly matured tyranny. He did this by a secret counsel, so that his targeted pinnacle of kingship might be seen before it could be known, having enticed to himself as partners in his perfidy Ranosind, the duke of the province of Tarraconensis, and Hildigisel, who was still serving in the office of a gardingus [royal bodyguard]. Intending to accomplish the vow of his perverse desire with incredible speed, so to speak, he gathered peoples from all sides and feigned that he was going to fight against the rebels. He set a day and proposed a place where they would march to fight in Gaul. When Argebad, the bishop of the see of Narbonne—a man of venerable life and fit for the care of saving the people—learned of this through the highly detailed report of certain men, he strove to close off the approach to the city against the tyrant. But this plan did not escape Paul either. Therefore, before that bishop could put his thoughts into effect, Paul suddenly entered the city of Narbonne with his army in a headlong march, early forestalling the ambush against him, and ordered the gates of the city to be locked under a delegated guard of armed men. When the surrounding multitude of the entire army was gathered there, Paul himself, the viperous head of perfidy, stood in the midst with certain of his companions, first rebuking the bishop for trying to close off his entry into the city.
8. After this, about to betray the counsel of his tyranny, he corrupted the loyalty of the people with a diverse argument of fraud, and, inflaming the minds of each individual to inflict insults upon the already mentioned Prince Wamba, Paul himself swore first to everyone that he could not have him as king, nor remain any longer in his service. Rather, he said: "Choose from among yourselves a head of government, to whom the whole gathering of the multitude may yield, and who may appear as prince among us." One of the conspirators, Ranosind, a partner in his wicked counsel, designated Paul as king for himself, and immediately wished that Paul, and no other, would soon be king for the peoples. But when Paul saw the acceleration of his own counsel, he at once granted the consent of his own will and compelled everyone to swear allegiance to him. After this, he seized the kingdom, and with wicked rashness, he drew to himself through the work of perfidy that crowd of conspirators whom he had not captured by the usefulness of arms. For with no difficult effort, he allied Hilderic, Gumild, and Ranimir to his perfidy. Why say more? All the land of Gaul suddenly conspired in the arms of sedition, and not only Gaul, but even a certain part of the province of Tarraconensis attempted the tragedy of rebellion. Thus, all of Gaul suddenly became a gathering place of the treacherous, a cave of perfidy, and an assembly of the ruined. While Paul wished to make the partners of his perfidy more numerous, by offering and promising gifts, he gathered multitudes of Franks and Basques to fight in his aid, and he remained within Gaul with a multitude of enemies, awaiting the arrival of a highly favorable time when he could march into Spain to fight and claim the usurped pinnacle of the kingdom.
9. At that time, while these things were being done in Gaul, the religious Prince Wamba, about to tame the fierce tribes of the Basques, was staying in the regions of Cantabria. When the report of what was happening within Gaul brought itself to the ears of the prince, the matter was immediately made known to the nobles of the palace to be thoroughly discussed: whether they could march from there directly into Gaul to fight, or whether they should return to their own lands, gather forces from all sides, and begin the journey of so long a march with an augmented army. Seeing many wavering in this two-sided counsel, the prince himself addressed them with this collective exhortation: "Look, young men," he said, "you have heard of the evil that has arisen, and you have recognized with what defense the instigator of this sedition has armed himself. Therefore, it is necessary to forestall the enemy, so that he may be intercepted by war before he grows in his own fire. It would be a disgrace for us either not to go out to meet those we must fight, or to march back to our homes before they perish. It should appear ignominious to us that a man who could not subject our rebels with his own arms should dare to resist men of such great glory; and he who, for the peace of the fatherland, was not strong enough to conquer the wretched hide of a single man, should dare to present himself as an enemy to a nation, judging us everywhere as if we were effeminate and soft, as though we lacked the arms, strength, and counsel to resist his tyranny. For what virtue will that doomed man have if he clashes with us by fighting with the strength of the Franks? Their manner of fighting is very well known to us and not uncertain. Therefore, it would be shameful for us to dread the shield-formation of those whose virtue you know has always been weaker. But if he relies on a conspiracy of the Gauls to maintain his tyranny, it must be considered a vile thing that a nation should yield from this extreme corner of the earth, and that those to whom an expanded kingdom extends should be disturbed by the movements of these people, whom they always defend in the place of a garrison. For whether they are Gauls or Franks, let them consider that the crime of so great a conspiracy must be avenged, if it pleases them; we, however, must avenge the name of our glory with avenging arms. For we must contend not with women, but with men, although it remains well known that the Franks can never resist the Goths, nor can the Gauls accomplish anything of great virtue without our people. If you oppose this with the necessity of provisions or vehicles, it will be more glorious for us, setting all else aside, to have gained a triumph in times of necessity than to finish chosen wars in abundance. For a man is always more august whom the strength of endurance elevates more than the abundance of things. Rise up now to the standard of victory, destroy the name of the treacherous! While the warmth of the soul is present, there must be no delay in hastening. While anger urges our minds against the enemy, no retardation should impede us; rather, if it can be done, by entering upon the undertaken journey without intermission of marching, the camps of our enemies can be overthrown much more easily. For, as a certain wise man says: present anger is effective, delayed anger grows weak. Therefore, there is no need for the soldier to turn back, whom a diligent approach to waging war makes a victor. It is not necessary to frustrate ourselves by a direct route. From here, therefore, let us advance to bring slaughter upon the Basques, and then let us hasten straightway to extinguish the name of the rebels."
10. At this speech, the minds of all grew warm and they longed for what was commanded to be done. Soon, with the entire army, he entered the regions of Vasconia [the Basque country], where for seven days, in every direction throughout the open fields, plundering and hostility by the camps and the burning of homes were carried out so powerfully that the Basques themselves, having laid aside the ferocity of their minds and given hostages, begged—not so much with prayers as with gifts—that life be given to them and peace granted. Wherefore, having accepted hostages and received tribute, and peace having been settled, he advanced to begin the direct journey into Gaul, making his passage through the cities of Calahorra and Huesca. Then, having chosen leaders, he divided the army into three columns, so that one part would advance toward Castrum Libiae [Llívia], which is the capital of Cerdanya; the second would seek the middle of the Pyrenees through the city of Osona; and the third would march along the public road next to the sea coasts. The religious prince himself, however, followed those going before with a manifold band of fighters. But because the undisciplined movement of certain of our men gasped not only for plunder, but also perpetrated the crime of adultery along with the burning of houses, the already mentioned prince avenged the crime in these and similar acts with such vigor of discipline that you would think he was inflicting heavier punishments upon them than if they had acted hostially against him. This is witnessed by the severed foreskins of certain adulterers, upon whom he inflicted this loss of vengeance for fornication. For he said: "Look! Already the judgment of war is imminent, and does it please the soul to commit fornication? And I believe you are approaching the trial of battle; see to it lest you perish in your filth. For if I do not avenge these things, I go from here already bound. For this reason I shall go, that I may be captured by the just judgment of God if, seeing the iniquity of the people, I myself do not punish it. An example ought to be provided to me by Eli, that priest recognized in the divine scriptures, who, on account of the enormity of their crimes, heard that his sons—whom he refused to rebuke—had fallen in battle, and he himself, following his sons, expired with a broken neck. These things, therefore, are to be feared by us, and for that reason, if we remain cleansed from crime, there will be no doubt that we shall capture a triumph from the enemy." Under this discipline, as was stated before, the already mentioned prince gloriously led forth his army, keeping the conduct of each individual under divine rules, and he saw both the arrangement of war and the victory of fighting prosper for himself through the increase of days.
11. First among all the cities in rebellion, Barcelona was brought into the power of the religious prince, and then Girona was subjected. For to the venerable Bishop Amator of this aforementioned city, the same pestilential Paul had sent a letter written in this sense: "I have heard that King Wamba is preparing to come against us with an army, but let your heart not be troubled by this; for I do not think this will happen. And yet, whichever of us two your holiness sees arriving there first with an army, let him believe that he has him as lord and ought to remain in his affection." The wretched man wrote these things himself and, without knowing it, pronounced a just judgment against himself. Wherefore, wisely interpreting the words of these writings, the religious prince is reported to have said: "Has Paul not spoken from his own self in these writings of his? Although he did so ignorantly, I nevertheless deem that he has prophesied here." Therefore, having departed after this from the city of Girona, marching with warlike incursions, the prince arrived at the ridges of the Pyrenees mountains. Having rested the army there for two days, he organized the defenses of the Pyrenees mountain through three divisions of the army, as was said, and he captured and subdued the Pyrenean fortresses—which are called Collioure, Vulturaria, and Castrum Libiae [Llívia]—in a wondrous triumph of victory, finding in these fortresses much gold and silver, which surrendered as plunder to his copious armies. For into the fortress called Clausurae [Les Cluses], an incursion was made by two leaders, the armies having been sent before them. There also Ranosind, Hildigisel, and the rest of the band of the treacherous, who had flocked together for the defense of that fortress, were captured; and thus, with their hands bound behind their backs, they were presented to the prince. Wittimer, however—one of the conspirators who had shut himself up, stationed in Sordonia—perceiving that our men had broken in, immediately fled and arrived to carry the news of such a great slaughter to Paul in Narbonne. This matter rendered the tyrant greatly terrified. The religious prince, however, having subjugated the army of the predicted fortresses, descended into the plains after crossing the Pyrenees mountain, waiting for only two days to gather the army into one.
12. But when the multitude of the armies, gathered into one from different directions, grew large, there was no delay in staying; but immediately, through four leaders, he sent a chosen number of warriors before his face for the storming of Narbonne, dispatching another army to approach to fight in a naval battle. And indeed, there were now only a very few days since the rebel Paul had departed from Narbonne, fleeing like a slave upon learning that the party of the religious prince was hastening with such a highly favorable outcome. Paul himself, reinforcing the power of his own right, had surrounded this city with a manifold garrison of the treacherous and had committed the command of the battle to his leader Wittimer. When the army of our men gently exhorted him to surrender the city without the shedding of blood, he utterly refused, and having locked the gates of the city, he cursed the army of the religious prince from the wall. He also redoubled his curses against the prince himself and endeavored to disturb the army with threats. The multitude of our side, not enduring this, burned with a sudden kindling of their hearts and sought the faces of the treacherous by the casting of weapons. Why say more? A monstrous battle was joined by both sides, and both parties resisted each other alternately by a turn of arrows. But when our men despaired of a quick victory, they not only transfixed with arrows the rebels fighting on the wall, but also shook such heavy showers of stones into the city that the city itself was thought to be submerged by the clamor of voices and the rattling of rocks. Wherefore, from about the fifth hour of the day until the eighth hour of that same day, it was fought fiercely by both sides. But when the minds of our men grew warm, they could not bear the delay of victory, but approached closer to the gates to fight. Then, by the victorious hand through God, they set fire to the gates, leaped upon the walls, and entered the city as victors, in which they subjected the rebels to themselves. While Wittimer, with an still armed hand, sought the church, disturbed by the approach of our men, the wretched man testified that he would defend himself behind the altar of the blessed Virgin Mary—not out of reverence for the place, but with an avenging sword, holding his sword in his right hand and threatening the death of everyone. Therefore, to suppress the swelling of this madness straightway, one of our men among the others threw down his arms, snatched a wooden board in his hand, and directed himself toward him with a most ferocious blow. But when he sharply endeavored to strike him with a huge cast of the board, Wittimer was soon cast down trembling upon the earth and was immediately captured, and the iron weapon was wrenched from his hand. Soon, having been dragged away vilely, he was confined by the weight of chains and afflicted with lashes together with the companions with whom he had endeavored to defend the city.
13. After this, the city of Narbonne having been conquered and subjugated, the march was directed to pursue Paul, who had betaken himself to be defended in Nîmes. Then the cities of Béziers and Agde were immediately subjugated. In the city of Maguelone, however, Gumild, the bishop of that place, when he saw the army surrounded for his siege and the city itself encircled not so much by those who had arrived to fight by land as by those who had journeyed through the seas to wage naval battle, terrified by the calamity of this matter, took the shortest route and betook himself to Nîmes as a companion to Paul. But when the army of Spain perceived that Gumild had fled, they soon captured the city of Maguelone with a similar victory. But when our men, with a direct line of battle, contended to subdue the city of Nîmes, the first face of the fighting was dispatched through four leaders with a chosen band of fighters, whose select youth preceded the prince by about thirty miles. These men, in a noble advance into Nîmes—where Paul had betaken himself to fight with the army of Gaul or the assembly of the Franks—forestalled the ambushes of the rebels. When they had finished the course of their hastened march through the whole night, suddenly, when the light of the turning day arose and came forth, the lines of battle of our men appeared all at once, arranged equally with the preparation of arms and minds. When they beheld them from the city, as if about to contend with few men, they resolved to receive them with arms in the open fields. But suspecting the deceits of ambushes, they chose rather to finish the war from their walls within the city than to endure the unforeseen chances of open danger outside the city, awaiting also the arrival of other nations to their aid. But when the sun shone back upon the earth, the battle was joined by our men. The first face of the battle, with the rattling sounds of trumpets, was carried out by a cloud of stones. For soon the sound of the trumpets rang out; our men, rushing together from all sides with a crash of voices, sought the walls of the city with the blows of rocks, and they drove away those stationed along the wall with all kinds of missile darts and arrows, although they also threw darts of many kinds against our men to resist them. But what shall I say? The battle was carried out more fiercely by both sides, it was fought with an equal balance by both sides, and it was contested in an equal struggle of battle. It was yielded neither by our men nor by them in the joined conflict. Therefore, it was fought through that whole day under the doubtful edge of victory.
14. One of the instigators of the seditions, seeing the battle pressing on fiercely and intending to mock our men from the wall, devised and uttered these words: "Why do you stand here," he said, "fighting persistently only to die? Why do you not return to your own homes? Or do you perhaps wish to receive the stroke of death before the natural end of your lives? Why rather do you not seek the clefts of the rocks to hide yourselves when the face of our reinforcements appears? Believe, therefore, that I feel pity for you, knowing what will happen and the arrival of the relief that is about to come upon you. For it remains a well-known fact to me how manifold the fighting reinforcements are that are coming to our aid. Indeed, this is the third day since I came hastening from there. Knowing this beforehand, I look with sadness upon the downfall of your miserable procession. That prince of yours, for whom you have come to fight, I shall show to you bound; I shall doom him to insults and mock him with scorn. It is therefore not expedient for you to contend here so monstrously for him, whom perhaps it is already certain has perished by the ambushes of our men. And what is worse, when our victory is made manifest, no mercy will remain for you thereafter." Saying these things, he not only failed to terrify the minds of our men, but inflamed them even more sharply into the fury of fighting. They approached the wall, stood their ground in battling more fiercely than they had begun, and sharply renewed the joined conflict.
15. Therefore, these things having been carried out through the span of the whole day, night finally put an end to the battle. Yet, in the very fervor of that first day, while our men were still persisting in the fight with untiring virtue, they sent word to the prince and requested that reinforcements be directed to them. They were consulting for their own safety with no small foresight—namely, lest they should either be forestalled by the deceit of a foreign nation, or be overthrown to their death with exhausted strength by those with whom they were contending. And the matter was well handled. For when the prince learned that Paul, the leader of the tyranny, was fighting with our men, no further delay was made. Therefore, with wonderful speed in organizing, he dispatched through Duke Wandemir about ten thousand chosen men from the army to aid those who were fighting, so that, keeping watch through the whole night, they might complete a hastened march and, by their sudden arrival, forestall the enemy more quickly—not so much to break the foe as to comfort the minds of our men. But when the exhausted watchmen were already somewhat despairing of keeping the enclosed enemy trapped for long, suddenly they beheld the reinforcements sent to them; straightway sleep fled from their eyes, and with rejoicing hearts and recovered strength, the encounter was determined upon to fight.
16. Now Dawn had left the saffron bed of the sun, and this hostile multitude, crowded along the wall and looking out, beheld through the clear sight of their eyes that the numerous lines of fighters had grown even larger than those they had seen on the previous day. Even then, Paul himself, the head of the tyranny, rushed to ascend a certain prominent lookout post to view so great a sight. As soon as he saw the arranged lines of battle of our men, his spirit immediately failed him, as it is reported, and he declared in these words: "I recognize," he said, "that this entire arrangement of battle proceeds from my rival; it is he himself and I think no other, for I recognize him in his arrangements." Saying these and similar things, and recalling his courage to virtue, he inflamed his men to battle. "Do not," he said, "be disturbed by fear. For this is only that most famous 'virtue' of the Goths, which was boasting with its accustomed rashness that it was coming to overcome us. Believe that the prince is here, here now, and his whole army with him: there is nothing left for you to fear. Indeed, their virtue was famous in the past, both as a defense for their own people and as a terror to other nations; now, however, all vigor of fighting has withered in them, and all knowledge of battle has failed. They have no custom of waging war, and no experience of conflict underlies them. Even if they should join battle massed together as one, they will straightway fly to predetermined hiding places, because their degenerate minds cannot sustain the weight of battle. Rather, when you begin to fight, you yourselves will prove in my words these things that I say. Therefore, there is nothing greater that you ought to dread, since you see both the king and the army itself present here." To these things, many of his men added that the king could not advance without his standards. To which he devised the explanation that the king had approached with the signs of his banners hidden for this reason: to give his enemies the impression that another army still remained, behind which he himself would yet advance, as if following later with a more manifold force than that with which he had previously come. But while he said these things, he was acting out a mockery and composing a fraud, so that he might humble to fear by the deceit of his counsel those whom he was not strong enough to conquer by virtue.
17. He had not yet finished these words, and lo! suddenly the trumpets of war rang out from our men, and having begun the battle, they renewed the face of fighting from the previous day. But those men, placing their confidence of victory more in walls than in strength, were positioned within the city along the wall and threw darts, and they renewed fresh struggles with our men all over again. Thus, the incentive of war boiled up fiercely from both sides, but the virtue of fighting was made manifest more sharply by our side. For while they were contending with all their strength and transfixing the enemy within the city with various kinds of weapons, many of the men from the foreign nations, having been sharply wounded and marveling equally at the virtue and the constancy of our men, turned upon Paul: "We do not perceive that sluggishness of fighting among the Goths which you were speaking of; for we see much audacity and constancy for victory in them. These wounds which we receive teach us among other things; they hurl such powerful throws against the enemy that the crash itself terrifies before the strike extinguishes life." Terrified by their words, Paul was broken again and again by the manifold dart of desperation.
18. But while our men, fighting with greater constancy, grieved that their victory was being delayed until the morrow, they rose up with sharper animosity, counting themselves thoroughly defeated if they did not conquer quickly. Wherefore, moved by a more ferocious kindling than they had been before, all the way until about the fifth hour of the day they battered the walls of the city with continuous strokes of battle, loosed showers of stones with a huge crash, set fire to the gates by placing fire beneath them, and broke through the narrow approaches of the walls. Then, gloriously entering the city, they opened a way for themselves with the sword. When the rebels could not withstand the fierce minds of our men, they shut themselves up for protection within the amphitheater [the arena], which was encircled by a stronger wall and older structures. But when it seemed to them that they could pursue certain of our men who had involved themselves in plundering, they were immediately intercepted, and before they could retreat into that stronghold of the amphitheater, they were slaughtered. Yet many from the rabble of our men also, who had stood gaping for plunder, were cut down by the forestalling of the sword—not because the rebels performed this among the many through open virtue, but rather in the manner of bandits, having recognized that our men had approached the barriers of the amphitheater for the sake of plunder, they the more easily prostrated those whom they found divided, and not even two standing together as one.
19. A new sedition also arose among the rebels themselves; and when the citizens or inhabitants cast the suspicion of betrayal upon some of their own people, they killed those upon whom suspicion was turned with an avenging sword. This was carried out to such an extent that Paul himself, beholding a certain personal servant of his slaughtered before his eyes by the hands of his own men, cried out with a lamentable voice that it was his own house-born slave; yet even so, he could not help the dying man in any way. By now, however, having himself become pale and trembling, Paul was despised by his own men to such a degree that you would have thought he was begging rather than commanding the rest. For he himself was now held in suspicion by the inhabitants, along with the others who had journeyed with him from Spain, lest he should devise their betrayal for his own liberation; while the Spaniards, on the other hand, feared that they would be put to death by the inhabitants before they could pass over to the prince. Why say more? A miserable spectacle of fighting took place within the city. On every side the crowd of pestilential men fell, on every side they were prostrated, and on every side they were slaughtered; for those very men who were escaping the swords of our men were perishing by the swords of their own companions. Thus the city was filled with human corpses in a tangled funeral of deaths. Whichever way the sight of the eyes turned, either human slaughter or the cut-down herds of animals lay open to view. The crossroads of the streets were full of corpses, and the rest of the ground was clotted with blood. A miserable death was visible in the houses, and where you searched the secret places of the homes, you would find the dead lying exposed. Along the streets of the city, too, you could see the corpses of men lying with a certain menacing countenance and a certain monstrous ferocity, as if they were still stationed in the very line of battle; yet their color was hideous, their skin lurid, the horror monstrous, and the stench intolerable. Some even among those lying dead, who had received fatal wounds, feigned the appearance of death so that they might escape the cause of death; yet, consumed by both the slaughter of their wounds and the calamity of hunger, they did not escape death either, except for one man, for whom it was proven that feigning death purchased his life.
20. But Paul, having now laid aside the monstrosity of his tyranny, lamented with a great sigh of his heart that these and similar things were being done, since he could neither resist the enemy nor help his own people in any way. Nevertheless, a certain man, sprung from his own household, approached him to mock him: "Why do you stand here?" he said. "Where are your counselors who led you to this mockery of calamity? What did it profit you to rise up against your own people, since you are now strong enough to profit neither yourself nor your own men in so great a slaughter of death?" Saying these things, he mocked him, provoked not so much by a desire to insult as by an eagerness of bitterness. But when he was exhorted by Paul with gentle words to spare his grief and not to add confusion to confusion, at length the man himself made a hasty descent from the marble steps—standing upon which he had come to mock him—and thus, enveloped by his own companions in the sight of Paul himself, he fell slaughtered. To whom Paul said: "What do you want with this man? He is mine, let him not perish." And he begged with frequent lamentation of voices that the man be spared; but having already been held in contempt, as if he himself were about to die immediately, he could not be heard. Then, moved by every kind of desperation, he sorrowfully laid aside the royal garments which he had taken up through an ambition for tyranny rather than by an preceding orderly process—the wondrous and hidden judgment of God bringing it about that the tyrant should lay down the received kingdom on the very same day on which the religious prince had received the sceptre of ruling from the Lord. For that day was the Kalends [first] of September, on which it was well established that our prince had previously assumed the royal pinnacle. This, therefore, is the day on which, as the orbit of the returning year revolved, the breakthrough into the city lay open. On this day, the royal vestment is laid aside by the broken tyrant; on this day, an avenging blood-retribution is brought upon the enemies.
21. Therefore, the third day after these things had now arrived, when Paul himself, after the deep sighs of the night, awaited his final doom. For when morning came, he began to hold speech with those whom he had kept as the attendants of his perdition, so that they might either say a final farewell to him or, if they could, still consult for their safety in some way. Then Argebad, the bishop of the church of Narbonne, was sent by common counsel to the prince to ask for life and to pray for pardon for their offenses. For, having offered sacrifices to God, some had already received the grace of holy communion of the Lord's body and blood in those very garments—wherein they would not so much receive the final penalties of death as take care that they should be wrapped up unburied, since burial would by merit be denied to them if they received the slaughter for their deeds. Now the bishop Argebad, having been asked for pardon, had gone out from them. And behold! seeing the swift advance of the prince with an incalculable column of fighters at about the fourth milestone from the city, he leaped down from his horse to meet the prince, prostrated himself on the ground, and prayed for pardon. At his meeting, the prince held his horse for a short while and, as he was abounding in the bowels of mercy, he himself wept and commanded the bishop to be lifted up from the earth. The man, having been raised up again and choked by the sobs of his tears, said in a lamentable voice: "Alas! we have sinned against heaven and before you, most holy prince. We are not worthy that the arrival of your piety should meet us, or that pardon conferred should help us—we who have both defiled the faith promised to you and have been tumbled into so great a crime of downfall. Let your piety spare us quickly, I pray, lest the avenging sword extinguish the half-dead remnants of our men, and lest the blade seek lives more than it already has. Command now the army to cease from blood, and citizens to spare citizens. We have escaped the sword as very few indeed, but pardon is prayed for the few. Spare, therefore, our remnants, so that, because the slaughter of death has already flowed out over the rest of our men, there may at least remain those upon whom you may have mercy. For if you refuse to prohibit the slaughter quickly, not even the inhabitants themselves will remain for the defense of the city."
22. Moved to tears by these words, the religious prince was not inexorable, since in the deep secret of his heart he knew that whatever was said to have perished was entirely a loss to himself if he did not grant pardon to the one begging for it. Therefore, the prince responded with these words to the man pleading: "Hold for certain what I shall say," he said. "Conquered by your prayers, I grant you the lives you requested. I shall not destroy them with an avenging sword; I shall not shed anyone's blood today, nor shall I ever extinguish a life, although the offense of such men shall not pass unpunished." The venerable man pressed him for a long time so that he would inflict no loss of vengeance upon those whose lives he had granted. But the prince, soon rendered harsher by a sudden stir of anger, said: "Do not impose conditions upon me time and again, since it is enough to have granted you your lives. Let it suffice that I have spared you completely and alone; for the rest, I promise nothing of the sort." Indignant at this, he burned with a certain kindling of the mind and hastened with a rapid advance to possess the triumph of victory, sending out runners ahead of him so that our men would abstain from fighting until the entire strength of the army arrived with the prince to take the inner parts of the city.
23. At length, by a hurried march of advance, the prince arrived at the city with the admiration of a terrifying procession and armies. For the frightening banners of war were there. And when the sun shone back upon the shields, the earth itself flashed with a double light; the radiant weapons themselves also increased the brightness of the sun more than usual. But what shall I say? Who will be able to unfold what a procession of armies was there, what beauty of weapons, what an appearance of young men, and what a consensus of minds? For there the divine protection was shown by the manifestation of an evident sign. Indeed, it appeared, as it is reported, to a certain man of a foreign nation that the army of the religious prince was protected by the watches of angels, and that the angels themselves portended signs of their protection by flying over the camp of that army. But leaving these and similar things under silence for a short while, let us follow the order of the undertaken work.
24. For when the prince perceived that the army was now gathered into one, positioned at about one stadium away from the city and moved by an incredible kindling of the mind, he arranged the leaders, aligned the common people, and divided the lines of battle, instructing them on the manners in which they should finish the fight. First, however, he deployed a line of brave men along the ridges of the mountains and the sea coasts which are joined to the regions of France, as had been done before, so that a free and unhindered hand of fighters might the more safely accomplish the commands of fighting, because it would have perceived no adversity from foreign nations. Then he sent each of the chosen leaders who were more prominent in both strength and spirit, so that they might drag Paul and the rest of the instigators of his seditions from the caverns of the amphitheater [the arena], in which they had hidden themselves fleeing death. And there was no delay, since they fulfilled the orders with deeds; Paul himself was suddenly dragged with his men from the secret places of the amphitheater, and thus, cast down along the wall, he was handled vilely. Then all that insolent multitude of Gauls and Franks, which had flocked together from here and there to fight against our men, was captured and detained along with immense treasures. And when that treacherous crowd, having already been captured with their king, stood together in one place with the army standing on the right hand and on the left, two of our leaders, sitting upon horses, held Paul in the midst between them with extended hands, gripping his hair in their hands, and they brought him forward following on foot to present him to the prince.
25. Seeing him, the prince, lifting his hands toward heaven with tears, said: "I praise You, O God, King of all kings, who have humbled the proud man as one who is wounded, and with the strength of Your arm have crushed my adversaries." Choked by his weeping, the prince uttered these and similar words. But soon the tyrant, raising his eyes, saw the face of the prince and immediately prostrated himself on the ground and unbuckled his own belt, by now truly breathless and disturbed by excessive fear, not paying attention to what was happening to him. It was a remarkable sight for the eyes, how he had come down from so lofty a pinnacle—albeit of a usurped rank—to this sudden humiliation and full ignominy. It was a great thing to behold how easy that transformation had been: so quickly you could see cast down a man whom you had shortly before heard was glorious, and one whom the previous day had still held as king fall by so headlong a lapse into ruin. The prophetic saying was fully and sufficiently fulfilled in him: "I saw," it says, "the wicked highly exalted and lifted up above the cedars of Lebanon. And I passed by, and lo! he was not: and I sought him, and his place was not found." Why say more? When Paul himself and the other captured leaders of this faction stood brought forth before the prince's horse, Wamba said: "Why did you break forth into such a great evil of madness, as to return evil to me for good things? But why shall I delay? Go and stand under the guards assigned to you until the severity of judgment is debated concerning you. For I shall grant you to live, even if you do not deserve it." Then, having divided them among the army, he handed them all over to assigned, vigilant guards. Whichever of the Franks had been captured, however, were ordered to be treated worthily. For some of them, born of the most noble parents, had been given as hostages, while certain others were from the Franks and some from the Saxons; all of these, loaded with royal munificence, he sent back to their own lands on the eighteenth day after they had been captured, saying that a victor ought not to be unmerciful to the vanquished.
26. Indeed, on the first day, the day before the Kalends [first] of September, the battle against the city of Nîmes was begun by our men. On the following day, the Kalends of September, the breakthrough into the city itself was accomplished. On the third day also, which was the fourth day before the Nones of September, the tyrant Paul was bound, having been captured in a famous detention. But after these things, the mind of the religious prince, anxious for the restoration of the broken city, immediately repaired the hollows of the walls, renewed the burned gates, provided burial for the unburied, restored the taken plunder to the inhabitants, and comforted all wounded matters from the public treasury. He ordered, however, that all the abundance of the treasure they had taken be kept under more diligent custody—not enticed by the gain of avarice, but provoked by divine love—namely, so that things consecrated to God could be more easily separated and restored to divine worship. For the most wicked Paul himself had heaped sin upon sin when he added sacrilege to tyranny. For, as a certain wise man says: unless he had brought spoil upon the holy churches, he would have had nothing from which his treasury could flourish. Wherefore it happened that he arranged to separate and restore most devotedly, as was fitting for the church, a great many silver vessels snatched from the treasures of the Lord and that golden crown which the prince Reccared, of divine memory, had offered at the body of the most blessed Felix, which the same Paul had dared to place upon his own insane head—all of this collected into one place he ordered to be sorted with great care.
27. Now the third day after the victory had arrived for the victors, and Paul himself, laden with iron, was exhibited to the prince sitting upon his throne. Then, according to the custom of the ancients, with a bent spine of his back, he submitted his neck to the royal footsteps; thereafter, he was judged along with the rest in front of all the armies, since by the judgment of all, those who had prepared death for the prince should also receive death. But no sentence of death was brought upon them; they sustained only the punishment of decalvation [scalping/shaving of the head], as is prescribed. Yet the opinion of certain men was being carried about that the Franks were running to the rescue of the captive as quickly as possible. But the prince, awaiting an opportunity for fighting with the Franks—and desiring to avenge not only this cause but also the past injuries of his own nation—remained waiting daily with a brave mind for the arrival of those with whom he was prepared to contend by all means. But when none of the Franks advanced to battle, he had vowed that he would rather go out to meet them, had he not been recalled by the timely counsel of his own heart and of his nobles, lest a broken promise of the pact between both nations should be an occasion for seeking blood. But while he was striving to finish the war against them, as was said, already the fourth day had passed since he had both captured Paul and was nonetheless awaiting the arrival of the adversarial nation. But no presumption of the enemy, no outcome, no hostile gathering at all was shown; for even the most fortified cities of France were already deploring their own final destruction, as was reported, and whichever of their citizens left their cities, lest they should be forestalled by our men, wandered far and wide with uncertain dwellings, securing their lives by hidden shortcuts. For the religious prince was also stationed in the plains with his army, at a distance from the city of Nîmes. There he pitched camp and surrounded it with a very strong wall with wondrous speed. While he was awaiting the arrival of the enemies there, he suddenly heard from a running messenger that one of the leaders of France, named Lupus, had arrived in a hostile manner in the territory of Béziers. Wherefore, on the fifth day after he had captured Paul, having departed from the city of Nîmes and hastening with his army with rapid velocity, he strove to forestall the reported ambushes of the enemy. But Lupus himself, hearing of the return of the prince near the estate to which the name Asperiano [Aspiran] is given, fled so terrified that the army seemed to lack a leader and the leader an army. For in fleeing, he neither sustained his own men, nor could his men overtake him in any way; for their hearts had been so dissolved by fear that, having escaped and running alike not so much through the scattered entryways of roads as through the precipices of mountains—as if they already saw the swords looming over their necks—they showed that they had gained their life by the shortcut of flight, leaving many spoils to our armies in this confusion, both of men who could not follow them and also of beasts of burden or substances which they had brought with them in wagons in multiple abundance to suffice them. And indeed, a chosen hand of fighters, dispatched by the prince, could have overtaken them in warlike incursions; but so foul was their flight, and so quickly did they reach the hiding places of their own borders, that they were deemed to have left no trace at all of where they fled or where they lay hidden and stayed.
28. Therefore, when the prince discovered that he could not find Lupus along with the others, he marched to Narbonne in a peaceful advance and entered the city as a victor. There, having appeased through gifts, restored through arrangement, and provided for through counsels whatever parts of the province of Narbonne had been broken, consumed, and devoured—which had been inflicted upon that same land as it gasped with heavy fevers from the plundering of our men and the incursion of the enemy—he settled the state of affairs with a wonderful peace. He left behind a chosen garrison of warriors there, wiped out the roots of all rebellion from the province, expelled the Jews, and appointed milder rulers over the cities, through whom the offense of so great an evil might be appeased and the land, which had been defiled by such great filth, might be granted pardon, having been thoroughly cleansed by a new baptism of judgments. For because the land of Gaul had raised itself up on its accustomed high-heeled buskin of proud arrogance, it was so ground down by a harsher plundering, stripped of its money, and devoured of its substances, that it is rightly believed to have rid itself through this of whatever rust or wickedness it had contracted.
29. Thereafter, the Gauls having been drained and completely subdued, the prince traveled secure on a direct route toward Spain, fearing no movements of the Gauls behind him, nor dreading any ambushes of the Franks, knowing for certain that there was no one left among his own people to stir up battles, nor among foreign nations to perpetrate ambushes. For with such great virtue of mind and constancy did he not only not fear, but even despise the surrounding nations of the barbarians, that while he was still stationed within Gaul at the place called Canaba, he satisfied the entire army with a gratifying report that they had gone forth successfully, and he disbanded them all immediately from that place. He himself, arriving at Elne, was delayed there by a stay of two days. And having set out from there under favorable successes, he returned to Spain and sought again the see of his throne in the sixth month after he had departed from there. And yet, it is necessary to unfold under what celebrated triumph he entered the royal city, exulting over his enemies, so that, just as future ages will proclaim the standard of his immense glory, so the ignominy of the rebels may not fall from the memory of those to come.
30. For at about the fourth milestone from the royal city, Paul, the leader of the tyranny, and the other instigators of his seditions—with their heads scalped, their beards shaved off, their feet bare, and clothed in filthy garments and attire—were placed upon camel carts. The king of perdition himself went before them at the front, worthy of every ignominy of confusion, and crowned with a pitch-black laurel wreath made of leather. There followed this king of theirs, in a long procession, the arranged order of his ministers, all sitting upon the same carts that were mentioned and driven along under the same mockeries, entering the city while the peoples stood by on this side and on that. Nor indeed is it to be believed that these things came upon them without the dispensation of the just judgment of God—namely, so that the riding upon carts, subjected beneath all others, might teach the deep and lofty pinnacles of their confusion; and those who, beyond human custom, had sought high things by the cunning of their mind, should pay for the injury of their ascent from a higher position. Therefore, let these things be preserved for the centuries to follow, as a desire for the upright, as an example for the wicked, as a joy for the faithful, and as a torment for the unfaithful, so that each party, behending itself in the reflection of this reading, may both escape the downfall of a lapse if it walks in upright paths, and if it has already fallen, may always recognize itself here in the proscriptions of these men.
End of the history concerning Paul.
