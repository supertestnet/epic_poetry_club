title: "The Visigothic Renaissance | 589-711 ad"
---

The Visigoths were a German group similar to the Vandals, but they invaded Spain instead of Africa. Scholars generally agree that this group experienced a resurgence of interest in classical art, architecture, and literature at this period, and this is commonly called the Visigothic Renaissance. Four of the towering literary figures of this movement (apart from Tajon and Leander, already mentioned) were Eugenius II of Toledo, Julian of Toledo, King Sisebut, and St. Isidore of Seville.

Eugenius II was a master of the classical style. He revised and expanded Book 1 of Dracontius’s epic poem The Praise of God, and the result was a unique, poetic adaptation of the creation story from the Book of Genesis that was commonly used to teach students a Christian-Vergilian hybrid style of poetry in the early medieval period.

Julian of Toledo observed that the dominant form of Latin in his time was becoming less like “classical Latin” (it was transitioning to the form that would later be called Old Spanish) and tried to resurrect the classical Latin style in a book called the History of King Wamba. Emulating classical authors like Sallust and Claudian, his book was an action-packed narrative filled with dramatic dialog and intrigue. This text was also designed for use by the laity. Julian wanted a text that the youths of the nation’s nobility could study to learn how to govern well.

King Sisebut was both a “product of” and a “contributor to” the Visigothic Renaissance. Through much of this period, the Visigoths were at war with the Byzantines, who invaded Visigothic territories in the south of Spain. Despite the war, the Visigoths deeply admired Byzantine culture, including their patronage of literature, famous among several Byzantine emperors. Theodosius and Justinian were already mentioned in a previous section, but Emperor Heraclius, Sisebut’s contemporary, also made successful efforts to attract intellectuals to his court to teach liberal arts (like Stephen of Alexandria), and commissioned new epic war poetry to celebrate his military achievements (like the works of his court poet, George of Pisidia -- who, by the way, was also known for another poem, The Hexaemeron, a poetic adaptation of the creation story of the Book of Genesis which is comparable to the one by Eugenius II).

Sisebut’s father Reccared the First imitated this, not just by attracting intellectuals to his court (such as the Greek and Latin scholar John of Biclaro), but also by encouraging the establishment of literary schools staffed by Catholic clergy. Visigothic noblemen customarily sent their children to receive a liberal arts education in these schools, and among them was the young prince Sisebut, arguably the first Christian “Philosopher King” (excluding several Byzantine emperors, like the ones mentioned above).

Sisebut was an intellectual peer of the brightest minds of his age. He was very interested in science, commissioning a scientific book by St. Isidore of Seville and himself writing a poem about the lunar cycles in the Christian-Vergilian hybrid style so popular in his century. He engaged in literary correspondence with the intellectuals of his court, and personally wrote a biography of Saint Desiderius of Vienna, a French saint at the time who established a school of liberal arts. Many of his successors were equally well educated, and this period of Spanish history is often called a “golden age,” a stark contrast to the “dark age” epithet so commonly (but wrongly) used to tarnish the literary brilliance of medieval Europe in the seventh century. In later centuries, people would fondly remember Charlemagne because he patronized education and high literary culture. But in Spain they remembered Sisebut for doing this much earlier.

St. Isidore of Seville was probably the crowning achievement of the Visigothic Renaissance. He invented a poetic style of literature (the Isidorian Style) which captured human emotion by having each stanza explore a central idea by rhythmically repeating the idea in new words on each line, with the lines featuring alliteration, frequent use of internal rhyme and end rhyme, and similar line lengths per stanza.

Here is an example from the opening lines of his book Synonyma, which introduced this style. First I’ll give the English so you can understand the meaning, and then I’ll give the Latin so you can hear the poetry:

======

My soul is in distress,  
My spirit seethes,  
My heart fluctuates.  

Anxiety of mind possesses me,  
Anxiety of mind afflicts me.  

For I am surrounded by evils,  
Hemmed in by hardships,  
Enclosed by adversities.  

I am beset by miseries,  
Covered with unhappiness,  
Oppressed by distresses.  

======

Anima mea in angustiis est,  
Spiritus meus aestuat,  
Cor meum fluctuat.  

Angustia animi possidet me,  
Angustia animi adfligit me.  

Circumdatus sum enim malis,  
Circumseptus aerumnis,  
Circumclusus adversis.  

Obsitus miseriis,  
Opertus infelicitate,  
Oppressus angustiis.  

======

Apart from being a literary innovator, a scientific preserver, and a teacher at the School of Seville, St. Isidore was also an encyclopedist. He wrote a twenty volume encyclopedia of ancient knowledge called The Origins, divided into topics like music, astronomy, medicine, law, geography, war, navies, agriculture, infrastructure, grammar, rhetoric, and word studies.

This encyclopedia became widely popular as soon as it was produced, and monks immediately copied it and spread it throughout their international scholastic network. For centuries, it was celebrated as the zenith of the Christian intellectual tradition, and, like practically all intellectual achievements of this era, it breathes with intense familiarity and dependence on classical knowledge from ancient greco-roman literature.

Despite the dominance of interest in classical literature so evident in this period, it is worth noting that classical studies also had some significant adverse influences. St. Isidore himself, despite encouraging classical literacy for laypeople and even priests, urged caution when handling such works for a specific class of people in his spiritual jurisdiction: Spanish monks. He wrote a set of “monastic guidelines” called the Isidorian Rule which encouraged Spanish monks to study the bible and Christian literature, but explicitly cautioned them about the pagan classics:

“A monk should be cautious about reading the books of the gentiles or the volumes of heretics; for it is better to be ignorant of their pernicious dogmas than to run the risk of falling into some snare of error through experience.” (The Isidorian Rule Book 8)

Urging caution is not the same thing as a prohibition, but this advice is notable as a slight tapping of the brakes on studying the classics, at least among those who tried hardest to, according to a common saying of the time, set their minds on things above, and not on earthly things.

An actual prohibition came from the pope himself: Pope St. Gregory the Great, in a letter to the French saint Desiderius (mentioned earlier), reprimanded him for the classical nature of his school of liberal arts, and told him a bishop must devote himself to higher pursuits. He went so far as to say it isn’t even fitting for lay people to study secular literature:

“what a grave and heinous offense it is for bishops to sing what is not becoming even for a religious layman. ... \[It\] should be clear that you \[must\] not apply yourself to trifles and secular literature.” (Register of Letters Book 11 Letter 54)

Now, this prohibition was not universal. He only said a bishop must not be so devoted to secular literature that he performs it and teaches others to do so; he did not say no one may ever study such literature in any circumstance. But he apparently thought it was also distasteful (“not becoming”) for laypeople to “sing” (perform) such works. It was common to study pagan literature to learn their style so as to use it in Christian literature, but apparently this pope thought performing the pagan works was a bridge too far, and if a bishop did so, it set such a bad example as to constitute a grave sin. Christians of the time certainly did not appear to regard this as a universal prohibition, but as for Desiderius himself, his biography duly avoids mentioning this episode, and emphasizes instead his studies in *biblical* literature. I suspect that means he pivoted the curriculum of his school of liberal arts away from the pagan classics to the Christian ones.

On that note, while this particular pope apparently stopped a bishop from performing pagan poetry for the education of his students, the same pope’s interest in Christian poetry was staggeringly advanced. He personally wrote a thirty five volume commentary on the latin translation of the Book of Job, the original of which is mostly poetry, as well as a commentary on the Song of Songs (a poem), in the preface to which, he outlines his attitude to Christian literature:

“the words and meanings...\[are like\] the colors and subjects of a painting... \[The\] letter\[s\] cover...the \[meaning\] in the same way that the husk conceals the grain... \[And\] it serves a good purpose for mysteries to be hidden by the cloak of the letter, seeing that wisdom that has been sought after and pursued is savored the more for that.” (Preface to the Song of Songs by Pope Gregory the Great)

He thus encouraged the use of an elegant style filled with imagery and allegories in Christian literature, and hoped that Christians would create and learn from such works. His seventh century contemporaries obliged, and pumped out new poems, narratives, and correspondence that rivaled the elegance of the pagan classics. Thus they studied pagan literature, and then turned their style to Christian use, creating a golden age of Christian high culture along the way.

Clarification:

I've spoken of a “twenty volume” medieval encyclopedia and a “thirty five volume” commentary on Job. One might be tempted to imagine these like “modern” multi volume works, such as the Encyclopedia Britannica, but that would be a misleading comparison. Due to varying bookbinding technology, changes in popular “fonts,” and the development of new conventions for abbreviating words and phrases, the amount of information the medieval world could fit into a book kept changing, as did the size of the books they could bind together. The latin words often translated as “book” and “volume,” such as liber, vellum, and codex, also acquired different meanings depending on time and location. “Liber” – now often translated as “book” – usually meant a length of text about the size of an ancient scroll, which could be somewhere between ten and twenty modern “pages.” Several such “books” (libri) would often be bound into a “codex,” which visually looked something like a modern book: a bunch of “pages” of parchment bound with a thick cover. The Latin word codex is often translated as “book” too, but many scholars leave it untranslated as “codex” – whose plural is “codices.”

Depending on which messy, handwritten “font” was used, a typical “book” (librum) was between 5,000 and about 10,000 words, and a typical “codex” contained up to ten “books” (libri), for a total that rarely exceeded 100,000 words per codex. By contrast, a modern “volume” such as the Encyclopedia Britannica has slightly over a million words printed in a tiny, machined font. And when scholars speak of “twenty volumes” or “thirty five volumes” in reference to works of this period, they generally mean “twenty books” (libri), not “twenty codices,” so they are using the smaller meaning of “book” which no one would call a “volume” today, except a scholar trying to make ancient literary output sound more impressive. The multi volume works of the medieval era may “sound” large, but in terms of word count, they were tiny by comparison with the multi volume works of today.

The total length of a masterpiece like Pope Gregory’s Commentary on Job, summing all thirty five “volumes” together (here using the smaller “libri” meaning), is a bit over 500,000 words, around the length of the complete Lord of the Rings trilogy of modern times – and it fit into around six codices. The entirety of Isidore’s encyclopedia (summing all twenty “volumes” or libri) is shorter than the single novel The Brothers Karamazov, and often appeared in only two or three codices of the time. So these works were “multi volume,” but though scholars often list the number of volumes as “twenty” or “thirty five,” the physical reality for even the longest works was more like three to six codices.

Poems tended to be shorter, and almost always could fit in a single codex. The longest of the “epic poems” I've referred to (excluding the old pagan ones) is Aldhelm’s In Praise of the Virgins, which is about 25,000 words, and is comparable to what we would now call a “novella,” or perhaps an unusually long “short story.” It's difficult for me to think of a work of comparable length today that “everyone knows,” but the average length of a Sherlock Holmes short story is about 8,000 words, so think of reading about three of those – that’s a common length for many of these medieval epic poems. Most of them are individually easy to breeze through in less than three hours if you know Latin or are lucky enough to have a modern translation.
