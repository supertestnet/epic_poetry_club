The Tragedy of Orestes is a poem by Dracontius, an African poet of the Vandal Renaissance. It uses a style influenced by Vergil, with strong evidence of Dracontius's legal training. The poem is also mostly a rewrite of three plays of Aeschylus, converted into a single epic, narrative poem.

The poem is notable as one of the last surviving examples of a major mythological epic poem in Latin in for hundreds of years. Until the Waltharius arrived in the 800s, Latin epics tended to be about relatively recent events starring real people, rather than embellished stories from ancient mythology.

This translation was created in several stages. First, I divided the poem up into sections of roughly 40 lines apiece, which I call sections. Then, I fed each section into Google Search's AI (based on Gemini), and asked it to translate each section idiomatically. Then I read through each section, modified them for readability, combined them, and decided it was good enough to post. Below is the result, and I hope you enjoy it.

# Text

Section 0.

I sing of mournful joys and of triumphs to be loathed,  
Of slaughter instead of praise for the victor, of bloody festivities,  
Of the sorrow of a funeral, and of the lamentable vow  
Of the Trojan mistress, not the wife who cut the throat of the son of Atreus.  
I sing of laurel wreaths, gleaming with royal blood,  
And of the leader's crown defiled by brains and gore.  
Of Orestes, forgetful of his father, but mindful of the murder his mother committed,  
Devout in his impiety, a man whose very righteousness was wicked.  
I sing of gods who were unjust, yet, by a fierce logic, justly so.  
Of an innocent culprit, of the cleansing temples of the sisters in Thrace,  
Where virginity offered salvation from disaster.  
Of a priestess sister, who improved her lot by a lie, a holy deception.  
I ask you, Melpomene, to step out of your stage clothes  
And let the iambic foot rest while the dactylic foot resounds.  
Grant that I may recount the praiseworthy sin of the son  
And clear before the court him whom the sisters condemned,  
Who was inflamed by grief, roused by shame, and lifted up by anger,  
Whom our mind raises, whom our spirits elevate, for a good impulse urges us on.  
Holy fury gave him weapons. Guilt gave him jeweled steel.  
Healing madness also shattered him in order to restore  
Extinguished honors and the arms of victory, once buried.  
For the author of the crime cut down the avenger of the crime,  
And the champion of the marriage bed was killed in full view of that bed,  
Til he, the lover of the bridal bed, lay dead before the couch.

Section 1.

The leader of leaders, the king of kings, the commander Agamemnon  
Returned after two decades, triumphant after two wars.  
That powerful warlord brought back the martial trumpets.  
That king carried vanquished Troy back on Argive ships,  
Along with Trojan brides and the young offspring of Trojan mothers.  
Silently counting the riches of Asia in his heart,  
The king ordained the greatest gifts for the thunderous Jupiter.  
He appointed choice offerings for great Juno,  
And added gifts for Athena's Athens,  
As well as for all the gods above who favored the Greeks.  
Unknowing of his wife and ignorant of the crimes of Aegisthus,  
He prepared many gifts for the unworthy Clytemnestra.  
Smiling, the father set aside numerous things for Orestes,  
Which a father’s love and affection made fitting,  
Despite not being equal to his future merits and spirit.  
He also saved beautiful gifts for his modest daughter.  
Meanwhile, he cut his path through the blue of the sea.  
With the god’s anger stilled and the southeast winds retreating,  
Bright winds, roaming the waves, stretched the canvas sails,  
But did not drive the leader where his course lay. For the forceful south wind  
Brought the Pelasgian fleet to the Tauric shores.  
The untamed commander then ordered the ships laden with spoils to go on ahead to Mycenae,  
Intending to follow himself once his vows to Diana were fulfilled.  
Thus he entered the temple as a suppliant, reverently worshiping  
The chaste divinity of the goddess. While he carried his offering to the altar,  
Purple silk threads adorned the embroidered veil,  
And robes glittered, jeweled with various small gems.  
Then he saw Iphigenia carrying the evening censers.  
He froze, and fixed his astonished eyes upon the maiden.  
Paternal affection stood aghast, as did his mind, conscious of its own baseness.  
He knew she had been sacrificed for the Greek host by the blow of a knife,  
And he could only wonder if perhaps someone resembling her had been born.  
The fair maiden, however, had already begun to recognize her father more fully,  
And she quickly finished her duty as a ministering servant,  
Filling her sacred vessel with fire without delay.  
Then the royal maiden hung around her father’s neck,  
Giving and receiving pure kisses, the tribute common throughout their world.  
Soon a pious rain of tears moistened the faces of both daughter and father.  
The father fell silent while their faces wept with joy,  
And their kisses were interrupted only by groans and frequent sobs.

Section 2.

At last, paternal love unlocked his voice box, and he began:  
"O daughter, my own, my love! O daughter, my crime!  
Do you live? Or are you a phantom and a fleeting ghost?  
Were you not then consecrated to destruction and appointed to death?  
Forgive me for rejoicing! For I thought you fell by the sword of the gods,  
But your limbs show me you are alive, and your actions confess it.  
Tell me, then, by what fortune do you remain here, torn from your mother?  
Has your brow recently been adorned with the sign of the goddess’s shrine?  
And how did you come to serve Diana by sacred rites? I marvel all the more!"  
The father said these words, and the maiden replied to him:  
"O father, when you sought the lands of Hector with your fleets,  
A letter forged by Odysseus’s cunning was fashioned,  
Bearing your name, as if you had ordered me to come  
To the marriage bed with all haste and be the betrothed bride of Achilles.  
My mother believed it, and she entrusted me to Odysseus.  
Yet I was not invited to the marriage bed. Instead, I was handed over as a victim to the altars.  
But mild is the holy temple of the goddess. Through Diana’s pity,  
A deer was given in my place, and no one regarded the substitution as a sad one.  
Thus I was rescued from the knife, and behold, I was adopted as an incense bearer.”  
On hearing these things, the son of Atreus grieved even as he rejoiced,  
And, after worshipping the divinity with prayer, fervor, and incense, he said:  
"O Phoebe, daughter of Leto, sister of the god who bears the lyre,  
By whatever name you are called as an illustrious goddess,  
By your great power you quickly grant whatever you are asked,  
And you show yourself more gentle by sparing sacred blood.  
You also dispel grief and grant an end to the laments of parents.  
The hungry blade of the priests flashes, for it is free  
Of human blood, and is content with lesser blood.  
You who rescue lives from the altars even when death is decreed,  
I pray that you restore this child, whom I thought was dead, to her king and father,  
So that her salvation from destruction may do some good. To you, Delia, as a suppliant,  
I will give a thousand beasts, stags and goats and swine,  
And heifers whose curved brows are armed with horns.  
Nothing has been achieved at Troy if I do not return to Mycenae  
Accompanied by this maiden, to restore her to her wailing mother,  
Who believes her dead. Let her realize she was redeemed by your mercy,  
And let her very lamentation turn to joy."  
Prompted by these prayers, the goddess grew harsh with anger.  
Despite being begged, she turned away her face, her eyes, and her mind.  
When the powerful warlord perceived that Diana’s heart was inflamed,  
He was terrified and withdrew, halting his steps and his voice alike.  
Sorrowfully, he quickened his pace toward the waves of the sea,  
Embarking on his ships and cleaving the waters on his course.  
Then, while he yet pursued his journey through the foaming waves,  
Flying Rumor filled the Mycenaean shores  
With the news that the leader had arrived, enriched by the fortunes of war.  
And there was no delay. All the plunder arrived on ships adorned with palms,  
And the eyes of the Greek city rushed to watch from the towers,  
And all the walls were filled with crowds of women.  
The wife of Agamemnon was there, intent on seeing the captive Phrygians,  
But the unchaste woman feared her husband's arrival.  
At her sight, mothers and young brides alike gave way.  
The foolish royal consort stood there as an adulteress,  
Cursing the public joys with tearful maledictions.  
Her anxious heart was shaken with trembling dread,  
Expecting punishment for her crimes as her husband approached,  
And, prompted by fear, she kept glancing all around her.  
A sudden rush of panic left her cheeks pale,  
Yet the wretched woman’s thoughts remained with her adulterer.

Section 3.

But when she did not see the king descend from the ship,  
She thought her crimes might yet remain unpunished,  
And she rejoiced. Then the secrets of her inner mind were revealed,  
For a bright blush returned after her paleness was dispelled,  
And a shameless joy enhanced the beauty of her guilty face.  
She believed the king had drowned in the waves of the sea,  
But didn’t long consider why he might be delayed. She was happy simply knowing he was absent.  
She wanted to go with swift steps to tell her lover  
That her husband would not return, but there her rash hope was deceived.  
Aboard the ship, the king’s lot was the priestess Cassandra.  
Amid the slaughters of the Dardanians and the triumphs of the Greeks,  
She received unique honor, despite being a portion of the plunder.  
Seized by a divine trance, she called out to Clytemnestra  
From afar: "Hail, queen of the Pelasgians,  
Avenger of the Trojans, consolation of captive Troy!  
And you, triumphant conqueror, good shepherd Aegisthus!  
You rest on a downy mattress now instead of an oxhide,  
And a royal court receives you now instead of a cottage’s roof.  
Why do you still hesitate to relieve your anxiety?  
Destroy him, or the blossom of your love shall perish soon.  
The time is here, the fates command it, and guilt urges you on.  
Now let the wielded axe sever his victorious neck!  
A similar judgment from heaven awaits you both,  
But Orestes must at least mature, leaving a long delay for your guilt.  
And you [Clytemnestra]! After the death of the father, you will be both the mother and the victim  
Of your son and executioner, and the fierce adulterer will be struck down with you  
When the hand of dear Pylades strikes. Believe me!  
A different sort of madness awaits that wretched boy, which Orestes must later expiate."  
She spoke, and, as her restraints held her back, she collapsed on the benches.  
The prophetic words terrified the wicked queen.  
The paleness returned to her face and, deep within her grim heart,   
She prepared to commit the crime. For the madness of love drove her,  
And anxious fear ran rampant in her trembling mind.  
In grief, she returned home, pretending to be joyful, but unnerved by dreadful terror.  
Then, entering her marriage chambers, she heaved heavy sighs  
Til she suppressed the groans in her heart and put a joyful countenance on her face.

Section 4.

Astonished by what he heard, the shameless Aegisthus asked Clytemnestra  
What news, reports, or rumors the messengers had brought.  
Armed with the guile of her sex, she made answer  
By a secret ruse: "Young man,” she said, “tell me, what must be done?  
For we are ruined! My husband is returning after his wars  
As an armed victor. With biting jealousy he threatens  
To dispel our Argive customs and enforce the laws of chastity  
With grim commands. What will this avenger of other men’s crimes  
Do for his own rights? We shall die unavenged  
And our love will now perish. The wild man will slaughter us both  
Violently before our time, for he is accustomed to punish the guilty  
Fiercely with a death dealing blade. We have no hope of safety  
Unless we risk the safety of both our lives.  
Let the glory and the weapons of the warlike tyrant be struck down,  
And let him fall before he learns of our passion.  
His success in warfare has made him too proud,  
As has the ruined fortune of Priam, for, by seeing so much bloodshed,  
He now thinks human blood is cheap.  
For your sake I am devoid of chastity. For your sake I bear shameful destitution.  
But I do not want to repudiate the fruit of our great misdeed.  
I’m not trying to extort you by speaking hostile words. No, these are facts!  
In life and death alike, a common fate binds us.  
We share the same risk and will share the same reward.  
A single lot awaits us both. I both command and entreat you,  
A queen advising her shepherd, terrified by the dread of death!  
I am terribly anxious, a wretched member of the feminine sex,  
Yet I urge you to do the one act capable of keeping us alive.  
I order it, lest I die, doomed to a bloody death at your side.  
For you yourself, wretched and impious man, will fall with me once Agamemnon appears.  
Use your wits! Rescue us from our deadly peril.  
It won’t be difficult to lay low the victor with the sword,  
For he who has overcome his enemy always lets his guard down,  
And, resting secure without fear of anyone, he is vulnerable to treachery.  
You have no one to fear. Orestes is small and young.  
Of my daughters, one has been burned in the temples of Diana,  
While the other, what would she dare to do? She is a wretch of my sex, reduced to helplessness.  
Meanwhile, Menelaus, the other son of Atreus, is in exile.  
You will act with impunity for whatever you do, and if you accomplish the crime,  
A reward will be given, not a punishment. You will walk more fortunate than ever,  
Possessing the kingdom and the court of Agamemnon.  
Moreover, I have a recent example to relate for you: just as the Spartan woman [Helen],  
The destroyer of so many kings and so many peoples,  
Lives happily and in peace even after such great slaughter,  
Even so, I need not fear the Greeks so long as I preserve the line of Thyestes."

Section 5.

The woman spoke these words, and much hope entered Aegisthus.  
"Tell me," the shepherd said, "by what method may I bring about  
So grand a crime? It is a terrific task to kill a king,  
And especially so when he reigns triumphant in his own court."  
Then the wicked queen spoke out, making the method clear:  
"The savage king will arrive stained with battle scars and thick blood.  
Thus the leader will find it necessary to change  
His garments. I will give him a tunic whose collar is stitched closed.  
While the king is striving to thrust his covered head out,  
Approach him with your axe as a fierce and violent assassin.  
Then split open the head of the son of Atreus, neck, throat, and brain.  
This final deed will alone save us, our sole remaining hope.  
Yield to my counsel. Our only chance  
Is to avert our own deaths by dealing doom to him.  
We’re wasting time, the avenger of our adultery is almost here."  
While still speaking these ill-omened words, she moistened her face with tears.  
Aegisthus, agitated by a consuming terror, was persuaded to violence.  
Love made him bold, and terror made him reckless,  
And he made trembling blows with his armed limbs,  
Fearfully striking at an invisible enemy who was not there.  
It was even as a serpent which guards a spring with its fangs bared, which is accustomed  
To prepare a deadly venom in its jaws, capable of bringing doom upon mortals,  
And its forked tongue darts forth, hissing, as it rises up on its breast.  
Cheered by his gestures, the woman swiftly recovered  
Her infamous and shameful courage, and her former delight returned.  
With a guilty lunge she hung around his rustic neck,  
Planting sweet kisses with her wanton lips.  
He, returning the favor, planted frequent kisses on her limbs.  
While they enjoyed their shared wickedness with united minds,  
The royal ship arrived, adorned with various wreaths.  
Their wickedness made their courage grow even bolder.  
The woman snatched up the tunic, and the shepherd snatched an axe from its place.  
Each stood at their own post. The hidden adulterer  
Lay concealed in the chamber, and the woman, stepping forward a little,  
Carried the tunic, eager for her husband's death.

Section 6.

Descending from his ship, the king triumphed as he touched land.  
He gleamed with his battle scars, bloody and beautiful,  
Grand in appearance, handsome in the grim terror of battle.  
He was just like Jupiter when, after the wars of the giants, he brought back to heaven  
The star bearing crown on his starry brow,  
Which breathed forth glittering flames from the top of his head.  
The sweet issue of his own flesh and blood rushed together to meet their father.  
They flew to his embrace and returned kisses to him as he gave them.  
The father turned his gaze, hoping to see his wife,  
And he proceeded toward the marriage chambers past the threshold of the front gate,  
Where the wicked woman greeted him on his return with a deceitful voice:  
"O warlike hero,” she said, “O man powerful in arms, lay aside your threatening attire  
And deign to put on a gentler garment. You who are scarcely a lover of peace,  
Behold this vestment, which my own hands wove for you.  
Its golden threads are radiant with a purple hue."  
She spoke, and over the king, stripped of his protective armor,  
The cunning woman cast the deadly garment on his body.  
But while his head sought the opening of the tunic for a way out,  
She, pinning his hand, summoned the armed Aegisthus.  
With a quaking right hand, he raised his grim axe,  
And, impious, struck the man’s unsuspecting head with a deadly lunge,  
Shattering the diademed brow along with the top of his head  
Into two separate parts. Then he redoubled his blows.  
Three times and four times he struck, til the skull poured out the brain.  
While the queen gestured happily with her hand, the innocent king collapsed,  
Wounded, and he shook the earth with his trembling body.  
It was just like a massive boar, which gets entangled in the hunters' nets.  
It fiercely desires to spring out and escape the snares,  
Wildly shaking its foaming jaws and hooked tusks,  
Wasting its empty blows with snapping bites.  
Even so the conqueror of Asia received a bloody, final destruction.  
Alas, a shepherd's axe laid waste to those limbs.  
How bitter is the lot of humans, and how ignorant are their minds of the future!  
Who could believe, even if the Delphic cauldron, driven by a hundred blasts,  
Should shake its prophetic sanctuaries,  
Move the caves, stir the tripods, and wear out the plectra,  
That the destroyer of Asia would be cut down by the tiller of a little field,  
A helpless deserter, a master of sheep and cattle?  
Who could believe that he who had set Troy ablaze now lacked the fire of a funeral pyre?  
Learn, you who are fortunate, never to trust in the fates.  
The gods are quick to grant the highest heights, but they abandon you all too soon,  
And in the end they bring wretched ruin, or perhaps suddenly  
They desert you, calling for punishment in place of the former favor of fortune.

Section 7.

Let those who are incredulous reflect on the fate of Priam,  
And let those who are slow to trust behold the palace of Agamemnon.  
The maiden of the line of Pelops was crushed by the sudden disaster,  
But she saved Orestes, the avenger of his father.  
Snatching her brother from the jaws of their parent’s doom, Electra  
Placed him aboard a ship and took him with her to Athens,  
To establish him in the study of wisdom with tender care.  
The very vessel that carried the king home now bore away his children,  
And with them, all the hopes of Agamemnon and all the treasures of Troy.  
Exceedingly loyal to Orestes was his friend Pylades.  
They were bound together by their famous eloquence and their intelligent endeavors.  
The pair shared the same likes and the same dislikes.  
If fortune permitted the wrestling school to exercise the youths through effort,  
Peaceful was their striving. If they hunted in the lairs  
Of wild beasts, they remained partners in the chase.  
If each one bridled a swift footed steed and reined him in,  
A shared passion drove them. If their minds were set  
On hurling spears, both youths threw together.  
If the boys played board games, their skills were evenly matched.  
Neither one feared to be beaten or burned with eagerness to win.  
It was just as Pollux loves and cherishes his brother Castor,  
And, with equal devotion, Castor loves Pollux.  
Thus they take life and death in turns, the price of their misfortune.  
Meanwhile, the murderer and adulterer clothed himself in Tyrian purple,  
And a glittering crown adorned his guilty head,  
As though he were the rightful heir to Agamemnon’s hall,  
Though it was more fitting to call him the heir of Thyestes.  
He went through every nook searching for the orphaned Orestes.  
But when Aegisthus realized the Greek wealth hoard was empty,  
One hope remained, to seize the Trojan treasures,  
Til he learned these too were borne away with the king’s son.  
Then he seethed with frustration, for he had found only the empty title  
Of kingship, having lost the riches by which he might have lorded over the people,  
And the power by which he might have armed himself with iron and gold alike.  
The cunning woman consoled her partner in crime with skillful  
Deceit, and steeled her wavering accomplice to hold to their plot.  
Meanwhile, she displayed the feminine finery befitting her sex,  
And brought jewelry boxes forth while chattering on,  
Draping precious necklaces over her shameless limbs.

Section 8.

"With these," she said, "we will buy the favor of the Argives throughout the realm,  
And by these gifts we will draw both new and old nobles  
Over to our allegiance. Take any chieftain you please. His wife,  
Even if she is outstanding in beauty and chastity,  
Will, once decorated with these glittering ornaments, attempt  
To win over her husband. With the honey of her tongue as her support,  
And with my wealth as her armor, she will skillfully pour sweet poison  
Into her man’s astonished ears with coaxing words.  
Twining her artful limbs in soft embraces,  
She will make high ranking friends for us, as befits her sex.  
Gold is a lovely thing, but a woman is lovelier than gold.  
With gold, foul deeds become pleasing. With gold, fair deeds become glorious.  
Cytherea softens fierce Mars in his warfare.  
Believe me, young man. As a woman, I know my own sex."  
By these wiles, the cunning queen restored her lover's spirits,  
And, rejoicing, he gave thanks for her feminine boldness.  
His base mind longed to reign, and to wield the scepter of a leader.  
By fraud was the leader slain, unwary of his plight,  
Through a treacherous wife, through the boldness of a wicked shepherd.  
Deep in their hearts, the citizens knew this about their prince.  
Weeping with anxious murmurs, they spoke these words:  
"Did pitiless Lachesis, by the everlasting law of the fates,  
Really decree that our king should perish by so shameful a death?  
Would that in his youth the raging Amazon Penthesilea  
Had struck him down in battle! For the son of Atreus barely escaped her.  
Rather that than to perish like this, laid low by a death so ignoble,  
Disgraceful to his race and ruinous to his people the Greeks!  
He was dragged on the ground by his feet. He was cast out to lie far from his palace,  
Where, stripped of all honor, his corpse lies in its grave each night."  
Tell me, Muse, I pray, why did that false mother  
Neglect to search for her children and hand them over captive  
To share their father's doom? Dorylas was a freedman of the house of the son of Atreus,  
And the tutor of the boy Orestes, the fugitive.  
In his clever heart, he devised a deceptive plan  
To pretend that the raging sea had drowned the descendants of Atreus  
In its ocean waves. Standing on the shore, he spoke thus:  
"O gods of land and sea, O pious source of the heavens,  
O love whose essence is divine, nod your assent, I pray you,  
To my bold deceit, and speed my false oaths.  
Let them heed and believe the lie of the advocate of truth.  
For I long to rescue the innocent ones from their father's fate,  
Prepared as it is by their guilty mother and their hostile stepfather."

Section 9.

Thus he spoke. Then, to cover himself in the rippling waters, he entered  
The dark blue sea, submerging himself in the foaming waves  
Right up to his head. Then he made for dry ground.  
Distraught and beating his breast, he ran to the grief stricken city  
And cried out with a plaintive voice: "O Jupiter, our enemy,  
Does it please you thus to avenge the Trojans and destroy the Greeks?  
The sea rages on behalf of the Phrygians! The ocean acts as the Trojan avenger!  
What crime did the children commit? What did they do, tender in their youth,  
On the plains of Ilium, in the season of war?  
Why should those poor children be denied the escape they sought on the sea?  
I myself saw the swallowing surge sink the ship  
I was sailing as companion and tutor to the descendants of Atreus!  
I alone escaped from it, helpless, using my feet as oars.  
O righteous Clytemnestra, O Aegisthus, cruel yet gentle!  
O savage waves of the ocean, which, even after the war, did not spare  
The children of the Greeks! Happy now must all men call Troy!"  
As he said this with a rapt voice, he was swept to the palace,  
Where he was heaped with gifts of wealth, for he brought joy  
To the minds of the incestuous pair. Then, without delay, the shameless queen  
Commanded that the people be summoned, and they came under compulsion.  
Standing in the midst of the hall, she addressed them thusly:  
"Distinguished nobles of the Greeks and common people,  
While Agamemnon triumphed, you endured five harsh years.  
Those who weren't plunged into death scarcely survived. Exhausted,  
Your bellies are hollow and you are emptied out by the shedding of your blood.  
That Bellona thus diminished you in savage wars  
Was the fault of the king, who emptied the city of its citizens,  
Bent on making widows of young wives, on bereaving parents of their young,  
And on robbing children of their father’s love.  
It was for that reason that the fierce man fell, struck down by a deadly axe,  
And his offspring have just now perished in the avenging waves.  
Now I pledge rest to you, with the expectation of undisturbed tranquility.  
Complete peace will be yours. No one resting at night  
Will be disturbed by bugle calls, no trumpet blasts will strike your ears.  
You will gather sweet sleep at the proper hours once again.  
Harsh weapons will stand idle, swords will be bent into sickles,  
And your horn carved bows will be strung with bone and sinew  
Only so you may hunt wild beasts and swift birds.

Section 10.

You will refresh your old age with delights and fine banquets,  
And you will nurture your sweet grandchildren, the issue of your children.  
The only death to come will be when illness ends your health  
In the natural course of life. As the years glide by,  
You will rightly expect to exit your body  
Unscathed by iron. The norm will be to expect a burial  
That is duly arranged, to expect funerary flames,  
And for each person to prepare a longstanding tomb for himself after the pyre.  
A cruel and impious enemy to his own offspring  
Was that king Agamemnon, and a bitter tyrant to his homeland.  
Aegisthus will be more civil, whom I now announce as my husband."  
Thus she spoke, and then ordered them all to depart from the hall  
And return to their own homes. Yet the base adulterer,  
Ignorant and unskilled at holding the reins of a kingdom,  
Arrogantly he assumed a haughty air for himself. With a menacing glare,  
He spoke harshly to the royal attendants, despite being a stranger to them.  
The commands he issued were not gentle, and if he himself  
Were a servant, he would consider such orders a shameful disgrace.  
The scepter of triumph was handed over to that shepherd, now a tyrant,  
As the reward of evildoing, and the royal purple as the reward for bloodshed.  
A wicked matriarch! A wife who disgraces the royal halls!  
Who, I ask, would not groan at this? Yet fear compelled them  
To obey the infamous upstart. They feared the cowherd  
Deeply, these men who had not feared the wrath of Hector.  
Where the law of Agamemnon had once held sway, there was now, after the fall of Troy,  
A guilty knave who played the peer of the strongly armed Achilles.  
Tomyris, queen of the Getae, ended a kingdom,  
But she was avenging her own people, and did nothing shameful.  
Medea committed an evil deed, but she was inflamed by the pain  
Of sweet love when she burned the royal palace,  
Bereft of her living husband because of his mistress Glauce.  
The impious Lemnian women took up arms  
And stained their own marriage beds with the blood of their husbands,  
But it was the fierce madness of Venus that drove them to that unspeakable deed.  
What the Scythian brides did was a great crime,  
But they were a barbarian crowd. But you, O queen of the Pelasgians,  
Whom Greece produced, that fertile mother of laws,  
O famous consort of the Mycenaean and wife of the avenger,  
You added to the crime of adultery by murdering your husband.  
You ought to have remembered Alcestis, who by her own death snatched  
Her husband from the shades below. Devoted to her spouse, she was merciless to herself.

Section 11.

Why should I speak of Evadne, burned in the Theban fires?  
She was joined to her dead husband in the crackling flames  
When an unholy devotion and a sweet affection prompted her  
To embrace a bitter doom. To end the pangs of grief,  
She found strength even to brave the fire. Availing herself of a cruel remedy,  
She entered hades mingled with her husband's ashes!  
One urn held the two lovers, united by a single funeral.  
O happy marriage! O grand example of devotion!  
By a sacrilege against piety, she wedded her husband after death.  
Flames were her bridesmaids, a pyre was her bridal chamber, and its wood was her bed.  
Many women have shown how true and honorable love can be.  
But now an idle shepherd ruled the realm, a reproach to the fates.  
And while the forbidden lovers took their pleasure together  
For the span of seven years and eight months,  
The whole band of loyal servants went to the royal tombs  
In the dead of night, bewailing their king for all those years.  
They anxiously stifled their groans and trembling wails  
With measured restraint, held back by terrifying dread.  
Then the freedman, the shrewd guardian of Orestes,  
Poured forth his voice in groans and words of grief:  
"O man who was the best of kings once, who now is a pitiable shade!  
Prosperity proved cruel to you! You, whose triumph attracted only crime,  
And whose victory in battle brought forth only death!  
It is clear the gods who heard your prayers became angry when you worshipped them  
In their temples. For the deities, despite your placations, denied  
Success to you. Was it not just for the Greeks to conquer  
While the abducted Spartan queen still lingered in Paris’s embrace?  
Did you not toil so that a shepherd might not keep Helen?  
Behold, a shepherd now possesses your own wife! If death is a transition,  
If consciousness remains after doom, if souls set free from their limbs  
Still endure, if the spirit continues living after death,  
Then hear the tears and the weeping of your own people!  
Break open the ground, and let the earth split into a yawning chasm,  
Forced upward and suddenly armed with the shades of the furies!  
Rise up like Achilles once rose from his tomb!  
Exact punishment from your wife, and let the adulterer pay  
As many penalties as he has limbs! Will you die unavenged  
While a shepherd holds your kingdom? Beyond the grave, the Thessalian hero  
Sternly demanded the death of a maiden  
Of blameless character. Will you, O great commander, leave unpunished  
The committers of so many crimes who hold your victorious realm?

Section 12.

O gods who rule in the cruel, chaotic abyss,  
Open the throat of Tartarus! Burst open its jaws!  
Send the maiden furies into that palace of death!  
Delay not. Fierce shall they fly to the realm of Thyestes,  
Winding their well worn way, tracing their typical track.  
You are no furies if I am forced to beg you  
To commit a crime, if you won't attack unless prompted.  
But lo, I hold back, for though I ask for blood, I ask for it justly.  
But I harbor great expectations. Seek these walls, the companions of Thebes,  
Walls once consecrated to the darkness of Tartarus,  
Robbed of the brightness and the luster of mid day.  
Therefore let this be my prayer: when, by a just sentence of death,  
Those savage partners in crime are taken and smitten with a merciless sword,  
Then fan the flames of Acheron and his tormenters.  
Double the deadly, dread venom of the furies.  
Permit cruel Enyo to torture the committers of these crimes.  
Whatever torments you prepare for the guilty will never be enough."  
Thus he spoke, and a voice came forth groaning from the depths of the tomb:  
"Cease troubling my mind with mortal griefs  
And burdening me with domestic woes. The devotion of my servant  
Should bring me comfort in my grave. My wife, the vilest of wives,  
Inflamed as she was by her shameful and criminal passion,  
Or by her exposed reputation, has, with womanish weapons,  
Destroyed me. Me, who returned to her victorious from the foe!  
For ten years I sacked Troy, avenging my brother's love,  
Avenging an adultery, now laid low by an adulterous wife.  
Now shall they fear me. Now shall I disgrace the house, now pollute the idols of this home  
With a deadly blow and a sudden, impious treachery.  
For I shall not go unavenged. A bloody punishment shall befall them.  
But ask me nothing further. Cassandra has spoken truly.  
Believe Cassandra, for Cassandra is a faithful prophetess."  
Thus he spoke, and all of them departed, leaving the tomb behind.

Section 13.

Yet on that night, the flitting shade of Agamemnon  
Came swiftly to the halls of Athens. For noble Orestes  
Was there, and with him his friend Pylades,  
Sharing their sleeping quarters, breathing alternately.  
Both lay exhausted and battered due to their love of the wrestling ring,  
And the son of Atreus stood before their beds, seen by both  
In dreams, not as he was when triumphing after war,  
But as he fell when his brow was struck by a double edged ax.  
Sorrowful was he, and shaking, and limp, interrupting his sighs with groans.  
He had drenched his pale face in crimson gore,  
And his hands too, which weakly trembled. From his severed neck  
And foot he dragged the chains by which he was dragged from the hall.  
He spoke: "Does it not shame you, young men, in the prime of your years,  
With downy curls clothing your blooming cheeks,  
With strong arms, well trained for noble pursuits,  
That a shepherd should claim my kingdom? Elevated to my palace  
By paying for it with my blood, he won renown through a high crime!  
Happy and unchecked is he, lounging at rest and swollen with pride,  
Welcoming the rumor that you have already perished! And is it not a rumor?  
Must I seek vengeance from the Athenians while my son is safe  
And his Pylades unharmed? Not so did Patroclus love Achilles!  
Not so did Pirithous love Theseus when he was put to the test!  
Take up your swords. Arms yourselves for the war within my household,  
And cut away a kinsman’s sin with a kinsman's blade.  
It will be no crime to punish your guilty mother.  
Justly shall you slay the wicked woman for her husband's death.  
A son, inflamed with dutiful love because of his father's murder,  
Who avenges his father by striking down his mother,  
Will expurgate the crime once committed by that mother,  
For he proves that man to be his father who was avenged by his arms,  
For whom adultery was rightly avenged by his heir, the dutiful champion.  
The Greeks love me, while they fiercely hate that pair.  
Go, united in spirit. The crowd of household servants will side  
With you, angry only that you were too slow to come.  
The servants shall mutter aloud, longing to tear with their teeth  
The living body of Aegisthus, and to hand over to the flames  
The cruel limbs of my unspeakable wife to be burned."  
He spoke these words, and groans broke the quiet of their sleep.  
Soon Agamemnon's son awakened his companion,  
And, while about to tell his dream, learned his was the same,  
And stood thunderstruck at how great was his father's power  
To warn both their minds in the same starlit hour.

Section 14.

"Tell me, brother," he said, "tell me right now what must be done.  
My breast, heart, senses, spirit, midriff, and mind  
Are thrown into turmoil by duty, grief, anguish, sorrow, ancestry,  
Affection, nature, shame, reverence, and reputation.  
Shall I drive a deadly blade into my mother's womb?  
She who, I weigh this deeply, for ten months endured  
Carrying me in the womb while pain goaded her?  
The seeds of nature, the beginnings of sweet life,  
Were in her womb, which became my pathway to light and my gateway to the wide world.  
And when fate saw fit for me to be born, and I came forth,  
She, with a stream from her breast, gave her milky teats to my lips,  
Pouring forth sweet honey with the taste of nectar.  
She who was mother became nurse to me. The queen became a servant,  
Showing familial care, and turning a stranger to sleep.  
She was both mother and father to me while my father fought at war,  
Until age carried me past my eleventh year.  
Shall I prove unmindful of such great kindnesses from my mother?  
Or shall my murdered father remain unavenged by me?  
Let the shepherd alone pay for his deeds and fall while my mother  
Survives, and I will sate my father's ghost with his blood.  
Let this be my mother's punishment, that she live while Aegisthus is struck down,  
Falling before her eyes. Let the wretched woman look  
Upon her lover, she who chattered while watching the son of Atreus die."  
Thus he spoke, wavering. But his friend, no hesitator,  
Gnashed his teeth and drew long sighs from the depths  
Of his fierce breast. Then, approaching Orestes, he thus  
Rebuked him: "Does dull feeling so cool your heart  
That you think things ought to be done that should not even have been spoken?  
To say it is a great sin, to hear it is an unspeakable crime!  
Are we going to Argive Mycenae to grant a pardon?  
Who is more deserving of punishment for a mother's crimes than she?  
Ah, shameful wickedness! She must be struck down because the guilt is theirs both.  
I beg you, young man. Beware the decrees of the grieving Greeks,  
Lest, by deciding that your father's murderess should live,  
Men stop believing you are his son. By a parent's right, will not  
The shade that visited you while you slept in Athens  
Haunt you on a thousand roads? At the threshold of the gate  
He will besiege you, and, assaulting the stars with his trembling voice,  
He will rail at you: 'Is this how you, my son, intend to defend your father?  
Is this a fitting way to mourn your slain parent's fate?  
Is this the funeral rite which you, as my avenger, will offer for my soul?  
Your stepfather will gladly perish by such a death!  
For then she, remaining alive, can send down to the cruel shades  
The soul of merciful Orestes as an offering for the corpse of the criminal.  
Unless your mother is made a victim, you shall be made a victim.'

Section 15.

What would you do if your own father spoke such sorrowful words to you?  
With the wicked at ease, will you do your duty or cast duty aside?  
Scatter, then, the limbs of the guilty on the tomb of your father!  
Rise, O noble hope of our youth, by the nobility of your royal blood!  
Let courage stir your spirit, let glory rouse your mind,  
Let your hands be armed with filial duty, let a merciless sword  
Sever the savage necks of both with a final groan.  
I myself will gladly go as your comrade through weapons and through fire,  
Nor will I fear anyone, for your father’s loyal men are there.  
Yet I will offer a clever, safe, and wholesome plan:  
Let me go ahead as a scout. In a quiet voice I will warn them  
To expect your arrival. As long as that crowd of servants  
Remains loyal, they will quickly favor us in their hearts  
And snatch up swords on your behalf. This is the safer way to go."  
Inflamed by these words and the bitterness of grief,  
Orestes arose, raging like a youth, threatening death,  
Grinding his teeth, and biting back his muttered curses.  
Then, as if he caught the shepherd Aegisthus in the very act,  
Tying his shameful limbs in his mother’s embrace,  
He struck at his absent foes, though neither of the criminals were there to die.  
Just as Pyrrhus raged after dreaming of Achilles  
When visions warned his mind, and just as, on that sleepy night  
When the shade of the fierce and heroic descendant of Aeacus spurred him on,  
Demanding the sacrifice of a maiden, and Pyrrhus raged against Troy,  
Even so, when the plan of Pylades found favor with Orestes,  
They steeled their minds, and, carrying only their swords,  
They trod hidden paths. Wherever the trail lead the two travelers,  
They went unseen, seeking to conceal their daring plot  
Til vengeance might reach those infamous throats.  
Just as Odysseus accompanied the steps of Diomedes  
When, traveling by night, he went to the Trojan camp,  
And they trod an anxious path, and no betraying horn  
Or war trumpet shook the air with martial blasts,  
And, without a breath from his lips, tiptoeing and silent,  
Bold Diomedes went forth, advancing from the Greek ships,  
With the clever hero of Laertes at his side,  
Even so the Athenian youths made for Mycenae.

Section 16.

When the journey they had begun was in progress, the old tutor of Orestes,  
Dorylas, suddenly appeared before them. As they trembled,  
He spoke: "Where do you come from, young men? And who are you, friends,  
And where are you heading?" When they made no answer  
But only tried to pass by, Dorylas recognized Orestes,  
And rushed to embrace his dear neck,  
Crying out, "Are you alive, my foster child? If you live then we all live!  
We have been yielding to treachery while the adulterer has been enjoying luxury.  
Having won your defiled mother, he revels in every pleasure,  
Prostituting the temples of triumph, the royal halls of the mighty,  
The shrines of commanders, and the sacred couches of kings.  
Rejoicing in the midst of his own crimes, he has turned the palace into a brothel.  
Yet we, going to the tomb of your great father,  
Lament endlessly. We have held fast to his promises,  
Though Cassandra’s prophecies came even earlier,  
That swift punishment awaits the guilty. Hurry, comrades!  
The gods will favor you for your Athenian valor.  
The royal household prays for the punishment of this crime.  
Do not hesitate, men, to kindle your spirits.  
Arm your hands with sword hilts and draw the steel!  
Let love of filial duty spur you, and let the bonds of friendship spur you.  
Let grief rise up on one side, and a noble desire for fame on the other.  
The shameful woman whom you must strike down will not be any trouble.  
Besides, what could hinder you? The limbs to be shattered belong to a rustic."  
So spoke the old man. This hope fired up the friends  
To quicken their pace. Each of the youths grieved  
That Mycenae was not yet close at hand, but Orestes said, "Let’s press on."  
Pylades added, "Let’s quicken our pace.  
Go ahead first, old man. We must hurry.  
Secretly whisper in the ears of the loyal servants  
To expect our arrival at the moment of the fourth dawn."  
Thus he spoke, and the old man departed, proceeding ahead to the city.  
Joy granted him the swift steps which old age denies.  
Soon the journey was finished. Exhausted, he reached the citadel,  
And secretly told a chosen few that Orestes was alive  
And would come at the next dawn as the avenger  
Of his father's blood. He urgently warned them all  
To keep silent and pass the word stealthily to every servant.  
All were permitted to know except only those to whom the knowledge was denied, namely,  
The accomplices of the criminals, lest they slip away  
Or take up weapons to defend their lives in battle.

Section 17.

Upon their master's arrival, one group of domestic servants held the gates,  
And another group occupied the palace, while a third manned the walls.  
Meanwhile, a crimson dawn rushed back in after the starry night,  
And nature restored the light it had enclosed in the hissing waves.  
The sun shone brightly and made better amends for the wrongs of Thyestes.  
The descendant of Atreus saw the walls he had left as a child,  
And, remembering them, he stretched out his right hand and spoke:  
"Hail, ancient home! Hail Mycenae, my fatherland!  
Though accursed at first, your honor shall soon be sustained.  
Once I sate my father’s ashes with my mother’s blood,  
His mighty shade will feel itself avenged by his avenging son,  
Just as when Eriphyle was wounded. Struck down by a blow  
Was that wife. He shall see it, as did Amphiaraus the seer."  
Thus he spoke, and glanced around at the walls like an anxious stranger.  
Once he remembered the way, he took his path, accompanied by his friend.  
Then he stepped inside, and the crowd of servants recognized  
The son of Agamemnon, his walk, his eyes, and his hands.  
Then they murmured, joyfully beating their breasts, but letting no sound pass their lips.  
"Now have the gates closed," said Pylades to Orestes.  
Bolts barred the entrances, and the trumpets of death rang out.  
Caught off guard, the criminals froze. Aegisthus was terrified:  
"Did I not promise the Greeks peace for generations to come?" he asked,  
"No one is left to stir up war against the Argives  
Now that Hector is destroyed and Troy has perished in battle."  
Then the enraged queen spoke angrily to the servants:  
"I will avenge this, as surely as I live and Aegisthus grows old with me!"  
While still speaking these words and making fierce threats of vengeance,  
One of the maidservants ran up, badly shaken and panting.  
"Orestes has come," she said, yet they didn't immediately believe her.  
While they hesitated, think she imagined it and that she spoke empty words,  
Pylades appeared in the hall, fierce and relentless.  
Just as Ajax, savage and implacable, faced his enemy  
When he attacked Hector, and, covered by his sevenfold shield,  
He roared with his mouth and threatened with his brow, even so Pylades flourished his sword,  
And thundered at the committers of the crimes: "By your cruel audacity  
You have reigned savagely until now! Did you hope to escape  
The hands of justice? Now you shall pay the brutal  
Penalty for your crimes, destroyed and destroyed again!"  
Thus he spoke, and turned toward the household servants with words of vengeance:  
"Strike down this miscreant! Hurl him off the prince’s throne,  
And let the savage victim be dragged by the foot to the royal descendant of Atreus.  
Let the axes of the executioners shatter his hardened limbs,  
And let the shepherd die by the very death with which he slew the king!  
But first let me drench my right hand in his blood."

Section 18.

Thus he spoke, then plunged his drawn sword into the man’s ribs.  
Servants dragged chains to his wounded limbs, binding his feet,  
And, behind the doors of the gate where the son of Atreus was laid low,  
They hacked him to pieces. They shattered his bones with a thousand axes,  
And yet his limbs twitched, though cruelly severed by every slice.  
Meanwhile, the mother, spared for the moment, thought her son would show her mercy.  
Thus, believing herself safe, began to mourn the shepherd,  
When suddenly Orestes appeared, savage and more ruthless than an enemy.  
Bent on his mother's destruction and supported by a terrifying band  
Of servants, he seized her by the hair and dragged her along,  
Reproaching her thusly: "Did you hope that after my father's death your wicked crime  
Would go unpunished? And did you wish to join the fates  
Of your two children to their parent's tomb?  
O wicked mother, behold, I am alive! And you shall die a bloody death,  
Slaughtered by my own hands over the bones of your husband."  
Terrified, the mother bared her naked breasts  
And begged her son: "By these breasts, spare me, my child!  
By the gods above and by your father! By the dear heart  
Of your sister, who snatched you away from my fury back then!  
And, since it means even more, I beg you by Pylades, your friend.  
Grant pardon, have pity, I pray! Have pity on your mother,  
If I am worthy to be called a mother." Then her son said: "Vainly  
You shed these tears. Your father awaits you among the shades.  
Trojan Cassandra wept over the fall of her master,  
And Andromache, enslaved after Hector’s death, did not strike Pyrrhus  
When she, the queen of the Phrygians, became a captive of the Greeks.  
But you preferred a shepherd to your leader, and bloodied his neck.  
My friend Pylades bids me do this, and my sister demands it,  
That you, savage woman, should fall by my blade!"  
But when the woman could win no mercy from her offspring,  
Quickly the prophecies of the Phrygian seeress returned to her mind.  
She said: "If it pleases you to avenge your father on both of us,  
Let the right hand of Pylades slay me, and let the same sword kill me.  
Then, as I fall back, let me collapse on the bones of Aegisthus while he yet breathes,  
And, as the accomplice of my crime and the author of my sin,  
Let our blood mingle together, as a witness to all the shades below,  
That a common fate awaits wicked partners in crime."

Section 19.

"As for what you ask, to lie dying on your adulterer's bones,"  
Said her son, "it's better if you fall on your husband's bones.  
Your blood shall not be mingled with his by my threatening sword.  
Nor shall your shared misdeeds be rewarded by dying embraced,  
Nor shall your wicked works receive a prize through a shared fate.  
You want a common punishment to unite you in sin? Then you shall lie apart."  
Thus he spoke, and, approaching his father's tomb, he said these words:  
"Sacred father, since you exist as an insensible shade and spirit,  
Receive the funeral offerings I bring you. The victim is just.  
I sacrifice Clytemnestra, the consort of King Aegisthus,  
And formerly yours, though I wish she were not. Take comfort in this,  
That he lies in the very spot where you were struck down and died.  
The delay of my youth was a just reason why this is happening so late.  
The will was there long ago." The Greek mother added:  
"O Sun, O righteousness, O elements of the heavens, sea, rivers, and earth,  
And you, O Nature, parent of all things, and you, dark abyss,  
Behold the recompense this son repays to his mother  
For his birth! Witness the reward for my milk!"  
Thus spoke the mother, yet she spoke to deaf ears.  
Doing all that filial duty could, Orestes turned away his face.  
Like before, the nurturing Sun denied his horses to Mycenae. Once more,  
The sky became veiled beneath a shadowy gloom.  
Soon Greece quaked beneath an unnatural night sky.  
Even the elements feared the darkness, and nature herself dreaded the chaos.  
Enyo gloried in the crime, and a fury guided the sword hilt.  
With clenched teeth the woman bit her purple cloak,  
Collapsed, and with her hands she stretched her tunic all the way down  
To the soles of her feet, fearing lest she lie naked in death.  
Sorrowful, she lowered her eyes with a modest gaze,  
Which she had never done before. For the unhappy woman, scarcely modest even now,  
At the very point of death, feared for her reputation as she died.  
Her fair limbs were reddened with crimson blood.  
Her twitching body shook the trampled sand,  
And, at last, beckoned toward death, she poured out her life along with her blood.  
Then, with grim faces, all returned together to the royal threshold.  
Just as two terrifying lions with tawny manes,  
Sated with the slaughter of heifers, seek their dens once more,  
Even so the royal palace received the blood stained comrades,  
And the Greeks gathered to greet Orestes with the honors due a king.  
Some grieved for the descendant of Atreus, but others grieved for his mother.  
Still, no one denounced the deed by open criticism,  
Nor did anyone fail to condemn Aegisthus.

Section 20.

The deadly day passed, and the sun plunged its wheels  
Into the hissing ocean. As his sister returned,  
A sheet of dew beckoned Sleep, the bringer of slumber, once more,  
And the stars kept turning through the middle of the night.  
A messenger arrived to tell of the maiden Hermione, seized  
By the plundering theft of Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles.  
Immediately, the descendant of Atreus raged, speaking thus to his friend:  
"Behold, another task calls us, and passion flames forth anew.  
What shall I do? It is a crime for my grown bride,  
Promised to my marriage bed, to be carried off at will. You keep guard of the kingdom.  
As for me, I will go through swords, flames, and a thousand cohorts.  
It is fitting that an avenger of his father should earn his own woman.  
I will go rescue her while yet she cries out the name of Orestes."  
Thus he spoke, and, girded with his sword hilt, he marched out against the enemy.  
He found the descendant of Aeacus [Pyrrhus] entering the temples of the gods.  
Then, attacking the young man, he struck him down unawares at the altar,  
And returned to the Greeks, elated by a second slaughter.  
While he reveled on his ancestral throne, handsome under his crown,  
His mother stood before his eyes, not unarmed,  
But armed with torches from the pyre and girt with glowing vipers.  
She thrust them at the young man's face and threatened deadly harm.  
With the flames brought close and the snakes uncoiled from their rings,  
The man fled in terror of these horrors, scouring the halls.  
But his dread parent followed him. Orestes sought out hidden corners  
In the secret chambers of the house, and there found his foe all the more.  
With the bolts drawn behind him, every door was soon shut fast.  
He found his mother inside, in the innermost chambers of the palace.  
She roared as she tried to speak: "Cruelest of men!" she said,  
"Impious wretch! Was your bitter injury to filial duty not enough,  
That your wicked hand must also stain the shrines of the gods?  
Close your gates of adamant on hinges of iron,  
Bar them with steel, hide behind thousands of panes,  
Yet I myself, fierce and ghastly, will haunt you in every place,  
Across seas, across plains, through forests, rivers, and mountains."  
She spoke, and threw his heart into turmoil with a hissing snake from the pyre.  
But fate would not let him outrun his swift mother,  
His hand went to his hilt, yet, despite repeated blows, he only sliced  
The air, and drew back an empty right hand without inflicting a wound.  
Then in his misery cried out: "Mine is the blood stained blade.  
You, harlot, who lie struck down by it, shall be struck a second time."

Section 21.

Thus he spoke, and the oppressive shade, recognizing the sword, was put to flight  
Through the yielding air. But, alas, the madness of Orestes remained,  
And the Argive avenger, the heir of Agamemnon raged violently.  
Just as that barely sober Lycurgus went away raging from Bacchus,  
Just as Hercules raged when fierce Megaera terrified him,  
Just as Ajax, the bravest of Greeks, once raged,  
Even so he roared uncontrollably and rushed throughout the palace.  
He thought the servants were his mother, he thought his friends were his mother,  
Whoever was there was his mother, and everywhere nearby  
Were the viper and the fire. He fled from these, while attacking those,  
And only the face of his comrade Pylades did not terrify him.  
Amidst delicacies fit for royal banquets  
He went hungry, for the candles on the tables dissuaded him from approaching.  
Even so men go starving in hell, tormented by the phantom of emptiness,  
Gazing at feasts crowded with the rich dishes of the mighty.  
Tantalus groans, yet there is no food, only the image of food,  
Which starving men are forbidden even to touch, as a fury holds them back.  
What are they to do? Which gods, which divine powers, begging as like suppliants,  
Can they invoke? Or should they weary the third kingdom of the dead?  
While all wept and the whole palace mourned,  
Another groan arrived, bringing with it fell grief and great fright.  
Molossus had come, the offspring of Andromache and Pyrrhus,  
To play the avenger of his father. Pylades took Orestes,  
Spirited him away, and sent him off to a foreign shore.  
He had reached the coast where the temple of Diana stood.  
There, a savage custom prevailed. The wretched stranger was led as a victim  
For the blade, and soon stood upright at the altar, bound with silk fasteners.  
As soon as he saw that the priestess wielded the customary iron blade,  
His madness was driven out by terror. Imminent death restored  
His mind, and adversity granted him a share of health.  
For the poor wretch, repeating the name of Agamemnon, cried out again and again.  
Clever Iphigenia heard the name of the son of Atreus.  
"Victim," she said, "tell me who you are, and why you cry out to Agamemnon?"  
Wounded in spirit, he answered: "I am pitiable Orestes,  
Born of my mother Clytemnestra and our leader. The son of Atreus  
Was my father." Not letting him speak any further,  
The priestess cast aside the knife and struck her forehead:  
"Executioners, untie the trembling limbs of this bound man!  
His heart is drained of its blood, and this is not a pleasing sacrifice."

Section 22.

Thus she spoke, and, tearing off the fillets, she removed him from the altar.  
With everyone else shut out, she did not deny that she was his sister.  
Yet she saw that at times, raging with a corrupted mind,  
He could speak nothing sensible. That night, she profited him  
With prayer, and, taking Diana's statue, she departed with her brother.  
When they returned to their homeland, Rumor soon informed  
Molossus. He accused Orestes of a double crime  
And brought him forward to stand trial in the heart of Athens.  
The elders ascended the temple with Minerva presiding.  
The descendant of Achilles rose, spurred by love for his father,  
And began his speech like this: "O noblemen, O font of law,  
I accuse the deadly Orestes of a criminal mind!  
Forgetful of the ties of blood, disdainful of the rights of men,  
Sacrilegious to the gods above, drenched in the blood of his mother,  
Shed by his own right hand. Perhaps it is true that his mother  
Was an adulteress. But why make of Pyrrhus a second Aegisthus?  
He slew him in the temple while he, the wrecker of Asia,  
The son of strongly armed Achilles, offered sacrifices to the heavens.  
Perhaps he argues that his mother was wicked and profane,  
She who added the destruction of her husband to the crime of adultery.  
She, the greatest of criminals, deserved to be struck down, but by a just judge,  
Not by his own sword. What sin will ever shame him now,  
Whose first crime was against his mother? Decree harsh penalties,  
A vengeance befitting Athens, you nobles of the line of Cecrops.  
More than a mere executioner’s sack is needed for his destruction.  
His deeds require this, to have as many wounds as he has limbs!  
Severed from his members, let him outlive his own death,  
A living corpse for a brief time, meant to be torn apart."  
Thus he spoke, and fell silent. Orestes gave answer to him thus:  
"Chiefs of the Greeks, men of wisdom, lights of the mind,  
Pinnacles of Athens, judges of honorable law,  
I am glad, not afraid, that my case is pleaded before you.  
For you have wives, and you possess cherished affection,  
And I trust you remember, from the years of your early youth,  
What the love of a bride is, and what the vows of a future marriage bed are.  
Thanks be to the gods above, that after so much wreckage of mind,  
I stand accused among benches like these, where I shall be safe and sound.  
Unharmed, I revere this court, made sacred by our laws,  
And I distinguish two things: an unspeakable crime, and a permissible action. Deliberate, revered ones.

Section 23.

Pronounce not your judgment on the strength of my arguments,  
But on the justice of the gods, who are proven to have cleansed me!  
For they granted healing sanity to my mind.  
No god of heaven, I think, would wish to save an unjust man.  
I am found all the more righteous than this champion, this avenger of a mother.  
For my guilty mother must be avenged, what then of my innocent father?  
Who, I ask, is so sacrilegious, who so mad, as to dare to accuse  
The charitable gods, to whom supreme power belongs?  
Let him accuse them, indeed! Let him summon the deities to battle,  
Let him charge them with a crime, and take up once more the war of the Giants.  
Perhaps he will object: ‘Why did Orestes have fits of madness then?’  
It was the distress of grief, O nobles, and not the punishment of guilt.  
Weariness troubles the spirit and exhausts the mind.  
Pyrrhus was an abductor, and I was the avenger of that abduction after the war.  
A single weak man accuses one whom the whole assembly of the gods approves.  
I beg you, leaders of the laws, let your verdict acquit  
One cleansed by divine right, by a kindly fate."  
Thus he spoke, and imposed a measured silence on his tongue.  
Counsel was sought, and the deeds of Orestes were weighed.  
It was the custom of the court that contrasting pebbles be cast.  
White stood for life, but red pressed for death.  
The judgment turned out contested. The twofold vote  
Was divided, and the count was equal for each color.  
But Minerva’s right hand held a pebble which was white,  
Added one to the count for life. "Orestes must be acquitted!"  
Was declared, and the verdict of the elders was pronounced thusly:  
"If mortals were allowed to judge the decrees of the gods,  
Orestes's deed could be investigated by legal process.  
However, we are bound by the divine command to the mercy of heaven.  
Thus we are not permitted to hear this case, and the judgment of the law must rest.  
Who will be so bold a violator as to challenge the laws of heaven?  
Paris did not go unpunished when he judged the goddesses,  
Nor did Tiresias act with impunity when he judged the Thunderer.  
Pyrrhus the abductor died slain by a just blade.  
As for his falling within a temple, the gods had supreme power  
To punish it, if they wished. Let Orestes be free from care  
And return to his homeland with no guilt condemning him."

Section 24.

The matter was settled. The crowds welcomed Orestes, cheering in support.  
Joyful Pylades received his friend as he stepped out,  
And his sisters held him in an embrace on his left and on his right.  
Then all four returned home in joy, making their way to Mycenae.  
The royal palace was reckoned rich once more with its wealth restored.  
O gods, by the Thunderer's command, to you has easy dominion  
Been granted over the air, the sea, the earth, and the sky.  
Piety entreats you pitifully, honor entreats you gently.  
Good sincerity and ancestral affection pray to you.  
The human race, the sacred bonds of kinship,  
The lineages of families, and the unity of the ties of blood beseech you.  
The crimes of the Lemnian women were enough, as were the Danaid wedding feasts,  
Which turned marriage beds into funeral pyres. Likewise the deeds of Thyestes,  
And countless unspeakable acts which it would be shameful to tell.  
Behold, a threefold tragedy at Mycenae now defiles  
The renown of the Greeks. Spare your world at last,  
And ward off such criminal acts from the wretched Pelasgians.
