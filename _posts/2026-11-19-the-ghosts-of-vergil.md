## Introduction

From the 300s onward, the tradition of writing secular epic poetry was alive and well in Europe. Poems by Claudian (like The Abduction of Persephone, composed in the 300s – [Book 1](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Claudian/De_Raptu_Proserpinae/1*.html), [Book 2](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Claudian/De_Raptu_Proserpinae/2*.html), [Book 3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Claudian/De_Raptu_Proserpinae/3*.html)), Dracontius (like [The Tragedy of Orestes](https://venatoverba.xyz/2026/11/18/the-tragedy-of-orestes-prose-translation.html), composed in the 400s), and Corippus (like [The Johannis](https://venatoverba.xyz/2026/11/07/the-johannis.html), composed in the 500s) demonstrate that there was both demand for and supply of epic poems on secular themes.

In the 600s, things seem to change. There are four places in that century where you might expect a secular epic poem in Latin to be written: France, Italy, England, and Spain. But, apparently, no such poem survives. In this essay, I want to explore each country separately, because on a first perusal, it seems unlikely that any single one of these regions produced a secular epic poem in this century, but, if you take them together, I think it’s probable that at least one of them did.

Note that I define the term “epic poems” in this essay to include some of the longer epyllions or “mini epics.” Namely, epic secular poems are poems of 500 lines or more about war, folklore, or civic history, without being primarily intended as devotional texts or aids to prayer.

## France

Let's start with France. I know of two literary schools that flourished in France in the 600s: the Merovingian Palace School and the School of St. Desiderius of Vienne. The latter was known for being absolutely steeped in classical poetry. The school’s founder, Desiderius, who was a Catholic bishop and saint, even received a personal reprimand from the pope about it:

“what a grave and heinous offense it is for bishops to sing what is not becoming even for a religious layman. … [It] should be clear that you [must] not apply yourself to trifles and secular literature.” (Pope Gregory the Great’s [Register of Letters](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3602.htm) Book 11 Letter 54)

If the pope thought that even this French saint strayed too deeply into secular theatrics, that proves that classical secular poetry still held a powerful grip on French elites.

From the Palace School, a bunch of letters from a correspondence club have survived, consisting mostly of Desiderius of Cahors (not to be confused with the bishop of Vienne – this one was a royal treasurer), Audoin of Rouen (the chancellor), Eligius of Noyon (a royal advisor), and Paul of Verdun (a civic official). They all eventually became bishops and wrote their letters in that capacity.

Their correspondence is filled with classical Latin vocabulary, grammar, and allusions to Latin epic poems. Here is an example: "as I turn the eyes of my mind toward your kindnesses, not even if I shouted with an iron tongue, as a certain poet says, or if a hundred mouths resounded, could anyone in any way recount such great wonders of God, as much as His grace is poured into your innermost heart." (Epistles of Desiderius of Cahors, Book 2, Letter 12 - Paul of Verdun to Desiderius) This is a reference to The Aeneid Book 6: "Not even if I shouted with an iron tongue... or if a hundred mouths resounded."

In one letter, Desiderius says his love of literature started when he was educated at the palace school: “If opportunity smiled upon us, I would wish to take part in sacred conversation with you more frequently. For we used to cheer one another with fables while in secular dress in the fellowship of the most serene Prince Clothar, and in the same way, with that vanity now completely set aside, we ought to be permitted to ruminate together on the sweet precepts of Christ.” (Epistles of Desiderius of Cahors, Book 1, Letter 9 – Desiderius to Bishop Abbo of Metz)

Since the Merovingians had an elite literary culture in the 600s who loved the Latin classics, one has to wonder, did they compose any new ones? One poem that is worth mentioning in this period, and which is likely from France, is called [The Praise of the Virgin Mary](https://www.mqdq.it/testo/testo?codice=VEN_FORT%7Cspur%7C001). At about 400 lines, it's almost as long as some of the classic “mini epics” of Roman antiquity. It is attributed to an anonymous poet commonly called Pseudo Fortunatus, which means False Fortunatus, because scholars used to think the author was the *actual* Fortunatus, who was a poet active in France in the 500s. Some scholars who study this period *still* think he's the author, but the rest don't think the poet is known.

Sadly, that's about the closest it gets. In the 600s, I know of no other complete poem from France that gets close to epic length, and not only that, but I can't even name a Merovingian whose *collected* poetic works approach that length. The closest you get is St. Columbanus, who is more associated with Italy than France (though he did spend twenty years in France), and who will be discussed later.

So, despite there being clear readership in Merovingian France for a new epic, plus classically trained authors, why does it seem like no one imitated the ancient poets by writing “new” epic verses with a secular theme, and only one anonymous poet apparently wrote a *religious* poem of similar length?

One might say, “The answer is clear: half of the people you cited in this section said they personally objected to pagan poetry, and the other half probably stopped pining for it when that papal reprimand came down.” But that objection forgets something: secular poetry doesn't have to be pagan. The Johannis was a Christian secular poem from these people’s recent past. It was Christian in terms of its background and secular in terms of its theme. Future generations wrote plenty of epics about the battles of Christian kings and the founding myths of their cities, without turning them into religious poems about paganism *or* Christianity. If the Merovingians liked the old secular poems but felt the paganism in them was unsavory, they could have written their own poems on secular themes *without* paganism in them.

Perhaps those poems were written and just didn't survive. According to the scholar Hildegar of Meaux, who lived in the 800s, the Merovingians *did* produce a poem of the kind I'm seeking: The Farolied. Hildegar was the biographer of a Merovingian saint, and he said the Farolied was a song, composed in the 600s, about part of a war between King Clothar II and the Saxons. Hildegar preserved eight lines of the song in his [Latin Life of St. Faro](https://archive.org/details/RecueilDesHistoriensDesGaulesEtDe3/page/n608/mode/1up), and I've versified the surviving lines in English. They go like this:

This song is of Clothar, the King of the Franks,  
Who battled the fierce Saxon race.  
How grievously would it have gone for their envoys,  
If Faro had not shown them grace!  
For envoys of Saxony, passing through France,  
When Faro was noble of rank,  
By heaven's assistance departed the city,  
Unharmed by the king of the Franks.

That's the only fragment of the poem discovered by scholars up to the present time, and most researchers think it probably doesn't even count. For one thing, I doubt it was composed in the 600s. The surviving lines contain folkloric characteristics, like attributing a Saxon war to King Clothar II which scholars think really happened under the later King Dagobert I. That's the kind of mixup folklorists make when writing about events centuries after they happened, meaning the Farolied was probably composed long after the 600s, and Hildegar merely *thought* it was Merovingian. Still, I wish its full text was available to study and sing today.

Epics like the Farolied may well have been composed in the right time frame, but none has apparently survived from France in the 600s except possibly those eight lines. So, despite France clearly having authors capable of writing epic Latin poems, and an interest in the ones from the past, it is sad if, truly, nothing more from them in this genre has survived.

## Italy

Italy is, in some sense, the most obvious place to hope for an epic poem. Most of the *classic* Latin epics were written there, and, although the control of Italy was divided between the Byzantines and the Lombards in the 600s, it still had excellent literary schools that taught people to read and appreciate the classics. Scholars in Italy at this time included Hadrian the Grammarian, Felix the Grammarian, St. Theodore of Tarsus, Boniface Consiliarius, and Bobbio Scholiast. The latter is a conventional name that researchers give to an anonymous grammar teacher who worked at Bobbio Abbey.

St. Columbanus was probably the most prolific poet in Italy in this period. He was an Irish monk who first moved to France and then to Italy, where he founded Bobbio Abbey. He died there after spending much of his final days writing letters and poems to his friends in Italy and France. The longest surviving poem by St. Columbanus is a collection of maxims about 200 lines long, and his [Letter to Fidolius](https://www.catholica.com/columbanus/poem-to-fidolius/) contains a poem of 165 lines, using examples from classical mythology to warn his readers about the dangers of greed. None of his poems are long enough to meet my criteria, and most of them are on religious themes, but he is proof that Italy had at least one known, named poet capable of writing lengthy poems.

Italy’s Lombard and Byzantine leaders also commissioned some poetry. One example is the Song of the Synod of Pavia by Stephanus Magister ([here](https://fontesistrie.eu/698_SP) is its Latin text). In 95 lines, this poem, commissioned by a Lombard king, celebrates the settlement of a theological controversy of the time. Another example is a poem written for display on the sepulcher of a Byzantine general named Drocton. That poem will be discussed shortly and given in full.

So if Italy’s schools fostered demand for epics, and their poets demonstrated the requisite skillset, and their culture had people wealthy enough to finance the creation of epics, where are their epics? Like in France, a possible answer is, they existed but are lost. In the 700s, a man named Paul the Deacon wrote a book called [History of the Lombards](https://elfinspell.com/MedievalMatter/PaultheDeacon-PaulusDiaconus/HistoryOfTheLangobards/PaulTheDeacon-TableOfContents.html) that includes dramatic, embellished myths about the early Lombard kings. The author sometimes indicates that he is drawing from Germanic songs. Here is an example:

“the name of Alboin was spread abroad far and wide… the good fortunes of his wars and his courage are celebrated, not only among the Bavarians and the Saxons, but also among other men of the same tongue in their songs.” (History of the Lombards Book 1 Chapter 27)

He goes on to relate dozens of those stories, sometimes pausing his narrative to discuss particular German words that he found difficult to render in Latin (e.g. the Lombards had a word that meant “a goblet made from a human skull”). This suggests Paul was summarizing the orally transmitted songs that he just mentioned.

However, I'd like to propose that maybe Paul pulled some of these myths from lost Latin court poetry or verse mini epics written in the style of Claudian or Corippus. The political figures of the time sometimes commissioned Latin poetry, as discussed previously. Maybe they commissioned one or more poets to make Latin poems about some of their heroes, and Paul drew on those sources. Byzantine poems in this style might well have been brought to Byzantium to be performed there, only for the manuscripts to languish, get erased by copyists who wanted the vellum for Greek texts, and then perish through warfare and fires. Lombard poems in this style might well be lost because the people who copied these things (mostly monks) preferred Paul’s lucid Christian prose over unannotated myths about pagan heroes and their deities.

I think this theory is possible, but I also think it is unlikely, for the following reason: Paul the Deacon is pretty good at citing his sources, especially poets. Historians have listed the sources he explicitly cites, and they include: the Germanic songs mentioned previously, histories by Secundus of Non and St. Gregory of Tours (Book 3 Chapter 29), the homilies and letters of Pope St. Gregory the Great (Book 4 Chapter 8), a short religious poem by Marcus of Monte Cassino (Book 1 Chapter 26), a long religious poem by Fortunatus (Book 2 Chapter 13), and a short secular poem written on the sepulcher of a general (Book 3 Chapter 19). The latter one is particularly noteworthy as a near example of the exact thing I am looking for, except far too short, so I'll copy it here:

Drocton lies buried within this tomb, but only in body,  
For in his merits he lives, over the orb of the world.  
First with the Langobards he dwelt, for by race and by nature  
Sprung from Suavian stock, suave to all people was he.  
Terrible to be seen was his face, though in heart he was kindly,  
Long was the beard that grew down on his vigorous breast.  
Loving the standards of Rome and the emblems of the republic,  
Aid unto them he brought, crushing the power of his race.  
Love unto us he bore, despising the claims of his kindred,  
Deeming Ravenna his own fatherland, dear to his heart.  
First of his valiant deeds was the glory of captured Brexillus.  
There for a time he remained, dreadful to all his foes.  
Later when here his power brought aid to the Roman standards  
First within his hands rested the banner of Christ.  
Afterwards when Faroald withheld by treachery Classis,  
“Fleet-town” in hope to avenge, arms for the fleet he prepares,  
Struggles in tiny ships on the flowing stream of Badrinus.  
Conquers and overcomes numberless Langobard bands,  
Vanquishes also in lands of the East the impetuous Avar,  
Seeking to win for his lords victory’s sovereign palm.  
Often to them as a conq’ror, sustained by the aid of Vitalis,  
Martyr and holy saint, honored with triumphs he came.  
And in the fane of Vitalis he sought the repose of his body,  
Pleased that this place should hold, after his death, his remains.  
When he died, he implored these things of the priest Joannes,  
By whose pious love he had returned to these lands.

That poem was presumably commissioned by a Byzantine official, probably near the end of the 500s or early 600s when scholars think Drocton died. It proves that the Byzantine part of Italy also had poets with the skills required to compose an epic poem, and one wonders if whoever wrote this poem considered writing a longer one about this general’s apparently magnificent campaigns. Perhaps some of the lines of this poem were, in fact, extracted from a longer poem about that man.

On the one hand, it seems likely that Paul the Deacon would have quoted such a poem if it existed. He apparently really liked poetry, given all of the poets he cites, and he even wrote two poems of his own for Book 1 Chapter 26. But on the other hand, Columbanus was a major poet in the right time and place, and Paul never talks about that. He only briefly mentions Columbanus as the founder of Bobbio Abbey (Book 4 Chapter 41).

Paul also has many “citations” that are incomplete: “It is also related by many up to the present time [by whom?] that a special kind of arms was made under him.” (Book 1 Chapter 27) “[King Alboin] determined, as is related [by whom?], to put over the city of Forum Julii…his nephew Gisulf.” (Book 2 Chapter 9) “It is believed that what is related [by whom?] of king Authari occurred about this time. For the report is that that king then came through Spoletium…” (Book 3 Chapter 32) “king Authari…died…from poison he had taken, as they relate [as who relates?].” (Book 3 Chapter 35) Since he has unnamed sources, who knows if a Latin epic, now lost, was among them?

If we allow for “prose epics,” there nearly *was* one among them. Much of Paul’s first book is based on a Latin treatise from the 600s called [The Origin of the Lombard Nation](https://blackewood.blogspot.com/2012/07/origo-gentis-langobardorum.html). Paul explicitly cites it in Book 1 Chapter 21 of his history. The opening of this “older” text appears to essentially be a prose translation of an old heroic song from the Lombard homeland. The Lombard kings were very proud of this Latin “origin story” and prefixed it to the Lombard legal code, which proves they had an interest in “Latinized” Lombard mythology.

Sadly, even if this text was translated in verse (which it wasn't), it wouldn't count as an epic, at least by the definition I'm using, unless it was greatly expanded, perhaps by stitching together several stories from other songs. The origin story is just too short on its own. But it’s possible that other, longer texts were done in poetry, though I doubt it because, despite my misgivings, I still think Paul the Deacon would have explicitly talked about such poems if they existed. And even if such poems did exist, none have apparently survived, leaving Italy seemingly devoid of any secular Latin epic poems that made their way from the 600s to modern times.

## England

England seems to be another near miss in the 600s. Two scholars from Italy (Hadrian the Grammarian and St. Theodore of Tarsus) moved to England in this century and started a literary school there known to history as the School of Canterbury. In the 700s, St. Bede the Venerable said, “there are still living at this day some of their scholars, who are as well versed in the Greek and Latin tongues as in their own.” ([Ecclesiastical History](https://ccel.org/ccel/bede/history/history.i.html) Book 4 Chapter 2)

We also know the Anglo Saxon culture was interested in epic poetry: a native poet wrote [Beowulf](https://books.google.com.pr/books?id=Rndhg5W4RcwC&redir_esc=y) at about this time, probably between 675 and 750 ad. Some scholars prefer later dates, but that debate is not very relevant for this essay, because Beowulf wasn't written in Latin anyway. Still, the poem is every bit as gripping an adventure story as anything ever sung about Odysseus or Orestes, and, assuming the consensus dating is correct, it proves the tradition of secular epic poetry still lived in Europe at this time, just not in Latin.

One of the brightest minds whom the School of Canterbury produced was St. Aldhelm of Malmesbury. Aldhelm wrote a religious epic called [In Praise of the Virgins](https://clasp.ell.ox.ac.uk/db-latest/poem/ALDHELM.CarmVirg) (note that, to read the English translation on the linked page, there is a button to toggle it on, otherwise it just shows the Latin text of the poem). It narrates the lives and deaths of various male and female Christian saints, and it's the only epic Latin poem I know of from England in the 600s.

Aldhelm also got remarkably close in another poem: his Carmen Rhythmicum, or [Rhythmic Song](https://clasp.ell.ox.ac.uk/db-latest/poem/ALDHELM.CarmRhyth.Octo) (note that, again, you have to toggle the English translation on the linked page). This partially narrative poem describes a storm that battered a monastery Aldhelm stayed at one night, tearing its roof off. Aldhelm wrote a 200 line poem about the experience, vividly describing the furor of the storm with Vergilian imagery. It's too short to count as an epic, and it doesn't even really have a plot. It describes a storm, details a terrifying moment at night when some monks were praying and the roof came off, describes reviewing the damage after the storm, and concludes with a short prayer of thanks that no one was hurt. But of all the secular Latin poems I know from this century, this one is the longest, and that counts for something.

One might wonder if a suitable poem was written in England and was just lost, but sadly, unlike in France and Italy, we have a definitive reason to infer that, in England, there are no lost ones of the right kind. Here's why. In the late 700s, Blessed Alcuin of York wrote a poem called [The Founders of York](https://venatoverba.xyz/2026/10/12/the-founders-of-york-prose-translation.html), which contains a catalog celebrating the famous Latin authors whose books were on the shelves of the great School of York. (See section 4.14.) That school was one of the premier learning centers of Europe in Alcuin’s time. If any secular epic poem was written in England in the 600s, and was done in Latin, Alcuin’s library would have it, because the monastery system distributed new Latin books all over England – and, indeed, across Europe.

Alongside classical giants like Vergil and Lucan, Alcuin proudly listed two poets from England: Aldhelm and Bede, an Englishman from the 700s who wrote a poetic Saint’s Life in Latin (among many, many other works in prose). Alcuin included these authors effectively to boast about England’s Latin literary triumphs. He wanted to prove that England could go toe to toe with Italy in terms of intellectual mastery. As a result, if the School of York’s library contained any author who wrote a long secular epic in Latin, there's almost no chance that Alcuin would omit him from his list. So we're left with the conclusion “if such a poem existed, its author would be on that shelf, and the authors on that shelf didn't write one.”

It's also implausible to think “maybe one of them *did* write one and it was just lost later.” That hypothesis is basically impossible, because both of the authors from England on Alcuin’s list were quickly studied, imitated, and discussed across Europe. If either of them shared a long Latin epic with their peers, future scholars as well as their contemporaries would almost certainly have talked about it, and they didn't.

Still, the available evidence shows that England knew how to write epic poems at this time. Their poets even did so, and some of those epics have survived. It’s just implausible that they wrote any in this century that were both “secular” and “Latin.”

## Spain

Spain is another place where, in the 600s, you might expect to find a secular Latin poetic epic. Spain was under the control of the Visigothic Kingdom at this time, which was in the middle of a golden age of scholastic achievement. As a former province of the Roman empire, Spain not only still had some of the old imperial grammar schools, but its bishops started new schools as well, which thrived in an environment of relative peace and stability.

These schools absolutely fostered a love of the classics. Braulio of Zaragoza was a bishop who, while writing to another bishop, alludes to his friend’s familiarity with classic Latin authors, which they shared from boyhood: "Keep it firmly fixed in your mind that I could bite back if I wished, and sink my teeth in when provoked. For I too, as Flaccus [Horace] says, learned my alphabet, and often pulled my hand back from the schoolmaster's cane [Juvenal, Satires Book 1 Satire 1]." (Letter to the Priest Tajon – Epistola 11)

Count Bulgar of Septimania was a secular figure who once wrote to a bishop about a financial dispute and used a Vergilian simile to demand restitution: “Just as a debtor might cast aside all human shame, and, out of sheer impudence, attack his lender with arrogant objections, while the timid lender, who has truth on his side, overlooks what the false accuser ought to blush deeply for saying, even so there remains to…King Gundemar…no small claim, for you must repay a great sum of money.” (Letters to a Frankish Bishop – Epistola 3)

Valerius of Bierzo was a monk from the 600s who mentions his early devotion to secular studies before he decided to become a monk: “I, a most unworthy sinner and a native of the province of Asturias, was occupied during the days of my youth with worldly allurements. Greedily longing for earthly gains and intent upon vain studies, I gave free rein to these pursuits…when suddenly I was compelled by a desire for divine grace… [So] I set out…for the monastery of Complutum…trusting that through the path of confession I would at last reach the light of truth.” (Statement of My Complaint Chapter 1)

These examples show that Visigothic students absorbed the classics, sometimes developed an appreciation for poetry, and then later decided whether to pursue a secular or a religious career. A particularly notable example is King Sisebut, who ended up writing Latin poems himself. A 61 line poem by him about lunar eclipses has survived to this day. ([Here](https://www.mqdq.it/testo/testo/codice/SISEB%7Ccarm%7C001) is the Latin text.)

The greatest Visigothic poet was probably Eugenius II of Toledo. He was a bishop whose Latin was good enough that he not only wrote poems himself, but he took ancient epic length poems by Latin authors like Dracontius, revised them, and released his own versions which went on to become more popular than the originals. His sole surviving epic length poem was commissioned by a Visigothic king, and is a revision of Book One of Dracontius’s poem The Praise of God, with an original additional section. Eugenius’s version is called The Hexaemeron, and, in about 600 lines of poetry, it narrates the creation story from the bible. ([Here](https://www.mqdq.it/testo/testo/codice/EVG_TOLE%7chexa%7c001) is the Latin text.)

Another Visigothic poem worth mentioning in this context is the [Exhortation to Penance](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Exhortatio_poenitendi). In about 200 lines of poetry, it uses dramatic language to urge its readers to live morally, warning them about what awaits them in the afterlife if they don't. The work is neither an epic nor is it secular, but it shows the Visigoths had another poet besides Eugenius who could have done the task. The author is unknown, but scholars call him Pseudo Sisebert because the author was once thought to be Bishop Sisebert of Toledo, and modern scholars no longer think there are sufficient grounds for that attribution. 

Even though the Visigoths clearly studied Latin poetry, absorbed its conventions, and wrote their own religious poems of similar length, I am sad to say that, like in the other regions, no secular poetic epic apparently survives from Visigothic Spain. Perhaps one has been lost. St. Isidore of Seville, the greatest scholar produced by the Visigothic Kingdom, wrote a book in the middle of the 600s called The Lives of Illustrious Men (its Latin text is [here](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/De_viris_illustribus_(Isidorus_Hispalensis))), where, in chapter 46, he mentions the author Maximus of Zaragoza. According to Isidore, Maximus wrote poetry and prose, and his prose included a treatise on Visigothic history. All of Maximus’s writings are now lost, and Isidore indicates even he had not yet perused them. But if anyone wrote an epic about the Visigoths, Maximus might have done it among his poems, because he was clearly interested in their history.

Among surviving works, I do know of one secular Visigothic epic, a prose one. That is [The History of King Wamba](https://venatoverba.xyz/2026/10/13/history-of-king-wamba-prose-translation.html) by Julian of Toledo, which is an embellished story about an uprising that happened during the reign of King Wamba. It covers both the intrigue that led to it and the glorious battles by which the king put down the rebellion. The story features all that you would expect from an epic: dramatic speeches, celestial interventions, a cinematic climax in an old imperial arena, and a glorious parade when the king returns to his capital in triumph. All of it is told in an elegant Latin style that imitates the classical prose of Sallust, but this is a prose story, so it doesn't count as a “poetic” epic, even though the author clearly could have written it that way if he had only known how much I, fourteen hundred years later, wish he did. Julian of Toledo could have made the greatest Latin poetic epic of Visigothic Spain, and even though he apparently didn't, he at least made their greatest surviving prose epic.

## Conclusion

Looking across France, Italy, England, and Spain, one thing becomes clear. In the 600s, Europe had all the ingredients: classical schools, interest in Latin epics, poets who could write them, patrons who could fund them, and plenty of heroic stories to tell. While England’s thorough library records pretty strongly indicate that its poets, in this century, didn’t produce a secular epic poem in Latin, the evidence from France, Italy, and Spain points to escalating likelihood. Something like “15% chance, 30% chance, 40% chance,” respectively. None of them is probable *individually,* but even though all three coins are biased toward tails, I bet one of them landed on heads anyway. The seemingly missing epic poem of the 600s was probably written down in one of three places: a lost Merovingian lay, an Italian heroic poem ignored by monastic copyists, or a vanished volume of Visigothic verse.

There are two more things I want to mention. First, perhaps future research will uncover a Latin poetic epic from this time period that *did* survive but which just hasn't been discovered yet. Lots of Latin literature remains improperly categorized or awaits discovery on damaged manuscripts whose contents have yet to be deciphered. Second, even though there seems to be no surviving secular epic poem from the 600s in Latin, there is one in Greek. The Heraclian Trilogy by George of Pisidia is a series of three verse narratives ([Book 1](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?docId=Fathers-Synchronized-OR/Georges_Pisides__De_expeditione_Persica.gr.html;query=;brand=default&surl=aHR0cHMlM0ElMkYlMkZjYXRob2xpY2xpYnJhcnkub3JnJTJGbGlicmFyeSUyRmJyb3dzZSUyRmNlbnR1cnklMkY2MDA=), [Book 2](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?docId=Fathers-Synchronized-OR/Georges_Pisides__Bellum_Avaricum.gr.html;query=;brand=default&surl=aHR0cHMlM0ElMkYlMkZjYXRob2xpY2xpYnJhcnkub3JnJTJGbGlicmFyeSUyRmJyb3dzZSUyRmNlbnR1cnklMkY2MDA=), [Book 3](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?docId=Fathers-Synchronized-OR/Georges_Pisides__Heraclias.gr.html;query=;brand=default&surl=aHR0cHMlM0ElMkYlMkZjYXRob2xpY2xpYnJhcnkub3JnJTJGbGlicmFyeSUyRmJyb3dzZSUyRmNlbnR1cnklMkY2MDA=)) about Emperor Heraclius’s campaigns against Persia and the Avars. So Europe *did* produce a secular epic poem in the 600s in one of the Roman Empire’s two official languages. It’s just in the Greek tongue, not the Latin one. And that's exciting too, so I praise God for it.
